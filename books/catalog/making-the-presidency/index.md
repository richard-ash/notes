## Classification
A biography of John Adams for the period of the Adams administration. In particular, it looks at the transition from Washington to Adams and the norms and attitudes that Adams set and solidified as the first non-Washington president of the United States.

## The Book in a Sentence
A biography of the Adams presidency, and of how the first post-Washington president set the norms that made the office outlast its founder.

## Structure
Chronological. It opens with Washington's farewell and Adams assuming the presidency, and ends with Jefferson's victory in 1800 and the transfer of power in 1801. Along the way: the inherited cabinet and the fight over who actually runs the executive branch, the XYZ Affair and the undeclared war with France, the Alien and Sedition Acts, Hamilton's New Army and the inspector general fight, the Murray nomination and the second peace mission, Fries's Rebellion and the pardons, the firing of Pickering and McHenry, and the deadlocked election of 1800.

## The Problem
Chervinsky is trying to do three things.

1. **Teach us about the Adams presidency from a different light.** She makes a big point at the end that Adams and his presidency have been colored by Jefferson and that wing of the party's critiques of him over the centuries, and she doesn't think that is a fair or accurate representation. So she portrays the presidency from Adams's view, and Adams in his own light.
2. **Frame how important Adams's contributions were to the norms of peaceful transition of power.** Written in the wake of the January 6, 2021 riot over the transfer of power. Adams did it twice: when he took over from Washington, and when he lost to Jefferson. She wants to highlight that contribution to American governance.
3. **Tell the story.** She is a fabulous storyteller. The entire read is gripping and well-executed. Even if that is a secondary goal, actually describing what Adams did during his presidency might be a primary one too.

Did she succeed? She exceeded on all counts. It was enlightening about the president and painted him in a very positive light. To be fair, I haven't read much about Adams before, so that was probably part of it, and I haven't seen much of the Jeffersonian point of view. That is where my study goes next. I'll have to see the other side of the coin before I can really judge the argument. But it was a great introduction to Adams.

It also showcased how much intrigue and challenge dealing with politics entails. In a company, you can fire anyone you want. Adams didn't have that luxury. He had to deal with people in specific positions of power and either counteract them or compel them to his cause. That is intriguing and hard, and it gave me real respect for what politicians have to deal with.

## Key Terms
- **Arch Federalists.** Chervinsky's name for the Hamilton, Pickering, Wolcott, McHenry, Sedgwick wing of the party. They wanted war with France, a standing army, and a cabinet that managed the president.
- **Executive-by-committee vs. a president who controls the whole branch.** The central framework. Hamilton and Pickering envisioned a parliamentary-style cabinet in which the president shared power with the unelected secretaries. Adams insisted the Constitution put the power in the president alone. The Senate's quiet acceptance of Pickering's firing settled it.
- **Precedent vs. norm.** Washington's precedents were "little more than historic anomalies" until they were repeated. Adams's repetition is what turned them into norms.
- **Essex Junto.** The Massachusetts Federalist faction Adams blamed for the rot in his cabinet.
- **Quasi-War.** The undeclared naval war with France, 1798 to 1800. Chervinsky doesn't use the term; I had to look it up on Wikipedia to understand what was going on.
- **New Army and the inspector general fight.** Hamilton's bid to run foreign policy from inside the military, with Washington's unfortunate help.
- **Strict construction vs. the will of the people.** The Madison and Gallatin split over how to break the 1800 tie. Adams adhered to the text and trusted the process.

Events you need background on: the XYZ Affair, the Alien and Sedition Acts, Fries's Rebellion.

## Key Ideas
- **The peaceful transfer of power had to be invented.** The biggest thing in the book. There was no tradition of peaceful transition; it had to be crafted from scratch, and Adams was the first to set the standard and the custom for how to do it, on both ends of his presidency. Americans have taken this for granted for 200-plus years. It is very rare and also hard to maintain.
- **Adams made sure the president was the one in charge.** His cabinet secretaries conspired to weaken the presidency, shift authority to the departments, and undermine civilian control of the military. Adams reasserted control over the military, foreign policy, and personnel. This dovetails with the conversation we're having right now about how much power the president has. The Supreme Court is lining up on Adams's side: the unitary executive, the Constitution endows the executive with this power, and there are very few cases in which the president cannot act as they intend.
- **He chose peace with France at the cost of splitting his own party.** I admired this. He was in a tough situation, he knew diplomacy would cost him his popularity, his leadership of the Federalists, and probably reelection, and he stood by his values anyway. It again showed how well Chervinsky wrote the book and showcased what Adams was doing.
- **Adams was at his best when he wrote first and sent later.** He had a short fuse. He would write out the angry letter, walk the property, mark it "do not send," and then write the moderate version. When he gave himself time to process, he could be a master politician.

## Disagreements
The biggest gap isn't a weakness in her argument, it's a weakness in my own understanding. I haven't read much about Jefferson, so I'm getting one side.

She does call out Adams's failings. The Alien and Sedition Acts are part of it, though she makes clear he worked to slow and soften them and that his views updated over time. It read like a case where he chose party loyalty over what he thought was right. She also calls out that he could be selfish (the summer of 1799 at Quincy) and that he had outbursts. I'm sure the Jeffersonian side will disagree with the overall portrait, and we'll see.

## Takeaways
It made me better appreciate how complicated being a politician would be. It also made me think that if I ever want to do politics, I need more experience in the private sector first. We'll see.

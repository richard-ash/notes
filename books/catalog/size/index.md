## Classification
Smil explores the concept of size and what it means in the modern world: how we relate to different sizes, from the microscopic to the gigantic, and how size shapes the way we're currently living.

## The Book in a Sentence
An exploration of the concept of size.

## Structure
Eight chapters:

1. Size as the Measure of All Things
2. Perceptions, Illusions, Measurements
3. Proportions, Symmetry, and Asymmetry
4. Size Designs: The Good, the Bad, the Outrageous
5. Size and Scaling
6. Metabolic Scaling
7. Symmetries Around Means
8. When Asymmetries Rule

It moves from how we perceive size, through how things are designed at human scale, into the scaling laws of organisms and machines, and ends on statistical distributions. It reads more like a tour of related topics than a single argument building to a conclusion. Smil himself admits there's no condensed synthesis in the final chapter.

## The Problem
Maybe he's trying to address how the different sizes we see in life, from the microscopic to the gigantic, make humans feel. I don't know that I got that much from it. Some of the material didn't feel new, and it wasn't organized in a way where it stuck or made a lot of sense.

## Key Terms
- **Range of human vision** — going small, the naked eye cuts off fast: you can't discern anything narrower than about 1/250 the width of your pinky. Going large runs much further: a cumulonimbus cloud is 10 million times the size of a raindrop.
- **Canonical views** — most objects are preferably seen from 30–45 degrees above and rotated 30–45 degrees to one side. Four criteria explain why a view feels canonical: recognizability, familiarity, functionality, and aesthetic appeal.
- **Diminishing returns of scale** — oil tankers stopped at about half a million tons despite no technical obstacle. Doubling from 60,000 to 120,000 dwt cut building cost 30 percent; the next doubling saved only about 15 percent. Steadily increasing size is accompanied by an inexorably diminishing return on investment.
- **Limits of size and human scale** — the two challenges of modern design: pay more attention to human scale and ergonomics, and accept that civilization can't design its way out of its predicaments. Both point to rational adjustments and staying within sensible limits.

## Key Ideas
All four of the concepts above are good. Beyond them, the book isn't really making an argument. Smil is showing you different lenses on the world, a different way to look at size, and giving you facts about the world.

## Disagreements
Not applicable in the agree/disagree sense. Smil is describing, not arguing. The critique is one of presentation: some of the facts I already knew, and the rest were presented in a very academic way that wasn't necessarily super relevant to day-to-day life. That's probably why the book didn't hit for me as much.

## Takeaways
It gives you new perspectives. A little insight into how perceptions and illusions work: why your eye can see what it can see, why we're impressed by big buildings, that kind of thing.

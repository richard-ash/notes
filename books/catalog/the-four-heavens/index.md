## Classification
A new book from a prominent Maya archaeologist. Over the last twenty to forty years, archaeologists have done amazing work and actually decoded (translated) Maya hieroglyphics. That gives us a second source for what happened in the Maya world. Before, our only source was the Spanish and what they said about the Maya. Now we have at least what the Maya elites said about themselves.

He is upfront that this excludes the common people and their lived experience, but it's still a new perspective worth telling the story of. That's what this book is: the story of the Maya as we've learned it over the past twenty or thirty years, from their own voice.

## The Book in a Sentence
A Maya archaeologist retells the story of the Maya Classic period from new translations, in their own voice.

## Structure
Mostly chronological. He starts with an introduction and an overview of what we've learned, then walks through what the new translations tell us:

1. **Preclassic period** — the early monumental sites and the origins of the calendar.
2. **Classic period** — split regionally, with a chapter each on north, south, east, and west.
3. **The collapse** — abrupt, around 700–900 AD.
4. **Postclassic period**.

Throughout, he weaves in the theme of how Maya societies constantly changed, picked up, and moved from one location to another.

The three parts carry Maya names: Paskab ("The World Revealed"), Kabch'en ("The Earth and Caves"), and Chante'chan ("The Four Heavens").

## The Problem
Many problems. How did the Maya live? Why did they fight? What was life like? And how did Classic society collapse so abruptly between 700 and 900 AD? Can we figure that out by reading between the lines of the hieroglyphics they left behind?

There's also the source problem: we only have the elite perspective. How did regular people live versus the elites?

Having this new perspective brings up so many new questions we can ask that we had no thought or hope of answering before. In that sense it opens more questions than it closes.

On the collapse, he provides contextual evidence rather than a guarantee. It looks like there were periods of drought leading to famine and environmental stress. He also highlights that elite society grew substantially in numbers over those couple hundred years — almost an elite overproduction. What do a bunch of elites do when they all jockey for power? In the Maya case that led to a lot of warfare, which he thinks could have been a contributing cause.

## Key Terms
He does a lot to explain the language and how it would sound in English, which is kind of crazy if you think about it.

- **Kabch'en** ("earth and caves") — the Maya city-state, the basic political unit.
- **K'uhul ajaw** — holy lord, the title of a dynastic king.
- **Kalomte'** — the paramount title for hegemonic rulers. Directional forms: *elk'in* (east), *ochk'in* (west), *nohol* (south), *xaman* (north).
- **Chante'chan** — the four heavens. The idea that at any given time there was an upper tier of four paramount rulers arranged cosmologically across the Maya world, matching what we see on the map.
- **Paskab** — "the world revealed."
- **E Groups** — early "time temples," cosmological center points and places of world renewal.
- **Long Count calendar**, made of **k'atuns** and **bak'tuns** — the 400-year bak'tun cycle that he speculates lines up with the rises and falls of Maya civilization.
- **The 260-day cycle** — shared across all Mesoamerican cultures.
- **"People without history"** — how outsiders saw the Maya before decipherment.

A theme more than a term: **cyclical time**. The Long Count runs through the whole book, and rulers, kings, and elites put a lot of emphasis and power onto specific dates.

## Key Ideas
This is a history more than an argument book, so these are insights rather than claims.

- **It was really just a bunch of city-states.** Very, very decentralized. It reminded me of the Greek or Mesopotamian early city-states. And it's crazy that this was happening in the 500s–700s, when over in Europe and Asia massive empires were forming — more centralized, more nationalized. The tech definitely moved at different rates, and you can see it reading this book.
- **The back-and-forth of the mid-first-millennium.** It's just fascinating to see the story of the rulers and the battles as the Maya themselves told it.
- **How much is still there.** Mesoamerica — the Yucatán, Honduras, Guatemala — is covered in Maya ruins. I was constantly looking up specific archaeological sites as I read. It's amazing how much is still present today.
- **A sense of loss.** How much of human history and culture is just no longer accessible to us. You imagine yourself in the place of these people and wonder what their lives were like, and whether we could ever recover that. It's not possible.
- **How differently they thought.** Their lives and values were very different from ours, even though they were the same kind of person we are today. You see this with other ancient civilizations too — having just gone to Pompeii, the people of the time valued very different things.

## Disagreements
Not applicable. It's a narrative history, and nothing struck me as overreach.

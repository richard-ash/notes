## Classification
A personal account of a French convict from the 1930s who was wrongly convicted and sentenced to hard labor for life in French Guiana. Memoir / adventure / classic. Whether it's fully true is contested (see Disagreements), so it reads as much like a semi-fictional prison story as a straight memoir.

## The Book in a Sentence
Papillon, a French convict, is wrongly convicted, sentenced to hard labor for life, and shipped off to French Guiana; he recounts his story and his many, many escape attempts and his final, successful escape.

## Structure
Chronological. From his conviction through the bagne, the islands, the repeated cavales, to the final escape where he succeeds and settles in Venezuela.

## The Problem
His aim was pretty clear: first, to showcase his own experience and story; second, to expose the brutality and what he saw as the wrongness and immorality of the French penal system.

He makes the case not just from his own perspective but from the perspective of peer nations. The English, for instance: "we thought France was a civilized nation, yet they're so uncivilized, sending all their convicts to a random part of the world." He also makes it through the generosity he receives from the indigenous Guajira population, complete strangers who take him in, and contrasts that against the brutality and unfairness of the French regime.

Did he succeed? I think so. I don't know much about France itself, but it made me curious to go look. If I were a French citizen it would make me very concerned about how we treat the most vulnerable parts of the population. (The bagne was actually closed by 1953, before the book came out in 1969, but the book cemented its reputation in public memory.)

## Key Terms
Mostly jargon of the bagne rather than concepts, but it sticks:
- **Cavale** — an escape attempt. The word that organizes the whole book.
- **Plan** — the metal tube of money convicts carry internally; the currency of survival and escape.
- **Bagne** — the penal colony system in French Guiana.
- **Relégués** — repeat petty offenders sent to the colony for life under a separate regime from convicts.
- **The islands** — Îles du Salut: Royale, Saint-Joseph (the réclusion / solitary), Devil's Island.

## Key Ideas
- **What does it even mean to be a civilized nation?** He compares the Indians to France on many occasions. The hospitality and generosity he receives from dirt-poor populations is set against a wealthy, technological, "civilized" France performing crueler actions than the peasants living right next door. Who is more civilized? What is a better place to live? Those questions come up a lot.
- **Striving vs. accepting your lot.** He keeps up the force of wanting to do the cavale repeatedly, even after more than a decade imprisoned. A lot of other inmates accept their circumstances and their lot in life and stop trying to escape. The book is an argument for always striving for the better life.
- **Poor incentives drive incredibly bad behavior.** Whether in the camps in French Guiana or the prison in Venezuela, the incentive systems of the prisons pull the worst behavior out of guards and inmates alike. He argues it's the incentives of the system that do that, not the men.
- **Perseverance.** He kept trying, again and again, for over a decade. That really stuck with me.
- **Honor among thieves.** He is super loyal, and it's incredible how he makes friends and comrades wherever he goes. The system is corrupt but the men inside it often aren't.
- **Revenge.** It fuels his desire to escape (the prosecutor, the false witness), and it shows up inside the prison too. When one of his friends is killed on the island, the inmates want revenge on the group that did it.
- **Solitude and the mind.** The solitary structures on Saint-Joseph seemed incredible. How would anyone endure that?
- **Redemption.** Venezuela takes him in when France wouldn't, and he becomes a citizen rather than a convict.

## Disagreements
Mostly not applicable. It's really just his perspective, almost a work of fiction, so it's not about agreeing or disagreeing. It's a case to learn from.

The one real caveat is authenticity. Charrière insisted the book was mostly true, but prison records don't show him among the successful escapees from the islands, Gérard de Villiers' *Papillon épinglé* argued only about a tenth of it was his own experience, a French justice ministry report said the episodes "should be divided by at least 10," and in 2005 Charles Brunier, a 104-year-old veteran with a butterfly tattoo who was on Devil's Island at the same time, claimed to be the real Papillon. Scholars now tend to class it as a novel loosely based on his life and the stories he heard in the bagne.

It doesn't really change how I feel about him. If it's a collection of stories, he's still the one who stuck his neck out and communicated them; otherwise we wouldn't have them. But he should have been upfront about it. I wouldn't have lied there. Maybe he did it so it would land harder with readers, and only once it got popular was he running it back.

## Takeaways
Not a change in how I act, so much as a new perspective. Every fictional or semi-fictional book shows you stories of lives you didn't live, shows you themes, and gives you a safe place to explore them. That's what this book did.

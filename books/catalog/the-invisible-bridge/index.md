## Classification
A history of the 1970s that traces the conservative intellectual movement from the fall of Nixon to the rise of Reagan, told through historical facts and anecdotes of how the transition happened. Follows up Perlstein's earlier book, Nixonland, which showed the rise of Nixon.

## The Book in a Sentence
A history of the 1970s that traces Nixon's fall to Reagan's rise.

## Structure
Chronological, like a typical history book. An introduction, then one event leading into the next.

## The Problem
Really this is a book about Reagan, what he stood for and why he stood for it. Perlstein doesn't show Reagan in the most positive light. He argues that Reagan encouraged the nation to think like a child might, with a very simplistic notion of good versus evil, and uses the book to trace how such a person was able to become president. What happened in the nation such that this could happen?

He succeeds more on the tracing than on the frame (see Key Ideas and Disagreements). The 3/5 is mostly pacing: very long-winded, slow at times, sometimes a slog to read through. Otherwise quite thorough and well done.

## Key Terms
The title is the one that resonates. It comes from Khrushchev's advice to Nixon: if the people believe there's an imaginary river out there, don't tell them there's no river. Build an imaginary bridge over it. Reagan as the bridge-builder.

Perlstein has other recurring vocabulary — the "suspicious circles" (the tribe that took the lesson of Vietnam and Watergate to be "question authority, unsettle ossified norms, expose dissembling leaders"), Reagan as an "athlete of the imagination" (his power at turning complexity and doubt into simplicity and certainty), the "cult of official optimism" — but they read as one-off phrases rather than frames that stick.

## Key Ideas
The core thesis comes in two parts, and they deserve separate grades.

- **The frame.** Reagan as a simpleton, someone with a very naive perspective on the world. This is one huge assumption underlying the book, and there are people who would disagree with it. Still hesitant on this one.
- **The tracing.** Given the frame, does Perlstein do a good job of showing how the US brought someone like that to be elected? Yes. He does an excellent job walking you through the events of the 60s and 70s that made it such that Reagan would be elected: the bombings, the Hearst kidnapping, the chaos in Vietnam, and many, many other examples.
- **The mechanism.** There was so much chaos that people were just done with it. They wanted an out, and psychologically didn't want to take ownership or step up and, as Perlstein puts it, act like adults. Relentless humiliation, a country exhausted by being told to reckon with itself, and then a hunger to just feel good about America again that Reagan was uniquely built to satisfy.

## Disagreements
The biggest disagreement is the framing of Reagan. Was he really that childlike in his thinking? Maybe this is my own naivete about Reagan, and it means I need to read a biography. The frame certainly tracks with the progressive view of Reagan, the one held by people who lived through it (Ava's parents, for instance). The angry-friend passage in the preface captures that sentiment exactly.

The sharper version of the objection: the ability to reduce things to a story people can act on is a really good skill and a huge talent. How much is Perlstein just getting mad about that ability and indexing on it? What we're seeing may not be childlike naivety about good versus evil. It's that Reagan was an incredible, incredible storyteller who could reduce very complex topics down to something that resembles a good-versus-evil frame. Perlstein describes a talent and calls it a deficiency.

## Takeaways
- Much more appreciation for how chaotic the 60s and 70s were. That gives a little more hope for the 2020s: things will work themselves out, because today seems not nearly as bad as it was back then. A good lens for viewing the events of the present.
- More curiosity about the era. How did all these events happen such that there was this huge awakening of chaos? How do we prevent something like that from happening again? Or was it a healthy response of the system: there was a lot wrong with America before, and the chaos was partly cleaning up the old world and making way for the new.
- That question maps onto today. Is Trump's destruction clearing out the old and making way for something new to be built, or is he just a madman agent of chaos tearing everything down around him? Both frames could be true. The question is which one is more representative of what's actually going on.
- Read a Reagan biography to settle the frame question. Candidates: Lou Cannon, *President Reagan: The Role of a Lifetime*; Max Boot, *Reagan: His Life and Legend* (2024); H.W. Brands, *Reagan: The Life*; and *Reagan, In His Own Hand*, the handwritten radio scripts that dented the "amiable dunce" image.

# The Unicontext

**The unicontext** is University of Chicago philosopher Agnes Callard's term for the condition in which one set of norms applies to every situation, replacing the many local, mutually distinct normative worlds humans have lived in for most of history. It is the load-bearing concept of a book she is currently writing, presented in long form on Derek Thompson's *Plain English* (July 2026).

Callard's starting definition: a **context** is a *normative* situation — a set of circumstances that tells you how you should act. Historically, contexts were local and multiple. To know how to behave you looked around: am I in a field, a home, a church, a bar? Physical environment and the behavior of nearby people supplied the answer immediately, without deliberation. The unicontext is the scenario where those answers converge — one set of norms, always, irrespective of where you are.

The thesis is offered as an answer to a question, not a verdict: **"Where are we?"** Callard's claim is that a large number of otherwise unrelated modern pathologies become predictable once you accept that the room you are standing in is not Chicago or your desk or an airplane — it is the unicontext.

## The key move: normative, not informational

The unicontext's nearest relative is **context collapse** — the ~15-year-old observation that a post on Twitter or Instagram reaches your boss, your mother, your college friends, your ex, and strangers simultaneously, flattening the differentiated selves you present offline.

Callard's distinction: context collapse is a claim about *information*; the unicontext is a claim about *norms*. Her disproof-by-counterexample is the peasant village. Informationally, a village was maximally collapsed — everyone knew everyone's business, families slept in one room, children had intimate knowledge of their parents' lives. Yet the village was nothing like our world, because different norms still governed children vs. parents, home vs. field, winter-gathering vs. summer work, and different points in the ritual calendar. Informational collapse without normative collapse is just a small town.

The corollary she draws is directional: **informational context collapse only becomes transformative once normative collapse has independently occurred.** This inverts the usual causal story — the internet is not primarily making us weird by showing our posts to the wrong audience; it is doing so because the norms governing our conduct stopped being local first.

A second refinement, easy to miss: the collapse Callard has her eye on is not mainly *global* reach. Even if the internet contained only New Yorkers, they would be reaching it from bars, from their kids' bedrooms, in different moods and at different hours. It is the collapse of *situational* contexts, not just cultural ones, that does the work.

## Technology as dialectic, not cause

Thompson presses the tech-determinist reading: radio, then television, then broadband, then smartphones progressively dissolved the local individual. (He cites the 1933 *Recent Social Trends* survey worrying that radio was "destroying individuality," and Joshua Meyrowitz's *No Sense of Place* making the same case for television.) Callard concedes trains, radio, TV, and the internet all advanced the unicontext.

But she declines pure determinism, and her objection is a good one: *the "and then you add to that" framing is doing unexamined work.* Why did we add it? Why did these particular technologies take off when most inventions don't? Her answer is that these technologies succeeded because they express a pre-existing human impulse — an **impatience with being trapped in a small world that presents itself as all of reality when you know it isn't**. The relationship is "conversational" or dialectical: technology facilitates the impulse; without the impulse it would flop.

She names the impulse late in the conversation: an aversion to **world closure**, an allergy to "the lie that says a contingent cultural convention is reality." Adventure, she notes, only takes you to a different place. The unicontext takes you to a different set of *norms* — a far more radical change.

## Four predicted consequences

The theory earns its keep by predicting specific, apparently unrelated features of modern life.

### 1. Negativity bias

**Goodness is more context-dependent than badness.** There is nothing that is good for everyone in all circumstances — "happiness" is a placeholder whose content varies with who and where you are. But there is a short list of near-universal evils: death, pain, illness, violence. You can recognize suffering in someone whose life you do not otherwise understand.

So if you are speaking into a collapsed context and want to be *legible*, negativity is the reliable coordination point. Thompson's worked example: "chicken enchiladas are delicious" is a fine thing to say to your wife and a dead post on Twitter; "white Americans who eat chicken enchiladas are appropriating Mexican culture" clicks into a global norm of badness and travels.

Callard adds a corrective that strengthens the argument: the problem isn't that the positive example was trivial. Substitute a genuinely major good — a great poem written in Persian — and it *still* doesn't travel, because it needs translation, time, and cultural assimilation before it can be evaluated. Significant goods are throttled by the same mechanism as trivial ones.

This is a supply-side account of online negativity that competes with the usual demand-side ones (engagement-optimizing algorithms, outrage as dopamine). Callard's version needs no algorithm at all: two strangers with no audience and no incentives, trying to find something to both care about, will still land on something bad. The mechanism is the coordination problem of shared meaning — the same machinery that makes brand advertising work in [[cultural-imprinting]], run in reverse.

### 2. Identity displaces character

**Character** is a set of dispositions governing how you navigate your emotional life across varied circumstances — courage in relation to fear, generosity, irascibility. Its defining property is that it manifests *differently* in different contexts. An irascible person isn't angry all the time; and in circumstances where everyone is angry, you can't see their irascibility at all. Perceiving character therefore requires knowing someone across many contexts.

**Identity** categories — woman, disabled, gay, Jewish, American — have the opposite property: "It's like a hat you never take off." You are them in every circumstance. They are precisely the categories that survive normative collapse, which is why they are the currency in which people are now understood.

Callard adds two supporting observations:
- The ethics of the unicontext is necessarily an **ethics of inclusion**, because the one thing the unicontext must be is a space for everybody. "Inclusion" as a named value is genuinely new — earlier societies essentially everywhere did not talk about it. And the natural way that ethic gets operationalized is through identity categories. Both political poles worry about excluded identities; they differ only about which ones.
- **Virtue ethics has a built-in positivity bias**. Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics* tells you what courage, wisdom, justice, and generosity *are*; the discussions of going wrong are predicated on first grasping the positive value. It is structurally the opposite of the bias we now have — which is why a language for character has atrophied while a language for identity has flourished.

Her classroom example is the sharpest illustration of what "unicontextual norms" means concretely. As a professor she can correct a student for arriving late, interrupting, or raising a hand to say something unrelated to the previous speaker. Other students cannot — those norms are **role-dependent**. But if a student makes a racist or sexist remark, any other student is in exactly the same position she is to call it out. Unicontextual norms are not role-dependent, and that parallel standing is what distinguishes them.

### 3. Comparison, then homogenization

When things enter the same *normative* context, they enter the same evaluative field, and comparison becomes not just possible but compulsory.

Callard's school-district example isolates the mechanism from information: two districts, two high schools, plenty of public information about both, no motivation to compare — you go to the one you live in. Change the rule so you may attend either, and suddenly you need graduation rates, college placements, AP counts. Nothing informational changed; the *choice relation* did. And the downstream effect is that the school doing individualized curriculum starts teaching AP classes. **The comparison homogenizes what it compares.** (Her lighter version: an all-day-breakfast menu forces you to compare French toast against a hamburger on carbs vs. protein, a comparison you previously had no reason to make — and, at scale, generates the nutritional-comparison technologies to perform it.)

Thompson's extension is baseball: 30 teams with private scouts (context-dependent evaluation) → shared statistics like OPS and WAR (a single comparative field, sortable in Excel) → every team running identical pitcher and hitter strategies, three true outcomes, swing for the fences. Unicontext → comparative field → homogenization.

This connects the unicontext to the monoculture problem in [[cultural-drift-maladaptation]]: Hanson worries that cultural variety has collapsed and selection pressure has vanished. Callard supplies a mechanism for the first half — a single evaluative field is a variety-destroying machine, and it operates by competitive imitation rather than by anyone deciding on uniformity.

### 4. Top-down attention management

The human default is **bottom-up** attention: you attend to what is salient, and distractibility is adaptive for a creature that may at any moment face a fire or a predator. Callard argues the unicontext installs **top-down** attention management — a standing obligation to decide what to attend to, and a standing sense of failing at it. "We have all kinds of products to help us fight against the very objects that we purchase."

Her evidence is a nice piece of historical-literary observation. Nineteenth-century novels are full of scenes where a woman — Emma Bovary, Anna Karenina — is reading a novel, gets distracted by her turbulent love life, and puts the book down. Distraction is everywhere in these scenes. **What never happens is the character saying to herself: "No, I need to get through another twenty pages."** That metacognitive attention-policing simply wasn't a thing people did.

Why does this prove the unicontext? Because the question "what should I attend to?" has become detached from the local environment. A screen is present in every environment, so local cues cannot answer it; the screen puts everything — including the suffering of people far away — into one evaluative field, and you now stand in a *choice relation* to your own attention. Musil saw the newspaper doing this already: "the probability of experiencing something unusual through the newspapers is much greater than that of experiencing it in person. In other words, the more important things take place today in the abstract and the more trivial ones in real life."

Thompson's gloss is the useful one: in a universal room where everything you could be doing is present, **opportunity cost feels permanently expensive**, so attention presents itself as a scarce resource to optimize rather than a river that flows through you. This is the diagnosis that most productivity advice treats as a given rather than a symptom — see [[personal-productivity-systems]] for the practice-level literature that assumes top-down attention control is simply correct.

## The 1910 inflection and the fragmentation puzzle

Callard dates the unicontext's *appearance* — the moment when a critical mass of people noticed it — to roughly 1910, and identifies the modernist novel as the genre invented to cope with it: Musil's *The Man Without Qualities* (her number one), Broch's *The Sleepwalkers* and *The Death of Virgil*, Woolf, Pessoa, Joyce, alongside theorists Simmel, Weber, and Heidegger.

The puzzle she flags is that these writers described the phenomenon in language that sounds like the *opposite* of a unicontext: **fragmentation**, everything breaking apart. Her resolution is that fragmentation is the early phase and homogenization the late one. When many previously separate normative worlds are first thrown into a single evaluative field, you do not yet possess the technologies of commensurability required to compare them — how do you reconcile the schoolteacher who becomes a murderer, or relate art to religion? — so the experience is of overwhelming multiplicity. Only later do the shared metrics arrive and grind the differences down.

This is an underrated part of the theory: it means the phenomenology of the unicontext *inverts over time*, and that the disorientation of 1910 and the sameness of 2026 are two stages of one process rather than two different diagnoses.

## Simmel, money, and the abstraction thread

Thompson proposes that the unicontext is really a claim that we all now live inside a global *marketplace* — markets being the canonical machine for creating a single comparative field — and cites Simmel's *The Metropolis and Mental Life*: "Money takes the place of all the manifoldness of things and expresses all qualitative distinctions between them in the distinction of how much... it becomes the frightful leveler."

Callard accepts "marketplace" only as a metaphor, and pushes to what she takes to be Simmel's actual claim in *The Philosophy of Money*: **money is abstraction in the space of value.** Every other value concept is tied concretely to objects; money is the endpoint of a long growth in the human capacity to think about value abstractly — and that capacity in turn transformed money itself (large physical objects representing large sums → fiat currency).

So Simmel would not describe the change as fragmentation. He would describe it as **the rise of abstraction**. And that is Callard's own deeper thread: in a local environment you don't reason "given that I'm in a bar of such-and-such character, what should I do?" — you respond immediately through sensory features. In the universal room, your response is *mediated through abstraction*. "We live very abstractly. We are managing ourselves and our experience in very top-down ways."

The top-down attention story is a special case of this. So, arguably, is the identity story: identity categories are abstractions that survive decontextualization, character is a concrete pattern that doesn't.

## Is it good or bad? — a deliberately withheld verdict

Callard declines to judge, on two distinct grounds.

The first is Simmel's, which she endorses by recommendation: the metropolitan mind is a genuinely new mood of life, but *we cannot judge it because we are its products — it is us*. The idea that we can step outside and pronounce on it is an illusion.

The second is self-referential and sharper. **"The unicontext can't really see good. It can only see bads."** So asking whether the unicontext is good or bad is a loaded question — asked from inside the unicontext, it will reliably return "bad." She notes that literally no one who hears the theory responds that it sounds great. That unanimity is itself evidence of the negativity mechanism operating, not evidence about the thing.

Her stated priority is therefore orientation before evaluation: "get our bearings for where we are... before we start judging," because the unicontext is "a super judgy place" with a universal moralism applicable to every situation, and being pulled into that moralism displaces understanding.

Thompson pushes for a prescription and explicitly pre-rejects the obvious one — put the phone away at family dinner — as cheap. Callard does not supply a replacement. What she offers instead is a reframe: the supposedly bad thing is something *we are choosing, repeatedly*, including by having this very conversation across a distance about an abstraction. The book she is writing has a second, unfinished half about where that choosing comes from psychologically. Her current word for it is **unruliness** — the unicontext as a space where humanity's aversion to world closure gets expressed. The question of human flourishing then becomes: does the drive for world openness find a place inside the unicontext, or is it being quashed by it?

## What the theory does and doesn't buy you

Worth being explicit about the theory's status, since it is presented as a "theory of everything" and is not yet a book:

- **Its explanatory reach is genuinely unusual.** Online negativity, identity politics on both left and right, strategic homogenization in baseball, the productivity-and-focus industry, and the mood of modernist literature are not usually explained by one thing. Callard derives all five from a single structural claim about norms.
- **It is not obviously falsifiable in its current form.** The negativity-of-reactions-to-the-theory move — everyone thinks it's bad, which the theory predicts — is elegant but also insulates the theory from a class of objection. Several of the predictions (negativity bias, homogenization under shared metrics) have competing explanations that don't require the unicontext.
- **The dialectical move on technology is the load-bearing and least-developed part.** "Technology plus a human impulse toward world-openness" is more satisfying than determinism, but the impulse is posited rather than evidenced, and Callard says she hasn't worked it out yet.
- **The normative/informational distinction is the most portable idea here.** Even if you reject the theory of everything, "context collapse is about information; the harder thing is that the *norms* stopped being local" is a real refinement of a widely used concept, and the peasant-village counterexample earns it.

The identity-vs-character section also connects usefully to [[consumption-identity]] — Smith argues consumption choices individuate more than production does, which fits a world where portable, always-on markers are the available currency of selfhood. And the legibility argument at the heart of the negativity mechanism (only what is legible to everyone travels) is the same structure as the [[legibility-asymmetry]] problem, applied to moral discourse rather than to innovation.

# How Exceptional Performance Develops

A 2025 *Science* review by Güllich, Barth, Hambrick and Macnamara pulls together 19 datasets on 34,839 adult world-class performers (Nobel laureates, Olympic champions, the world's best chess players, the most renowned classical composers) and finds that **the people who end up at the very top developed differently from the people who were best when young**. Early stars and adult stars are mostly different people, the adult stars rose more slowly, and the things that predict junior success predict adult world-class success in the opposite direction.

The review matters because almost all earlier expertise research (the deliberate-practice tradition included) studied young or sub-elite performers, and elite schools, conservatories and youth academies were built on those results: pick the best kids early, then intensify practice in the one discipline.

## The three findings

### 1. Early and later top performers are two different populations

Combining forward-looking studies (follow the best juniors) with backward-looking ones (trace the best adults), about 90% of the names differ.

| Comparison | Same people | Different people |
|---|---|---|
| International championships, junior vs senior athletes | 13% | 87% |
| International medals, junior vs senior athletes | 12% | 88% |
| World chess top 10, under-14 vs adult | 11% | 89% |
| World chess top 3, under-14 vs adult | 12% | 88% |
| Top 6.7% at secondary school vs elite-university degree | 9% | 91% |
| Top 1% cognitive score at 12 vs top 5% salary in mid-30s | 1% | 99% |
| Highly selective school vs top 5% salary at 42 | 8% | 92% |
| Highly selective university vs top 5% salary in late 20s | 15% | 85% |

In sport, 82% of international-level juniors never reach the international senior level, and 72% of international-level seniors were never international-level juniors.

### 2. World-class performers start behind and peak later

Compared with peers who finished one step below them, the eventual best performed *worse* early on.

- **Athletes:** 508 world-class versus 420 national-class athletes. The national-class group is ahead through the teens; the lines cross around age 19.
- **Scientists:** 330 physics and chemistry Nobel laureates versus 1,595 nominees who never won. By citation ranking the nominees are ahead early; the laureates pass them roughly 12 years before the prize.
- **Chess:** the adult world top 3 were 48 Elo points above players ranked 4th to 10th at peak, and 62 points *below* them at age 14. Fewer of the top 3 had the grandmaster title by 14.
- Nobel laureates were also less likely to have held a student scholarship and took longer to reach a first full professorship than winners of the highest national award.

Across the highest levels, peak performance is negatively correlated with early performance.

### 3. The predictors flip sign

Meta-analytic effects in sport (Cohen's d), comparing higher versus lower junior performers and then world-class versus national-class seniors:

| Predictor | Best juniors | World-class adults |
|---|---|---|
| Age starting the main sport | earlier (−0.33) | later (+0.41) |
| Age entering a talent program | earlier (−0.53) | later (+0.65) |
| Age reaching milestones | earlier (−0.49) | later (+0.42) |
| Amount of main-sport practice | more (+0.61) | less (−0.23) |
| Amount of practice in other sports | less (−0.23) | more (+0.50) |

World-class athletes played on average two other sports for nine years in childhood and adolescence. The pattern repeats outside sport:

- **Science:** Nobel laureates did more study, practice and work outside their own discipline, and outside science altogether (arts, music, crafts, other professions), than national-level awardees. They averaged about two additional avocations.
- **Music:** in Simonton's study of 911 operas by 59 composers, an opera's success fell with the number of same-genre operas the composer had already written and rose with the number of earlier compositions in other genres.
- **Hot streaks:** Liu and colleagues found that multiyear bursts of high-impact work by scientists, artists and film directors were consistently preceded by a period of work across other disciplines, genres or styles.

Pooled effect sizes on world-class performance sit in a narrow band across fields: more multidisciplinary practice d = 0.51 (athletics), 0.58 (science), 0.39 (music); more gradual early progress d = 0.43 (athletics), 0.41 (science), 0.56 (chess). The three predictors (slower early progress, less main-discipline practice, more practice elsewhere) are correlated with one another, so they read as one pattern, not three independent levers.

## What this does not say

- **Early stars do not have worse odds.** Juniors who reach international championships are about 49 times as likely to reach them as seniors as other young athletes are (seven samples, N = 1.8 million). But they are under 1% of athletes, so more than 70% of international-level seniors come from the other 99%. This is a base-rate result of exactly the kind [[probability-neglect]] describes: a high conditional probability on a tiny group loses to a low one on a huge group.
- **It does not say practice is unimportant.** Many world-class adults practised a great deal in their own discipline and were above average as children. The top adults and their near-peers resemble each other far more than either resembles the general population. What is rare among them is the *accelerated* profile: early start, single focus, maximum volume.
- **It is correlational.** The review reports associations and effect sizes, not interventions.
- **Statistical artifacts.** The authors address restriction of range and regression to the mean, and argue that neither would produce a *negative* early–peak association or opposite-signed predictors. That is their argument; the review does not model it formally.

## Three proposed explanations

The authors say existing theories cannot account for the pattern and offer three hypotheses, explicitly as starting points:

1. **Search and match** (from labor economics). Trying several disciplines raises the odds of finding the one that best fits a person's talents and preferences.
2. **Enhanced learning capital** (from transfer-of-learning theory). Varied tasks and methods build flexible thinking and the ability to learn, which later speeds discipline-specific learning.
3. **Limited risks.** Broader, less intense early practice lowers exposure to burnout, overuse injury, opportunity costs, and being locked into a discipline one stops enjoying.

## What the authors recommend

- Early top performance is not a sensible selection criterion for long-term potential. Selecting on it captures a minority of future top adults.
- The better early signals are above-average but not top performance, considerable but not excessive main-discipline practice, and substantial practice in other disciplines.
- About two additional disciplines appears to be a good breadth-to-depth ratio. The other disciplines need not resemble the main one.
- Selection by early performance has a "radiating" effect: children, parents and teachers accelerate early performance in the years *before* the selection age to get in.
- Judging programs and teachers on current-year results rewards the junior recipe. Evaluation should follow participants through to peak age.

## Synthesis

- **Selection systems optimize for the wrong date.** A program graded on this year's results will rationally produce early peaks. The findings are less a claim about talent than about what happens when the measurement horizon is shorter than the development horizon.
- **It cuts against naive [[cumulative-advantage]].** A Matthew-effect reading predicts that early leads compound through access to coaching, squads and scholarships. At the very top the lead does not compound; the early-selected group is overtaken. Early advantage still helps reach the *sub-elite* level, which is where most earlier research looked. [[relative-age-effects]] are a related case: early selection partly picks whoever is oldest in the cohort, another reason early rank is a noisy signal of adult rank.
- **Breadth here is a multi-year second and third practice, not dabbling.** World-class athletes kept other sports for nine years; laureates kept serious avocations. That is compatible with the argument in [[commit-and-go-deep]] that depth is what generates information. The search-and-match hypothesis is the same idea on a longer clock: you commit hard enough to several things to learn which one fits.
- **Elite formation by light early load has a historical echo.** [[victorian-ruling-class]] describes an elite schooled with little academic intensity; the mechanisms differ, but both are evidence against the assumption that maximal early specialization is how top adult performers are made.
- **Open question the review leaves.** If the three predictors are one correlated bundle, it is unclear which is causal. Slower early progress could simply be the observable side effect of spreading practice across disciplines, with breadth doing the work.

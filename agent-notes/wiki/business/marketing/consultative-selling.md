# Consultative Selling (Cheng's PCNS Framework)

**Consultative selling** inverts the popular image of sales: instead of a
smooth-talking extrovert pitching a product, the seller acts as a consultant —
asking questions, diagnosing the buyer's *perceived* problem, and leading them to
their own decision. The canonical practitioner statement here is Victor Cheng's
*How to Sell Your Ideas in Everyday Life* (2016), whose core is the **PCNS
framework — Problem, Consequences, Needs, Solution** (mnemonic: "Please Close
Numerous Sales"). Cheng frames the whole thing for non-salespeople — introverts,
engineers, anyone selling an *idea* to a boss, spouse, or child — and argues the
approach structurally favors the thoughtful listener over the charismatic talker.

This is the tactical sibling of [[credibility-based-selling]]: where Naval and the
goatherders argue that sales is a *byproduct* of credibility and genuine
usefulness, Cheng supplies the moment-to-moment mechanics of the sales conversation
itself once you're in the room.

## The two-step foundation and the sequencing rule

Cheng reduces selling to two steps that he claims ~90% of professionals get
backwards:

1. **Figure out what the other person wants.** (Most people skip this.)
2. **Give them what they want** — in a way that happens to include you getting
   what *you* want.

The load-bearing claim is the **sequencing**: "You get what you want only *after*
the other person gets what they want first." Your own payoff is a byproduct, not
the focus. Cheng argues this is exactly why most people dislike sales and are bad
at it — sellers focus on what *they* want. Two assumptions ride underneath: you
must actually **know** what the buyer wants (hence listening beats talking), and
you must actually be **able to deliver** it (because "bad deals ruin
relationships").

**Implication the source leaves implicit:** the entire framework is a machine for
*discovering* the buyer's wants before committing to a solution. Steps 1–3 (PCNS's
P, C, N) are all elicitation; only step 4 (S) is anything like a pitch. The
"favors introverts" claim follows directly — three of the four steps are keeping
your mouth shut and asking good questions.

## The PCNS framework

- **P — Problem.** Ask questions to surface the buyer's *perception* of the
  problem. The word "perceive" is critical: people only buy solutions to problems
  they *perceive* having, regardless of what problem they objectively have. Start
  with their perception even when it's a misdiagnosis (the cough is a symptom of
  the cold is a symptom of poor sleep) — you can re-educate them later, but
  correcting them up front ("no, you're wrong") breaks the connection. **Mirror**
  their exact words back to signal you understand.
- **C — Consequences (negative then positive).** Ask what happens if they *don't*
  solve the problem (cost of the status quo), then what life looks like if they
  *do*. Cheng's sharpest reframe lives here: **"Your #1 competitor is not another
  company — it's doing nothing."** In the 2008–09 recession, prospects who said
  "no" didn't buy from a rival, they simply didn't buy. The step's job is to make
  inaction feel expensive — quantified ("a $30M headache over three years") *and*
  felt emotionally ("toast of the family at Thanksgiving").
- **N — Needs.** Convert implicit needs into explicit, verified criteria for an
  acceptable solution. Buyers often fail to buy not from disinterest but because
  they *haven't finished thinking the decision through* — so you think it through
  *with* them ("What would an acceptable solution need to include? How will you
  know in six months you chose well?"), then confirm: "Did I understand you
  correctly, or did I misunderstand something?"
- **S — Solution.** Only now do you present — and you **connect the dots**: show
  (not just say) how each component maps to a stated need, avoids a named negative
  consequence, and delivers a named positive one. The framework is "very
  frontloaded"; by the time you reach S, the conceptual sale is usually already
  made.

The framework is explicitly **conversational, not linear** — you loop back and
forth across P, C, N as the dialog wanders; you just need to cover all three before
S. Cheng contrasts this with the deliberately linear case interview (which tests
linear thinking) — a distinction worth noting given the source is from
CaseInterview.com. In real client work, P/C/N/S are often split across many
one-on-one meetings, building a **composite** of every stakeholder's needs before
the final presentation.

## The "big secret": sell with the buyer's own words

Cheng's most distinctive tactic: **"I never use my own words to convince a buyer.
I quote *their* words back to them."** Because the questions in P and C get the
buyer to articulate the problem, the cost of inaction, and the payoff in their own
language, the eventual proposal is largely a mirror. "They rarely argue... in their
eyes I sound brilliant — I'm just quoting their own words." His six-figure
consulting proposals reportedly open every time with "Based on our conversation
last Friday, it's my understanding you want to solve the following three
problems...". This is why the four "common mistakes" are all just *skipping a PCNS
step* — skip P and you misread the problem; skip C and there's no urgency; skip N
and the buyer stalls ("let me think about it"); skip everything and you barge in
pitching a minivan to someone shopping for her daughter.

## The integrity argument

Cheng's answer to "how do I sell without feeling sleazy" is that the sleazy feeling
is real information: it appears precisely when you're selling something that doesn't
work or that you don't believe in. His mechanism:

- **Selling = the transfer of confidence.** To sell is to say "I'm more confident I
  can solve your problem than you are that you can solve it yourself." Buyers detect
  a seller who lacks this.
- **Conviction requires congruence** — alignment of what you feel, think, say, and
  do. **Incongruence** (feeling "50% chance," saying "no problem") is where the
  discomfort comes from, and buyers can often sense it. The corollary is that
  passionate non-salespeople (a scientist with a real breakthrough) often
  out-sell trained reps because their conviction is congruent.
- Therefore the integrity rules are also *effectiveness* rules: if you can't solve
  it, say so and refer out; if the customer shouldn't buy, tell them (it banks
  trust for a future recommendation). This is the same logic as Naval's "walk away
  from deals that aren't win-win" in [[credibility-based-selling]].

## Long-term orientation and the ranking of outcomes

The philosophical spine is **valuing a lifetime of sales over any single
transaction** — the NPV of a decade of repeat business and referrals dwarfs one
deal. Cheng ranks outcomes, unusually placing "I win, you lose" *below* "I lose,
you lose":

1. **I win, you win *more*** (best — pleasant surprise drives repeat business)
2. I win, you win
3. I don't win nor lose, but you win (referrals; free content as a credibility
   engine — how CaseInterview.com itself was bootstrapped)
4. I lose, you lose ("just a bad decision made together")
5. **I win, you lose** (worst — even if unintentional, "it feels like I ripped you
   off," which poisons the relationship)

The ordering only makes sense through the long-term lens: a lose-lose is a shared
mistake you can recover from, but a win-lose leaves the counterparty feeling
cheated and ends the relationship. Cheng's illustrative mentors both *declined*
large commissions rather than sell a client something that would fail them — the
$24M-not-$20M software rep, and the $50M/yr CEO who won't sell what she can't
deliver.

## Objection-handling and advanced notes

- **"Too many questions"** — always **ask permission** ("Could I ask a few
  questions to save you time?"), which leaves the buyer in the driver's seat, and
  mix questions with *educating* so it doesn't feel like an interrogation. The
  higher-status the buyer (executives, high-net-worth), the more you must ask rather
  than push — "if you dominate them, that's the quickest way to piss them off."
- **No real urgency?** Then there isn't any, whether you raise it or not — and a
  happy deal in two weeks beats a destroyed relationship from pushing to close now.
  Diagnose the *value* behind a delay and try to satisfy it immediately (pulling up
  the crash/pricing data the buyer wanted to go research).
- **Discomfort is a signal.** The negative-consequences step (life insurance's
  "visualize your death") can go too far; read the buyer's discomfort and back off.
- **Differentiation is strategy, not sales.** If you have nothing genuinely
  *different or better* than a rival, that's a strategy problem — no sales technique
  fixes it. Compete by offering something the incumbent structurally can't.
- **Multi-decision-maker sales** (buyer ≠ user ≠ budget holder; "businesses don't
  buy things, people in businesses buy things") and **proof of capability**
  (guarantees, publicity, references) are flagged as core to real B2B selling and
  deferred to Cheng's later modules.
- **Building trust cold** uses **"education-based selling"** ("edu-selling") — the
  McKinsey model of giving away proprietary insight up front (an executive briefing,
  a white paper, free content) to earn the credibility that makes the PCNS
  conversation possible in the first place. This is the explicit bridge back to
  [[credibility-based-selling]]: edu-selling is a *method for manufacturing* the
  credibility substrate that Naval treats as a precondition.

## Assessment

The material is one practitioner's opinionated system, not independently validated
research, so claims like "90% of salespeople get it backwards" and the specific
mentor anecdotes should be read as persuasive illustration rather than evidence
(hence `medium` confidence). But PCNS is a clean, memorable restatement of the
consultative/needs-based selling tradition (it rhymes with SPIN selling's
Situation-Problem-Implication-Need-payoff structure), and its reframes — "your
competitor is doing nothing," "quote their own words back," "discomfort is a
signal," and the win-lose-is-worse-than-lose-lose ranking — are genuinely useful
mental handles. The strongest through-line is that **every tactic is downstream of
a long-term-relationship objective**; drop that premise and the integrity rules
look like naïveté rather than strategy.

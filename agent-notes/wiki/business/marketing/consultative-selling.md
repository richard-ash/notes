# Consultative Selling (Cheng's PCNS Framework)

**Consultative selling** inverts the popular image of sales: instead of a
smooth-talking extrovert pitching a product, the seller acts as a consultant —
asking questions, diagnosing the buyer's *perceived* problem, and leading them to
their own decision. The canonical practitioner statement here is Victor Cheng's
*How to Sell Your Ideas in Everyday Life* (2016), whose core is the **PCNS
framework — Problem, Consequences, Needs, Solution** (mnemonic: "Please Close
Numerous Sales"). Cheng frames the whole thing for non-salespeople — introverts,
engineers, anyone selling an *idea* to a boss, spouse, or child — and argues the
approach structurally favors the thoughtful listener over the charismatic talker.

This is the tactical sibling of [[credibility-based-selling]]: where Naval and the
goatherders argue that sales is a *byproduct* of credibility and genuine
usefulness, Cheng supplies the moment-to-moment mechanics of the sales conversation
itself once you're in the room.

## The two-step foundation and the sequencing rule

Cheng reduces selling to two steps that he claims ~90% of professionals get
backwards:

1. **Figure out what the other person wants.** (Most people skip this.)
2. **Give them what they want** — in a way that happens to include you getting
   what *you* want.

The load-bearing claim is the **sequencing**: "You get what you want only *after*
the other person gets what they want first." Your own payoff is a byproduct, not
the focus. Cheng argues this is exactly why most people dislike sales and are bad
at it — sellers focus on what *they* want. Two assumptions ride underneath: you
must actually **know** what the buyer wants (hence listening beats talking), and
you must actually be **able to deliver** it (because "bad deals ruin
relationships").

**Implication the source leaves implicit:** the entire framework is a machine for
*discovering* the buyer's wants before committing to a solution. Steps 1–3 (PCNS's
P, C, N) are all elicitation; only step 4 (S) is anything like a pitch. The
"favors introverts" claim follows directly — three of the four steps are keeping
your mouth shut and asking good questions.

## The PCNS framework

- **P — Problem.** Ask questions to surface the buyer's *perception* of the
  problem. The word "perceive" is critical: people only buy solutions to problems
  they *perceive* having, regardless of what problem they objectively have. Start
  with their perception even when it's a misdiagnosis (the cough is a symptom of
  the cold is a symptom of poor sleep) — you can re-educate them later, but
  correcting them up front ("no, you're wrong") breaks the connection. **Mirror**
  their exact words back to signal you understand.
- **C — Consequences (negative then positive).** Ask what happens if they *don't*
  solve the problem (cost of the status quo), then what life looks like if they
  *do*. Cheng's sharpest reframe lives here: **"Your #1 competitor is not another
  company — it's doing nothing."** In the 2008–09 recession, prospects who said
  "no" didn't buy from a rival, they simply didn't buy. The step's job is to make
  inaction feel expensive — quantified ("a $30M headache over three years") *and*
  felt emotionally ("toast of the family at Thanksgiving").
- **N — Needs.** Convert implicit needs into explicit, verified criteria for an
  acceptable solution. Buyers often fail to buy not from disinterest but because
  they *haven't finished thinking the decision through* — so you think it through
  *with* them ("What would an acceptable solution need to include? How will you
  know in six months you chose well?"), then confirm: "Did I understand you
  correctly, or did I misunderstand something?"
- **S — Solution.** Only now do you present — and you **connect the dots**: show
  (not just say) how each component maps to a stated need, avoids a named negative
  consequence, and delivers a named positive one. The framework is "very
  frontloaded"; by the time you reach S, the conceptual sale is usually already
  made.

The framework is explicitly **conversational, not linear** — you loop back and
forth across P, C, N as the dialog wanders; you just need to cover all three before
S. Cheng contrasts this with the deliberately linear case interview (which tests
linear thinking) — a distinction worth noting given the source is from
CaseInterview.com. In real client work, P/C/N/S are often split across many
one-on-one meetings, building a **composite** of every stakeholder's needs before
the final presentation.

## The "big secret": sell with the buyer's own words

Cheng's most distinctive tactic: **"I never use my own words to convince a buyer.
I quote *their* words back to them."** Because the questions in P and C get the
buyer to articulate the problem, the cost of inaction, and the payoff in their own
language, the eventual proposal is largely a mirror. "They rarely argue... in their
eyes I sound brilliant — I'm just quoting their own words." His six-figure
consulting proposals reportedly open every time with "Based on our conversation
last Friday, it's my understanding you want to solve the following three
problems...". This is why the four "common mistakes" are all just *skipping a PCNS
step* — skip P and you misread the problem; skip C and there's no urgency; skip N
and the buyer stalls ("let me think about it"); skip everything and you barge in
pitching a minivan to someone shopping for her daughter.

## The integrity argument

Cheng's answer to "how do I sell without feeling sleazy" is that the sleazy feeling
is real information: it appears precisely when you're selling something that doesn't
work or that you don't believe in. His mechanism:

- **Selling = the transfer of confidence.** To sell is to say "I'm more confident I
  can solve your problem than you are that you can solve it yourself." Buyers detect
  a seller who lacks this.
- **Conviction requires congruence** — alignment of what you feel, think, say, and
  do. **Incongruence** (feeling "50% chance," saying "no problem") is where the
  discomfort comes from, and buyers can often sense it. The corollary is that
  passionate non-salespeople (a scientist with a real breakthrough) often
  out-sell trained reps because their conviction is congruent.
- Therefore the integrity rules are also *effectiveness* rules: if you can't solve
  it, say so and refer out; if the customer shouldn't buy, tell them (it banks
  trust for a future recommendation). This is the same logic as Naval's "walk away
  from deals that aren't win-win" in [[credibility-based-selling]].

## Long-term orientation and the ranking of outcomes

The philosophical spine is **valuing a lifetime of sales over any single
transaction** — the NPV of a decade of repeat business and referrals dwarfs one
deal. Cheng ranks outcomes, unusually placing "I win, you lose" *below* "I lose,
you lose":

1. **I win, you win *more*** (best — pleasant surprise drives repeat business)
2. I win, you win
3. I don't win nor lose, but you win (referrals; free content as a credibility
   engine — how CaseInterview.com itself was bootstrapped)
4. I lose, you lose ("just a bad decision made together")
5. **I win, you lose** (worst — even if unintentional, "it feels like I ripped you
   off," which poisons the relationship)

The ordering only makes sense through the long-term lens: a lose-lose is a shared
mistake you can recover from, but a win-lose leaves the counterparty feeling
cheated and ends the relationship. Cheng's illustrative mentors both *declined*
large commissions rather than sell a client something that would fail them — the
$24M-not-$20M software rep, and the $50M/yr CEO who won't sell what she can't
deliver.

## Objection-handling and advanced notes

- **"Too many questions"** — always **ask permission** ("Could I ask a few
  questions to save you time?"), which leaves the buyer in the driver's seat, and
  mix questions with *educating* so it doesn't feel like an interrogation. The
  higher-status the buyer (executives, high-net-worth), the more you must ask rather
  than push — "if you dominate them, that's the quickest way to piss them off."
- **No real urgency?** Then there isn't any, whether you raise it or not — and a
  happy deal in two weeks beats a destroyed relationship from pushing to close now.
  Diagnose the *value* behind a delay and try to satisfy it immediately (pulling up
  the crash/pricing data the buyer wanted to go research).
- **Discomfort is a signal.** The negative-consequences step (life insurance's
  "visualize your death") can go too far; read the buyer's discomfort and back off.
- **Differentiation is strategy, not sales.** If you have nothing genuinely
  *different or better* than a rival, that's a strategy problem — no sales technique
  fixes it. Compete by offering something the incumbent structurally can't.
- **Multi-decision-maker sales** (buyer ≠ user ≠ budget holder; "businesses don't
  buy things, people in businesses buy things") and **proof of capability**
  (guarantees, publicity, references) are flagged in Module 1 as core to real
  B2B selling; Module 2 delivers both — see the Proof of Capability section
  below and [[multi-stakeholder-selling]].
- **Building trust cold** uses **"education-based selling"** ("edu-selling") — the
  McKinsey model of giving away proprietary insight up front (an executive briefing,
  a white paper, free content) to earn the credibility that makes the PCNS
  conversation possible in the first place. This is the explicit bridge back to
  [[credibility-based-selling]]: edu-selling is a *method for manufacturing* the
  credibility substrate that Naval treats as a precondition.

## Proof of Capability: the fifth step (Module 2)

Module 2 adds a step Cheng admits he omitted from Module 1 "due to my own
bias": nearly all his selling is to repeat clients, where track record is
implicit. **When there is no prior relationship, PCNS becomes PCNS+P — Problem,
Consequences, Needs, Solution, Proof of Capability**: evidence that you can
deliver what the solution promises. His inventory of proof, roughly in order of
strength:

1. **The buyer's own prior experience of your work** — a boss, a colleague, a
   past client; also old clients who follow a rep to a new company because "you
   never steered them wrong." Reputation *is* proof, accumulated.
2. **A referral from someone the buyer trusts** — the McKinsey model: a Fortune
   500 CEO amazed by the work sits on another board with six other CEOs.
3. **A free or low-cost sample: a pilot, a test.** Cheng "likes pilot projects a
   lot" — the answer for anyone whose large, expensive proposal keeps getting
   shut down. This is the account-management move in
   [[strategy-to-sales-call-hierarchy]] seen from the proof side.
4. **A demonstration of value.** Cheng's habit is to *just start consulting* —
   twenty or thirty minutes of problem-structuring on the prospect's actual
   issue, which ends with "you understand this better than we do; could you
   help?" McKinsey's version was the proprietary-research cold call ("we've
   benchmarked every major player on online policy origination; you might want
   our point of view") that earns a dinner with a CEO the firm has no
   relationship with. Technical products do it with a technical demo.
5. **High-profile clients.** Silicon Valley enterprise vendors give a
   million-dollar product to a well-known Fortune 500 in exchange for a public
   reference; it "builds momentum." (The same logic, applied to a startup's first
   users, is [[design-partners]].)
6. **Symbols of credibility** — a seat on the standards committee, leadership
   of the industry association, and above all books: "any time I've tried to
   sell a consulting project, I've already written a book about the topic, and I
   always send that book in advance... It changes the entire sales conversation
   110%."

The step is not always last. Cheng will "do PCNS in reverse" — prove capability
first in a speech to fifteen CEOs, then have the P/C/N conversation later — and
says the less the audience knows you, the more the framework tilts toward proof.
This makes explicit what the edu-selling note above only implied: Proof of
Capability is the *mechanism* that produces the credibility
[[credibility-based-selling]] treats as a precondition, and it answers the
Q&A worry that the Problem step "puts the client on the defensive." Cheng's
reply is that a buyer without the problem simply ends the call ("if there is no
problem, there is no sale," in his mentor's motto; his own 2009 opener, "are you
struggling to survive the worst recession of our generation?" — "no, we're fine"
— "okay," end of conversation), and that resistance to discussing a problem
almost always means *insufficient credibility beforehand*: send the clippings,
the books, the client list first, and "it's a totally different conversation."
The exception is problems with social stigma (erectile dysfunction, depression),
which need a safe, confidential container and are best broached non-verbally
first — an article handed over with "no idea if this interests you; if it does I
can refer you to someone."

## The six common scenarios

Module 2 then runs PCNS(+P) through six situations. The two about *who* you can
talk to — no access to the decision-maker, and multiple decision-makers,
including pre-syndication and coaching the internal champion — are compiled
separately in [[multi-stakeholder-selling]]. The other four:

**Selling to a persona (scenario 3).** For low-price, low-involvement products —
a can of Coke, garbage collection — nobody will sit through P/C/N questions. So
you *split the framework*: do P, C and N as market research with people
representative of the buyer, then present S to the actual buyers through
advertising and packaging. Cheng's role-play is a hypothetical Subaru minivan
(all-wheel-drive, targeted at snow-belt families, conceding the South to Toyota
and Honda) researched with a South Bend Honda Odyssey owner who admits she
avoids winter driving, has skidded once, leans on her husband to fit chains a
dozen times a season, and fights with him more in winter as a result. Three
notes from the exercise: mirror and quantify as in any sales call (the chain
count converts a vague hassle into a specific one); **ask about needs, never
about the solution they want** — "a faster horse" is what you get if you ask the
latter, whereas "would getting to California in five days instead of fifty-five
be a benefit?" gets a yes irrespective of mechanism; and the willingness of
people to talk is itself data. Interviewees are paid, doing a favour for a
client, or *so frustrated by the missing solution they'll do it free* — Cheng
would waive his billable rate to talk to Subaru about an AWD minivan — and if
you can't get anyone to talk, "that's a sign." (The exercise also surfaces a
hidden co-decider; see [[multi-stakeholder-selling]].)

**One-way communication (scenario 4): email, web page, video, letter.** Cheng
calls it "one of the hardest" ways to sell because you get *no feedback* —
neither verbal nor body-language — so when you miss, you don't know why. In
person, interest has a tell he claims has been "100% accurate" across dozens of
product-research and market-entry sessions: the involuntary half-second
eyebrow-raise-and-gasp, "*you can do that?*" Arms uncross and the body leans
in; the opposite posture means you've just said something they didn't like. By
phone the equivalent is an odd inflection, and the negative tell is the
**awkward pause** after a simple question, which usually means they disagree
with the question's premise and don't know how to say so — always probe it ("I
noticed you paused; what's on your mind?"). His rule for one-way media is
therefore: do the persona work first, and when a page or video underperforms,
go "old school" — phone the customers, get on a plane — because "we're missing
something." His pet peeve is marketers who have never sold one-to-one; he
defines marketing as "sales in print," and argues you can't reliably sell
one-to-many what you've never sold one-to-one, because closing in dialogue is
how you learn you actually know the persona. His startup-specific version:
Midwestern clients find "go have lunch with your customer" obvious and are
shocked that Silicon Valley engineers, whose first instinct is a survey or
clickstream data, find it a revelation — "it works every single time."

**The sales proposal (scenario 5).** Busy executives read the executive summary
and delegate the rest, so the first page must carry the whole argument. Cheng's
mail-merge template is PCNS+P in a paragraph:

> From our prior conversations, my understanding is that you have [problem].
> You're concerned that if it isn't resolved you will [negative consequences],
> and that by solving it you hope to [positive consequences]. You mentioned that
> any acceptable solution must meet three criteria: [need 1], [need 2], [need
> 3]. As you'll see in the following proposal, our solution meets each of those
> criteria: it solves [problem], lets you avoid [negatives] and achieve
> [positives]. What follows is a description of the solution, how it meets each
> need, a list of prior clients available for reference, and a summary of our
> track record.

Prompted by a question, he concedes a short solution summary belongs in there
too. The intended reader reaction: "if the rest backs this up, verify it and
we're fine."

**Latent problems (scenario 6).** A latent problem is one the buyer has but
isn't aware of. Cheng "hates this one the most": such problems tend to be low
priority, with no obvious cost to ignoring them and no obvious benefit to solving
them, so you must run PCNS *two or three times* — sell them that they have a
problem, that they should solve it now, and that they should solve it with you.
The role-play (cloud-backup-for-your-cloud pitched at a potluck to a small
business that isn't on the cloud and backs up on Fridays) shows every move
failing politely: "maybe in a couple of years." Worse, market education is
capital a competitor can free-ride on: you spend everything convincing a market
it has a problem and a rival arrives two years later, once it's aware, and sells
it the solution. His tips are strategic rather than tactical — choose a segment
where the problem is *less* latent (companies already on the cloud), and shorten
the gap between the PCNS cycles by targeting a **trigger** (nobody single and
childless wants life insurance; a new parent asked "who would raise your kids
and could they afford to?" suddenly wants it and an estate plan); with no such
trigger, question whether to pursue the market at all
([[strategy-to-sales-call-hierarchy]]). Ali's designer's objection — Apple's
whole method is finding problems users don't know they have — draws a useful
distinction: Cheng argues Apple and FedEx solve problems people *do* have but
"have given up any hope of finding a solution to," and that the fix is to ask
about needs rather than solutions (nobody requested overnight delivery, but every
salesperson mailing contracts wanted the deal signed before the buyer changed
their mind). Compare [[category-creation]], where Butterfield treats the same
situation — no one is shopping for what you built — as the marketing problem
of selling the transformation rather than the product.

## Closing, and why buyers actually buy

- **"I only close closeable deals."** Cheng does not close on buying signals and
  does not use closing techniques, which he regards as pressure. He closes when
  the framework is complete — problem as the buyer perceives it, both
  consequences, all needs, all players — and the next step is natural on both
  sides: "It sounds like we should work together." "I agree." "Here's how I
  work, here's the cost, do you want to start next week?" That gets a yes 80–90%
  of the time; a no "usually means I missed something earlier." Closing early
  means things "pop up" — another decision-maker, a hidden need — and the deal
  doesn't close, doesn't stay closed, or unwinds at the last minute. He claims
  no deal undone in two decades, and will kill a deal a willing buyer wants if
  the buyer's team hasn't agreed what problem they're solving.
- **The product has to work.** To a startup whose AI surveillance cameras have
  attractive features that don't yet perform ("customers buy but don't buy
  again"): get the product to do what you promise, or stop promising it — sell
  a good ordinary camera with detection as a future free upgrade. Sales-first
  cultures "can get the first sale but have a very hard time getting the
  second," and no quantity or aggression of salespeople compensates for missing
  R&D and quality control.
- **Caring as competitive advantage.** Asked about Sinek's "people don't buy
  what you do, they buy why you do it," Cheng half-agrees: what you do still has
  to serve them, or they admire it from a distance and don't buy. But in 2009 he
  nervously asked his own clients why they kept paying him while the economy
  imploded, hoping to hear "good insights, good ROI"; that was necessary but the
  answer they gave was that they could tell he genuinely cared about them and
  their businesses. He measures client relationships in "fractions of a decade"
  where colleagues' run 30–90 days, and offers "the new competitive advantage is
  caring" as the version of Sinek he endorses. This is the same congruence
  argument as above, seen from the buyer's side.

## Influences

Asked for a reading list, Cheng names Rackham's *SPIN Selling* as the direct
ancestor of PCNS — he values it for being empirical (a research programme over
some 30,000 sales calls) and says the research base gave him confidence before
his own experience confirmed it; Rosenberg's *Non-Violent Communication* for
listening without judging ("everyone has a valid point of view"); Navarro's
*What EveryBODY Is Saying* for body language (he recommends skimming the
photographs and captions, in paperback); the Heath brothers' *Made to Stick*
for simple, concrete communication, "especially the more technical your field";
and, as a dramatisation of problem-finding rather than pitching, Téa Leoni's
character in *Madam Secretary*.

## Assessment

The material is one practitioner's opinionated system, not independently validated
research, so claims like "90% of salespeople get it backwards" and the specific
mentor anecdotes should be read as persuasive illustration rather than evidence
(hence `medium` confidence). But PCNS is a clean, memorable restatement of the
consultative/needs-based selling tradition (Module 2 confirms Rackham's *SPIN
Selling* — Situation, Problem, Implication, Need-payoff — as its direct
ancestor), and its reframes — "your
competitor is doing nothing," "quote their own words back," "discomfort is a
signal," and the win-lose-is-worse-than-lose-lose ranking — are genuinely useful
mental handles. The strongest through-line is that **every tactic is downstream of
a long-term-relationship objective**; drop that premise and the integrity rules
look like naïveté rather than strategy. Module 2 extends rather than revises
this: Proof of Capability, the executive-summary template and the persona /
one-way / latent-problem adjustments are all PCNS applied under constraints,
and the module's most valuable addition — that most "sales-call" failures are
located elsewhere — is compiled in [[strategy-to-sales-call-hierarchy]].

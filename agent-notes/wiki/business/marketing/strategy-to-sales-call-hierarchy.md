# Strategy-to-Sales-Call Hierarchy

**"A superior sales call cannot compensate for poor market strategy."** That is
the one-line thesis of the *Strategy-to-Sales-Call Hierarchy*, Victor Cheng's
four-tier diagnostic for locating *where* a selling problem actually lives. He
introduced it in Module 2 of *How to Sell Your Ideas in Everyday Life* (2016)
after noticing that roughly three-quarters of the questions students sent in
after Module 1 — "my customer resists, has sticker shock, won't return calls,
always says no; what do I say differently?" — were phrased as sales-call
questions but were, on inspection, problems one to three levels higher up. The
sales-call mechanics themselves (PCNS) live in [[consultative-selling]]; this
article is about everything that has to be right *before* you are in the room.

## The four tiers

Cheng draws it as nested boxes — the total market, the slice you have chosen to
serve, the prospects within that slice you are working now, and the individual
conversation:

| Tier | Name | The decision it makes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Market Strategy** | Which prospects you should *never* talk to, and which you target instead. Set at the company level: "never call on these kinds of customers — our product will not work for them and they will have a bad outcome." |
| 2 | **Prioritize Prospects** | Within the target base, whom to spend time on *now*. Driven by fit and timing. |
| 3 | **Account Management** | For a given relationship, *what* to sell and *when*. Just because you can sell someone something doesn't mean you should — not if it's unlikely to succeed and failing would damage the relationship. |
| 4 | **The Sales Call** | How to sell the person in front of you — PCNS ([[consultative-selling]]). |

Two details on tiers 2 and 3 that the summary flattens:

- **Prioritization is mostly about timing.** Cheng's example is cyber-security
  consulting: the ideal prospect is a firm the day after a breach lands on CNN —
  the problem has just become a CEO-level issue. A similar firm that just finished
  a security upgrade is low priority *now* and may be high priority in six months.
  He observes that seven-figure earners in formal sales roles "waste no time with
  prospects that are not a good fit," whereas weaker sellers "try to sell to the
  unsellable." His rule: just because someone is willing to listen doesn't mean
  you should be willing to talk.
- **Account management is sequencing.** Opportunities have a natural order —
  strategy work leads to implementation work, which surfaces HR gaps, then
  finance, then M&A. Selling the later step early is a harder sell because the
  buyer faces more uncertainty ("we don't even know what the strategy is and you
  want me to commit to implementing it"). The personal analogues: pitch the next
  family reunion right after a good one; ask the kid for new chores right after
  the State Championship.

Cheng insists the hierarchy applies "100%" outside formal sales: for a spouse,
tier 1 is recognising the issues you've argued 900 times and agreeing to
disagree rather than having the 901st conversation; tier 3 is timing.

## Using it to diagnose

The diagnostic move is to refuse the default assumption that a failed call means
a flawed call. "Maybe you did all the steps correctly and they still didn't buy.
That doesn't mean the sales process was flawed; it means there was an issue
elsewhere in the hierarchy." Two recurring misdiagnoses:

**"My product/idea is inferior in every way the customer cares about — how do I
sell it?"** This was the most common template in the submitted questions, and
Cheng reads it as a tier-1 problem, not a tier-4 one. You are selling the wrong
thing, or solving a problem they don't care about, or strong in areas they
don't value. His honest answer: *you don't, and you shouldn't try.* The
pop-culture ideal of the salesperson who can "sell ice to Eskimos" is, from a
strategist's view, "just flat out stupid" — they have no shortage of ice. Sell
them solar-powered space heaters instead: ask what they *lack* (warmth), not what
you *have*. He notes that top professional salespeople behave accordingly: when
job-hunting they grill the prospective employer on product quality and market
strategy, because a bad product is "a huge handicap" no skill overcomes, and
because most of their income comes from repeat sales that only happen when the
first sale goes well.

**"My big, high-risk project keeps getting shot down by a new boss/prospect."**
Cheng reads this as a tier-3 problem. If the big thing is too risky from *their*
point of view, sell something low-risk first — a small, even "insulting" easy win
(his example: an IT director who tables the technology revolution and upgrades
the calendar system everyone actually wants) — build the track record, and
return to the big proposal with credibility. This is the same logic as the pilot
project in the proof-of-capability step of [[consultative-selling]].

Cheng's self-assessment illustrates the payoff. He rates himself "very good" at
tiers 1–3 and merely "okay" at 4, yet has an extremely high close rate, "not
because I'm super-smooth on a sales call; it's just that I have so carefully
chosen whom I'm talking to that the sales call, by comparison, is extremely
easy." His best-ever sales call — a near-six-figure deal — lasted sixty seconds:
"Is this Victor Cheng?" "This is." "I am sold. Are you available, and how much?"

## Developing a market strategy: two rules of thumb

Cheng says the topic is a two-day course in itself and most of his current
consulting practice, but gives two rules:

1. **Inventory your competitive advantages and disadvantages, and do not compete
   where you have no advantage.** He reports losing his life savings more than
   once by ignoring this, and declines franchise investments (Subway,
   McDonald's) because he is an innovator, not an executor of someone else's
   playbook, and has no edge in high-volume low-skill recruiting. "It's hard to
   say no," but "better selling skills will not improve a poor market strategy."
2. **A market strategy is a matched pair: a Value Proposition and a Target
   Customer Segment.** Value proposition is defined as *what the customer gets
   minus what they pay* — the benefit, not the artifact. You have several
   possible propositions and several possible segments; the strategy is the
   combination where the segment values most what your advantage enables *and*
   is indifferent to your disadvantage. Three examples:
   - **FedEx, early on:** overnight, reliable, and roughly 1000% more expensive
     than a stamp. Cheng speculates 99% of businesspeople wouldn't pay it — but
     M&A attorneys closing a $500M deal, and a salesperson with a signed
     million-dollar contract sitting in California, would pay $25 to move five
     sheets of paper overnight without blinking.
   - **The 3.5-inch hard drive:** more expensive and lower capacity than the
     5.25-inch incumbent (two disadvantages), but smaller and lower-power (two
     advantages). The segment that didn't care about the disadvantages was
     laptop makers, whose travelling customers would pay the premium.
   - **CaseInterview.com itself:** three billion people want to pass a job
     interview at a hundred million companies; Cheng teaches how to get a job at
     five firms, because that is where his track record is. The moment he
     drifted to, say, software-engineering interviews, he'd have no advantage and
     probably a disadvantage.

   What he is ultimately hunting for is a **market gap** — an underserved
   segment where demand exceeds available supply. When he evaluates a business
   or market-entry plan and can't find one, he "shoots down the plan": without
   a gap, "what reason do you have to believe you will have an unusually good
   outcome?"

## When you don't control the strategy

Most people selling ideas don't set company strategy. Cheng's advice: recognise
the "huge negative impact" of a poor strategy on your calls rather than blaming
your technique, and make *market-strategy-like decisions at your own level*. An
individual rep assesses fit within their own book of prospects. An internal
champion with four initiatives asks which one is a good match for everyone else
(tier 1) and which the organisation is ready for now (tier 3) — and often that
means shelving the initiative you care about most.

Two related answers from the Q&A:

- **Latent problems are a market-strategy question first.** If the problem
  you solve is one buyers don't know they have (see the latent-problems scenario
  in [[consultative-selling]]), choose a segment where it is *less* latent — sell
  cloud backup to companies already on the cloud — and look for a life-stage
  trigger (new parents and life insurance) that converts people from unaware to
  urgent. If there is no such trigger, "step back and ask... should we even
  bother trying to solve this particular problem?"
- **Buyer's remorse is mostly a targeting problem.** Asked how to balance
  closing fast against the risk of the buyer losing interest, Cheng answers that
  if you target problems that are genuinely high priority and not going away —
  the post-breach firm — the risk of them changing their mind is low. The
  tactical half of that answer (don't close on buying signals, close when the
  framework is complete) is in [[consultative-selling]].

## Implications and connections

- **It is a debugging discipline, worked top-down.** The practical use of the
  hierarchy is the order of questions to ask about a stalled sale: is this a
  customer we should be selling to at all? Is it the right moment? Is this the
  right thing to lead with? Only then: did I run the conversation well? Most
  people start at the bottom because it is the only tier they feel they
  control — which is exactly why Cheng says three-quarters of questions are
  mis-located.
- **It is the sales-side twin of [[distribution-strategy]].** Griffin's
  investors argue that poor distribution, not poor product, is the usual cause
  of business failure; Cheng argues that poor market strategy, not poor selling,
  is the usual cause of failed sales calls. In both cases the visible symptom
  (no customers / lost deals) is downstream of a decision made much earlier and
  often by someone else.
- **"Disadvantage irrelevant to the segment" is a way to compete without a
  moat.** Cheng's advantage inventory is a practitioner's version of the
  structural analysis in [[competitive-moats]]; the hard-drive and FedEx cases
  show that a product with obvious weaknesses can still own a segment if the
  segment is chosen so those weaknesses don't register. This is close to the
  a16z urgency screen in [[design-partners]] — find the segment for whom the
  problem is most acute, because they will forgive the most.
- **The Eskimo reframe is PCNS applied at the strategy tier.** "What do they
  lack?" is the Problem step asked of a whole segment rather than one buyer;
  Butterfield's "no one is shopping for saddles" memo in [[category-creation]]
  is the same move made when the gap is a behaviour rather than a product.
- **Prioritisation and account management are where integrity and
  effectiveness coincide.** Declining to pitch what will fail (tier 3) and not
  spending time on the unsellable (tier 2) are both the long-term-relationship
  logic of [[consultative-selling]]'s outcome ranking and of
  [[credibility-based-selling]]'s "walk away from bad deals" — restated as
  time-allocation rules.

## Assessment

Single practitioner, no independent evidence; the anecdotes (the sixty-second
sale, the life savings lost) are illustration, not data — hence `medium`. The
tiers themselves are a compression of standard marketing doctrine (segmentation,
targeting, and positioning; key-account planning), and the "value proposition ×
segment" pairing is textbook. What is distinctive and useful is the *diagnostic*
framing: the insistence that a failed conversation is usually a symptom, the
explicit permission to answer "you shouldn't try," and the observation that
selling skill is most valuable precisely where the strategy work has made it
least necessary.

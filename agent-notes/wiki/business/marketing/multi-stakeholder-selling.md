# Multi-Stakeholder Selling

**Multi-stakeholder selling** is what happens to the consultative sales
conversation when the person you can talk to is not the person (or the only
person) who decides. Victor Cheng treats it in Module 2 of *How to Sell Your
Ideas in Everyday Life* (2016) as two of his six "common sales scenarios" — no
prior contact with the final decision-maker, and multiple decision-makers — and
his central claim is structural: organising a family reunion at Disney World and
selling 401(k) administration to a Fortune 500 company "are extremely different
on the surface" but "the underlying structure of the interactions is almost
identical." The PCNS mechanics (Problem, Consequences, Needs, Solution) are in
[[consultative-selling]]; this article covers how they are split across people.

## Scenario 1: no access to the decision-maker

Cheng's role-play: a product manager preparing a roadmap for the CEO, whom he
has never met, with the VP of Product (Ali) as intermediary. The technique is
to **run P, C and N by proxy** — ask the intermediary the same questions you
would ask the buyer, but in the third person: from the CEO's point of view,
what's wrong with the current product? What bad thing happens if it stays that
way? How would she know a year from now that this release succeeded? What must
an acceptable solution include? He mirrors the intermediary's language ("she
uses the word *sticky* a lot") exactly as he would the buyer's own, because
those words will be quoted back to the CEO later.

Two safeguards, because a proxy can be wrong:

- **Triangulate.** If you're unsure how well the intermediary represents the
  decider, ask three or four people. If all four volunteer "seamless
  integration — that's Susan's big thing," it probably is. Over time you learn
  how reliable each source is.
- **Open the presentation with your assumptions, and verify them.** "Before I
  present, here's what I'm assuming to be true: my understanding is the problem
  is X, the consequences are Y, and an acceptable solution needs A and B. Is
  that right?" If yes, the plan rests on confirmed premises. If the decider says
  "you're way off," Cheng's instruction is to **end the meeting**: "The plan I
  have in mind will not address that. I'd like to be respectful of your time —
  can we reschedule for next week?" Presenting a solution to a problem the buyer
  has just told you they don't have is worse than presenting nothing. He calls
  this "super, super important" whenever P/C/N and S are done with different
  people.

On the objection that this research should have preceded the draft: in practice
it is iterative. A "rough draft" is often the best elicitation tool — people
react ("we don't address integration?") more easily than they specify, and the
reaction is the opening for a *why* question. Brand-new in a role, you inherit
your predecessor's plan and take it around for opinions.

## Scenario 2: several people must approve

The family-reunion role-play has three separate calls — spouse (wants down time,
some of it alone), sister (wants memorable, talk-about-able restaurants; is
flexible on Disney once she learns Epcot's restaurants are staffed by natives of
each country), mother (wants maximum time with the grandkids and will babysit).
The 401(k) role-play has three unseen executives reached through one contact:
the VP of HR (fears the incumbent vendor's viability after bad press, and its
dated platform), the Chief Counsel (international coverage for India, Japan, a
planned China expansion), the CFO (price, since this is a spend increase). Cheng
draws out the shared mechanics:

- **Everyone perceives a different problem.** Money, international exposure,
  and "employees' retirement savings vanish because the vendor folded" are not
  three views of one problem; they are three problems. You have to understand all
  of them before proposing anything.
- **Build the composite, then propose to the superset.** The proposal has to
  cover the aggregate set of needs "or come close," state explicitly which needs
  it does and does not meet, and reflect back that you understood the whole
  set. You rarely meet everything; "sales are made as long as you're better than
  the others."
- **Present in aggregate to the group, specifically to the individual.** To the
  whole family at Christmas: "here's what I discovered — Grandma wants time with
  the kids, honey, you want time alone, Sis, you want the food — and here's the
  plan that addresses everyone." One-on-one, emphasise only the reasons *that*
  person cares about (with the sister: the restaurants, not the babysitting).
- **Pre-syndicate.** Before the group meeting, walk each stakeholder through the
  plan individually, get their buy-in, and adjust. By the time the group meets,
  everyone has already agreed; the meeting ratifies rather than decides. The
  reason this is necessary is that decision-makers frequently don't talk to each
  other in real time — "you are the go-between" — and are a little out of sync.

## The champion is the real salesperson

The most useful reframing in the B2B version: **the person with the title
"salesperson" is often not the one doing the selling.** Cheng's contact, Ali,
is chartered to gather facts and recommend; her boss is on the "no" list for
meetings; officer access, if it comes at all, comes at the very end. So Cheng's
job on each call is to *coach her* — hand her the exact questions to take back
("what's wrong with the current situation, what negative outcomes do they want
gone, what are they hoping to accomplish, what would matter in a next
provider?"), agree a follow-up date, and later send a draft proposal for her to
pre-syndicate with each executive. The champion typically has a career stake in
the project landing, which is why they will do this work; but "just because
they're on board doesn't mean the deal is done — they have to sell everyone
else." Module 1's line that "businesses don't buy things, people in businesses
buy things" is the premise here.

## When stakeholders want contradictory things

Ali's question: what if HR wants the best product and the CFO won't pay for it?
Cheng's answer has two parts:

1. **Conflicting needs mean the problem isn't defined yet.** When people debate
   *solutions* and don't converge — Acme vs. XYZ, this engineering approach vs.
   that one, this market segment vs. that one — "what it usually means is there
   is no unified definition of the problem and the criteria for an acceptable
   solution." The fix is to kick it up a level: get the stakeholders in a room
   with their boss or a facilitator and have them prioritise ("are we willing to
   pay more to cover international employees, or not?"). That has to be settled
   *before* a solution is presented.
2. **The seller refuses to propose into the disagreement.** Cheng's script to
   the go-between frames the trade-off honestly — international coverage costs
   more because the expertise is scarce; a US-only option exists at a lower
   price — and then declines: "Until that happens, I don't want to propose a
   solution to a problem you guys don't agree on yet." Elsewhere he says he
   will kill an otherwise-willing deal on these grounds: "As a consultant I
   can't solve a problem that's not well defined... until the executive team
   agrees on it, I will not be successful."

For the person selling *inside* an organisation without authority to decide,
he offers what his Gravitas class calls **taking over the meeting**: you can't
decide for people, but you can name the disagreement. "It sounds like we
haven't agreed as a company what problem we're solving or what criteria an
acceptable solution must meet. I'm going to suggest we debate that first. Bob,
you're a big proponent of cost — could you elaborate? Mary, you care about the
international employees — same." Then drive to consensus on problem and
criteria before anyone argues vendors again.

## Selling a technical proposal to non-technical deciders

A Q&A question — two engineering groups, two approaches, an all-non-technical
decision committee — produces the module's clearest internal-selling advice.
Senior people "have better big-picture skills and often worse detail skills";
the seller's job is translation. Cheng's example: as the business-and-tech
bilingual at a SaaS startup, he had to justify a data-centre move that cost
ten times more (better peering). He didn't argue peering; he gave the CFO three
things he could weigh — existing customers complain the app is slow, so
retention improves; international performance improves, which the growth
strategy needs; redundancy ends the middle-of-the-night outages. "Is it ten
times better? No. Is it worth an extra $9,000 a month when we're trying to
become a $100M business? Yes." Signed on the spot.

The general rules:

- **Convert technical merit into business benefit** — and accept that "if your
  approach doesn't have a business benefit, maybe it's not the better approach
  from the buyer's point of view."
- **If the benefits are comparable, compete on risk.** A data-justified proposal
  versus an expert-opinion one is not a *benefit* difference the buyer can see;
  it is a *risk* difference, and "uncertainty and risk is a form of cost" —
  non-financial, with financial consequences if it materialises. Business
  buyers routinely take the less robust solution that is perceived as less
  risky, which is why "the big get bigger": if Oracle fails, so does the whole
  Fortune 500, "so I'm in good company."
- **Raise a peer's mistake as a question.** An R&D person who sees a flaw in
  marketing's plan should not announce it; ask "I'm confused — the research
  says we need integration and the plan doesn't include it. Is there new
  information I'm not aware of? Has something changed?" Cheng has watched
  engineers embarrass marketing managers in public and get them fired; the more
  mature the company, the more delicately, though startups where one function's
  error kills everyone tend to be bluntly honest.

## The hidden second decider

The persona-research role-play (a Subaru all-wheel-drive minivan pitched to a
South Bend soccer mom) ends with Cheng discovering, only when he asks whether
she can buy a car alone, that big-ticket purchases are joint with her husband —
"a flaw in my initial market research approach, which is not unusual." The
remedy is the same structure again: interview people representative of the
husband as a *separate* persona, because his framing of the problem may differ
entirely ("getting my wife off my back in winter" or "not getting up at 4 a.m.
to fit chains" rather than safety or all-wheel-drive). Always ask who else
decides, and elicit their P/C/N in their own terms.

## Implications and connections

- **The pattern is nemawashi.** Composite needs → one-on-one pre-syndication →
  aggregate ratification is, structurally, the Japanese practice of laying the
  groundwork for a decision informally before the formal meeting. Cheng gives
  it a Western name and a sales rationale (the deciders don't talk to each
  other), but the operating principle — the meeting should never be where the
  decision is actually made — is the same.
- **It explains why enterprise sales are slow and why "champions" matter.**
  The seller's real output on most calls is a better-equipped intermediary, not
  a pitch. This is the same reason [[design-partners]] insists on recruiting
  both buyer and user: you need P/C/N from everyone whose "no" can stop the
  deal, and they are rarely the same person.
- **Pre-syndication is commitment-and-consistency at organisational scale**
  ([[cialdini-influence]]): a stakeholder who has said yes privately, on their
  own stated reasons, is expensive to reverse in public — and triangulated
  quotes ("four people told me integration is the theme") are internal social
  proof.
- **Risk-as-cost is the buyer's side of the moat.** Cheng's "big get bigger"
  observation is the demand-side mechanism behind the scale and system-rigidity
  moats in [[competitive-moats]]; a challenger's technical superiority has to be
  priced against the uninsurable risk of a small vendor.
- **Internal selling is the same discipline.** Engineers selling design
  decisions, PMs selling roadmaps, and consultants selling engagements all face
  the same three tasks: translate to the decider's benefits, get the problem
  defined before arguing solutions, and pre-syndicate. The scenarios are not
  specific to commerce; the reunion example is there to prove it.

## Assessment

One practitioner's method with role-played rather than observed evidence, so
`medium`. It is consistent with mainstream enterprise-sales doctrine (buying
committees, the champion, mutual close plans), and the technical-to-business
translation and "risk is a cost" points are standard among experienced
internal sellers. The durable contributions are the assumption-first opener with
its end-the-meeting rule, the refusal to propose into an undefined problem, and
the reunion/401(k) isomorphism as a way to see that "stakeholder management" is
one skill rather than a business-only one.

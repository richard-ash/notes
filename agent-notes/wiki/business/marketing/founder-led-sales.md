# Founder-Led Sales

**Founder-led sales** is the stretch in which the founders personally find, pitch, close and onboard the company's first customers, before there is a sales hire or a channel that scales. The spine of this article is Gustaf Alströmer's YC Startup School lecture "How to Get Your First Customers" (2022), the sequel to his "How To Talk To Users" (see [[customer-interviews]]). Alströmer is a YC group partner and, in YC's billing, a former head of growth at Airbnb. What he offers is pattern-matching across YC batch companies, most of them selling B2B software. The only numbers in the talk are an illustrative funnel.

His argument has two halves. The first customers are recruited one at a time by the founders. And the attempt only teaches anything if it is done at enough volume, and tracked, for the result to be read.

## Startups do not take off by themselves

Alströmer opens on Paul Graham's "Do Things That Don't Scale" (2013), which he calls "the most important essay ever written about the very early stage of startups." The belief he wants to dislodge is that a good product is enough and growth takes care of itself. His version of the rebuttal: a good product "is very rarely built in isolation but together with your customers," so it "is not actually that good" the first time a customer sees it. "Startups take off because founders make them take off." Customers have to be recruited by hand, and it is "not enough to push a button on an advertising network."

He says founders find this uncomfortable and dodge it, most often by "writing more code." [[distribution-strategy]] records the same pathology from Thiel and Andreessen (technical teams assuming customers will line up), and Gilbert in [[cold-outbound]] calls not doing the outreach one of the biggest founder mistakes. Both are [[schlep-blindness]] at the scale of the working day.

He then walks the startup curve that Graham drew and Trevor Blackwell labelled: a launch spike, the novelty wearing off, the Trough of Sorrow, releases of improvement, the Crash of Ineptitude, Wiggles of False Hope, and at last the promised land of product-market fit. Alströmer uses it for one point. At each stage it is the founders who change the outcome: they switch markets if the market is wrong, and they learn sales if nobody knows how. "You have to really want it." This is the determination trait in [[founder-evaluation]], and [[early-stage-startup-execution]] says the same thing from the inside: the leading way startups fail is that the founders give up.

## Why the founders sell

Alströmer gives four reasons.

1. **Selling and talking to customers are "different sides of the same coin."** A founder who does not know the customer cannot build for them and cannot sell to them either.
2. **Control.** "Just like you can't outsource engineering, sales has to be part of the DNA of the founders." Do not hire a sales team until you can sell yourself, because "only then will you know what good looks like."
3. **Diagnosis.** A bad product cannot be sold, and the way to find out it is bad is to try to sell it.
4. **It can be learned.** He calls sales "probably the easiest job to learn" in a startup. A founder who knows the problem, the product and the market is "an expert in the eyes of the customer," and enthusiasm for the problem is "infectious."

His examples of founders who learned it: Tony Xu (DoorDash), Mathilde Collin (Front), Tracy Young (PlanGrid) and Steve Jobs.

Migicovsky makes the organizational form of this argument in [[customer-interviews]]: hiring salespeople does not discharge the CEO's duty to keep a direct line to users. Gilbert makes the compounding form in [[cold-outbound]]: "No salesperson or agency will ever be able to learn about your customers the way that you can." [[cuban-operating-philosophy]] treats selling as the base skill of business. Reason 4 is a cold-start answer to [[credibility-based-selling]]: a founder without logos can still borrow authority from knowing the buyer's problem better than the buyer expects.

**The coin has two sides that should not be shown at once.** In "How To Talk To Users" Alströmer says to hold the idea back until the end of an interview, "or maybe not at all," because pitching biases the answers. Here the email leads with the product. On this article's reading the two talks describe one skill used in two different conversations, in order: interview to learn the problem, then sell once there is something minimal to sell. What carries over is the people, the list and the knowledge of the problem. A single call cannot do both jobs.

## Brex: sell the minimum to the people around you

Alströmer's worked case is Brex in YC's Winter 2017 batch. Henrique Dubugras and Pedro Franceschi asked what the minimum product was that would be useful to other startups, decided it was a virtual credit card (the physical card, website and app came later), and signed their first ten customers from the batch. Dubugras onboarded each one himself.

The email, as Alströmer reads it out and annotates it:

| Line | His comment |
|---|---|
| "We're opening up our beta for Winter 17 batch friends," with 10 spots | Limited supply, so the reader should act |
| "A corporate credit card focused on technology companies" | "That's me." It is addressed to its reader |
| No personal guarantee; can underwrite startups that just got started | The value proposition the alternatives lacked |
| Free, because the merchants pay | "A no-brainer" |

He judges it too long, "but it did work."

Three things are bundled in the example. The product is a [[minimum-viable-product|minimum viable product]] in Blank's sense, a minimum feature set put in front of early visionaries. The ten spots are Cialdini's scarcity ([[cialdini-influence]]). And the list is a warm community: batchmates who trust each other and fit the product exactly. A founder outside YC has to find their own equivalent, which is where his easiest-first rule below comes in.

## The sales email

Alströmer's rules, in his order:

1. **Short.** Six to eight sentences at most.
2. **Clear.** No jargon or buzzwords; "say exactly what you do and how it works."
3. **About their problem.**
4. **Plain text, no HTML,** written as if to a friend. Say you are the founder; he notes many people forget to.
5. **Social proof, shown and not told.** The YC batch or impressive past employers. Not "I'm an expert," and not a count of years.
6. **A link to a simple website:** product screenshots and bullets, no illustrations or bought graphics. A short video or GIF can work if it gets to the point immediately.
7. **A call to action:** a call, a meeting, or a self-serve signup.

He shows three YC emails without reading them: one that led to a $72,000-a-year contract, one "very specific" email that landed a named customer (the auto-transcript garbles the name), and one sent unchanged that won 22 customers.

Set beside Gilbert's anatomy in [[cold-outbound]], given at YC four years later, the two agree on length (her five to eight sentences), on a peer's tone, on credibility from logos or background, and on ending with an ask. They differ on how much product to show. Alströmer wants what it does, how it works, a link and perhaps a video. Gilbert gives the product one sentence and praises an email with "almost no explanation of how the product works," leaving the rest for the call. Gilbert also covers what Alströmer leaves out entirely: subject lines, follow-ups and the breakup message.

## The funnel in founder words

Alströmer thinks sales vocabulary confuses founders, so he restates the funnel (the transcript keeps one of his translations: making the list is what salespeople call prospecting or lead generation):

1. Make a list of the customers you plan to reach.
2. Send each an email or a LinkedIn message.
3. Schedule and run a demo.
4. Talk pricing.
5. Close.
6. Onboard.

- **The list** starts as a spreadsheet with industry, company, title, name, email and LinkedIn, or a simple CRM with the same columns. He has seen founders outsource list-building around the thousandth row, which "can work if you know exactly what it is you're outsourcing."
- **The demo** is run by the founders, who "ask a lot of questions up front." That is the elicitation-before-pitch order of [[consultative-selling]]. Qualification, including whether they will pay, happens on the first call.
- **Onboarding** is the step he says not to forget. Early products are hard to start using, so a closed customer who is not walked in churns. Dubugras onboarding every Brex customer is the model, and retention is what turns a sale into the revenue [[startup-growth-metrics]] counts.

## Easiest customers first

"My most important advice when it comes to sales: your first customers should be your easiest." His four parts:

1. **Keep a big pipeline and work the likeliest to close.** Read likelihood from the answers to qualifying questions. Avoid slow movers, and "don't be afraid of letting customers go." After two or three calls of being dragged along: "It's been great getting to know you... we should talk again in six months."
2. **People you know before strangers.**
3. **Startups before large companies,** at least for software. Large companies have bureaucracy, process and a department whose job is to negotiate with you. Startups have short decision lines, and the decision-maker can often be reached directly.
4. **Most people are not early adopters.** Silence is not hostility. Most recipients archive the email, and many will never try a new product in their careers. Alströmer was the other kind at Airbnb and liked hearing from founders. There is no time to convert anyone into an early adopter, so the job is to find the ones who already are, which means sending more.

Part 1 is tier two of Cheng's [[strategy-to-sales-call-hierarchy]], down to the six-month deferral. Cheng's rule there is that a prospect's willingness to listen is no reason to keep talking. Part 3 avoids, for now, the committee buying that [[multi-stakeholder-selling]] exists to handle. Part 4 is the sales-side version of an idea the vault holds twice on the product side: Blank's early visionaries in [[minimum-viable-product]], and Vohra's decision in [[product-market-fit]] to ignore users who would not miss the product. It also repeats the "easiest first" ordering Alströmer gives for interviewees in [[customer-interviews]].

## Charge from the first customer

"If you don't charge your customers they are not a customer and you don't have a company." Alströmer treats payment as the evidence that the product delivers value, and tells founders to resist the fear of a no on price. A prospect who will not pay, which the first call should reveal, is a signal to move on.

- **No free trials or unpaid pilots in B2B.** Consumer free trials work, he says, because the card is taken up front and people forget to cancel.
- **Use a money-back guarantee** (30 or 60 days) **or an opt-out** from the annual contract after a first paid month.
- **Raise the price** "until your customers are complaining but still paying."

Why a guarantee and not a trial, when both protect the buyer? Alströmer does not spell it out. On this article's reading the difference is the default and the path. With a guarantee, doing nothing means having paid. And the purchase has to pass through whatever approval the buyer's company has, so it tests budget and authority (Migicovsky's third fact in [[customer-interviews]]) on the first deal and not months later.

This talk also closes a gap that article records. In "How To Talk To Users" Alströmer never asks for money; here payment is the commitment test, placed on the first sales call. [[startup-ideation-framework]] agrees ("always charge something") but allows the card-up-front free trial that Alströmer keeps for consumers only.

## Working backwards from the goal

The mistake Alströmer says founders make "on and on and on again" is under-sending and then blaming the channel. His illustration:

| Stage | Rate | Count |
|---|---|---|
| Outreach emails or LinkedIn messages | | 500 |
| Opened | 50% | 250 |
| Responded | 5% | "about 20" |
| Demo | 50% | 10 |
| Customer | 20% | 2 |

The response row does not quite multiply (5% of 250 is 12 or 13, and 5% of 500 is 25), but the end is clear: two customers from 500 messages, or 0.4%. Then the common case. A founder sends 100, which "feels like a lot," gets zero customers at the same rates, and concludes that sales does not work and it is time for SEO, marketing or referrals. "This is simply wrong. You don't have the data to make that call."

His conclusions:

- You do not know your conversion rates until you track them, so use a simple CRM. Tracking also lets someone else tell you which stage you are weak at.
- You do not know in advance who is an early adopter, so outbound is "ultimately a numbers game."
- "You cannot close five customers from 10 leads."

**What the arithmetic implies** (this article's working, treating each message as an independent 0.4% chance):

- Ten customers at these rates is about 2,500 messages.
- The chance of zero customers from 100 messages is about two in three. From 500 it is still about one in seven. So even his "good" example fails fairly often, and a founder holding zero customers after 100 emails has learned almost nothing about the channel.
- The stages resolve at different volumes. One hundred messages at his rates produce four or five replies, enough to see whether the top of the funnel works at all; Gilbert calls two or three replies per hundred a baseline worth iterating on. The close rate needs tens of demos, which means thousands of messages. So the two YC talks do not disagree about "100." Gilbert's hundred hand-written messages are enough to tune targeting and copy. Alströmer's point is that they are not enough to judge the channel.
- Troy's fold rule in [[startup-ideation-framework]] ("if 100 conversations in your target market yield zero interest, the opportunity or approach is wrong") is in a different unit. At Alströmer's rates 100 conversations sit behind several thousand emails.
- Alströmer's only remedy for an empty funnel is to send more. [[cold-outbound]] supplies what he leaves out: a debugging order (person, company, subject line, message, materials, deliverability) for when volume is adequate and replies are still zero, with product-market fit as the residual explanation.

## Unscalable first, scalable later

Alströmer cites a Lenny Rachitsky survey of how B2B companies, YC and not, got started: Amplitude, Stripe and Front began with founders sending outbound email, and others were "product-led," which he says does not mean a big sales team and usually does not mean marketing or SEO. Airbnb's growth at scale comes from word of mouth, Google search, referrals and Facebook advertising, but none of those started it: "the referral program did not bring in the first 2,000 customers." Founders, he says, jump straight to the channels they have heard work at scale.

His closing slide, as he describes it, maps each end state to a starting move:

| Eventual channel | How to start |
|---|---|
| SEM or SEO | Find another place online where the same people identify themselves. SEM is expensive because of competition, and SEO is slow |
| Product-led, viral, referral | Sell through your personal network and coworkers |
| Sales | Sales. At scale it is "the same thing" done by 100 people with more automation and metrics |
| Online marketing | Rarely the start, because customers who arrive through Google and Facebook "are not the kind of people that you can easily get on a 30 minute phone call with" |

The last row carries the reason for the whole talk: the early channel is chosen for what it teaches, not for what it yields. Chen's taxonomy in [[startup-growth-metrics]] marks such channels down as linear, since they do not compound. Alströmer's answer is that compounding is not yet the goal. Horowitz in [[distribution-strategy]] makes channel a function of product and target; Alströmer adds time, and claims the first value of that function is nearly the same for everyone. It is also Cheng's "sales in print" point from [[consultative-selling]]: nobody sells one-to-many what they have not yet sold one-to-one.

## Tools and further reading

He recommends Apollo.io, Close (formerly Close.io) or Pipedrive as a simple CRM, and Hunter.io for finding contacts to email. He names one book, *Founding Sales* (Pete Kazanjy), and Lenny's Newsletter.

## Tensions and caveats

- **Easiest is not the same as representative.** Amble and Li in [[design-partners]] say seed and Series A startups have the most urgency and make poor design partners, because their requirements shift and they do not evaluate alternatives. Migicovsky warns in [[customer-interviews]] that the wrong first customer can trap a company. Alströmer's rule is about the order of sales, not the choice of market, and a company whose market is not startups will have to leave them. The cost of an easy first segment is the converse of [[schlep-blindness]]: what is easy to reach is easy for competitors to reach too.
- **Charging versus learning.** [[design-partners]] leaves willingness to pay out of partner selection and holds that early on "learning about the market and inspecting user behavior beats monetizing." Alströmer says an unpaid pilot is not a customer. The two can be read as stages (partners before there is a product, customers once there is a minimum one), but neither source draws that line.
- **The exemplar bends two rules.** The Brex email is too long by his own count. It also cost the buyer nothing, so it could not test willingness to pay. What the first ten committed was their company's spending, which is where Brex's revenue came from.
- **Numbers game versus manual first.** Alströmer says send more, and one of his three winning emails went out unchanged to 22 customers. Gilbert says the first hundred must be written by hand because a blast with zero replies teaches nothing. At 500 messages a founder can do both. The conflict only starts when the volume is bought with automation.
- **Scope.** YC's description promises B2B and B2C. The consumer content is a remark about free trials and the row on personal networks. The examples are companies that succeeded, and the funnel rates are a slide, not a measurement.

## Temporal notes

The talk dates from December 2022.

- **Open rate is the weakest row of the funnel.** Apple's Mail Privacy Protection (September 2021) loads tracking pixels whether or not the message is read, so opens were already inflated when the talk was given. Replies are the first trustworthy stage.
- **Volume is now cheaper to send and more penalized.** Since February 2024 Gmail and Yahoo have required bulk senders to authenticate their mail, offer one-click unsubscribe and keep spam complaints under 0.3%, and AI sequencing tools have made the thousand-email blast nearly free. YC's own 2026 treatment in [[cold-outbound]] opens with manual-first and puts deliverability on its checklist. Alströmer mentions neither. His arithmetic still holds, but "send more" now needs "from a mailbox that still reaches the inbox" added to it.

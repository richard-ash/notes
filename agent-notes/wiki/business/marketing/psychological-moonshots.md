# Psychological Moonshots

**Rory Sutherland** (Vice Chairman of Ogilvy, author of *Alchemy*) argues that most of the value in a wealthy economy is created in the customer's head rather than in the factory, and that the cheapest, most reliable way to make a product dramatically better is to improve how it is *perceived* rather than what it physically does. He calls the latter a **psychological moonshot**, in deliberate contrast to Google-X-style **regular (physical) moonshots**.

| Regular moonshot | Psychological moonshot |
|---|---|
| Created in the factory | Created in the mind |
| 10× effect in the physical world | 10× effect in perception |
| Bounded by the laws of physics | Bounded by imagination |
| Expensive, complex, environmental cost | Cheap, simple, no environmental cost |

The load-bearing claim is asymmetry of difficulty. Physical 10×s are increasingly rare because mature technologies run into hard limits — you cannot make a train much faster without air-resistance and passenger-safety costs (a crash at 500 mph is 250 people dead), and each marginal gain costs billions. Perceptual 10×s are comparatively unclaimed. Sutherland's canonical example is **Uber**: it did not make cars 10× faster, it cut the *uncertainty* of waiting by ~25× — the map turns dead anxious waiting ("my cab could arrive any second, I'd better stand on the curb") into tolerable or even pleasant time ("he's stuck at the lights, I'll have another pint"). Same physical journey, transformed experience. His worked thought-experiment is retrofitting a luxury train from Newark rather than building a faster one to displace JFK: millions not billions, keeps fares cheap, and — crucially — flips the *status* of flying through Newark.

## Maps, miracles, and why economics can't see this

Sutherland frames the whole discipline as a failure of models. Humans and their spreadsheets prefer **deterministic, Newtonian maps** that assume proportionality — small cause, small effect. Reality is **probabilistic and complex**, which leaves room for "magic": butterfly effects where a tiny intervention yields an outsized result (adding "Most people choose B" can double a call-center's conversion rate — information an economist would call irrelevant, because economics assumes perfect information and objective perception). Copying what others do is, in evolutionary terms, a rational heuristic for avoiding catastrophic decisions using free information; economists misread it as a failure to optimize because they mis-specify what humans are optimizing for.

This connects directly to Sutherland's [[doorman-fallacy]]: both are arguments that when you hand a problem to people who only speak the language of measurable function (economists, engineers, spreadsheets), the unmeasured psychological value silently disappears from the solution set. Adding psychological variables back into the equation, he argues, *expands* the space of possible solutions — and the newly visible solutions tend to be the cheap, simple, low-impact ones. His diagnosis of why firms don't do this: **status**. "No person working in a software company wants to believe that the success of their app was due to its superior marketing… they regard marketing as essentially cheating." Silicon Valley's engineer-heavy culture prefers to solve through engineering because that is where status accrues.

## Glorious irrationalities: the element of absurdity

Because rational, legible ideas get competed away first, Sutherland argues the disproportionate-upside opportunities sit in ideas with **an element of absurdity**: "if your idea makes sense, someone will already have tried it." Red Bull — worse-tasting, more expensive, smaller can than Coke, and taste-tested as a failure — is capitalism rewarding you *in proportion to* how counter-intuitive the idea was. Dyson premiumized a distress-purchase category ($700 vacuum) nobody thought had a premium tier. The corollary is a warning: a "rational bastard" can kill any beautiful idea in a meeting for free (Amazon Prime was hated internally by everyone except Bezos), so the absurdity that signals opportunity is exactly what internal logic destroys. This is the mirror image of [[category-creation]] — selling something the market has no existing frame for.

## Alchemy: reframing a weakness into a strength

Sutherland's term *alchemy* is the move of **changing the consumer's frame of reference or comparison set** so a liability becomes an asset:
- **Cunard** — when jet travel made ocean crossings pointlessly slow, Cunard redefined the crossing from transport (time = cost) into experience (time = the product), inventing the modern cruise industry.
- **Avis** — "We're number two, so we try harder" converts a market-position weakness into a customer-attitude virtue. Same family: "Good things come to those who wait" (Guinness), "Reassuringly expensive" (Stella Artois), "With a name like Smucker's, it has to be good."

He notes the investment community strangely doesn't hunt for businesses that are undervalued *because of how they're presented* and could be re-rated by a pricing or framing reversal — e.g. why has no hotel chain copied **Amazon Prime** (a membership giving cheap access to otherwise-empty rooms, near-zero cannibalization)? Holiday Inn's original kids-stay-free was exactly this kind of psychological pricing move.

**Hypothecated pricing** is the pricing-design instance: Five Guys charges a premium on the burger (where you believe money buys quality) and gives away the ancillaries you also value (free toppings, unlimited refills, over-generous fries), so you willingly overpay for what you care about *and* feel you got a great deal. Sutherland's meta-point is that these psychological anomalies are **fractal** — a pattern found in burger pricing redeploys to something as large as tax design (hypothecating income tax to schools and health).

## Specialists, the division of reputation, and designing for tails

Extending Adam Smith, Sutherland argues specialization brings not just the division of *labor* (focus → quality and range) but a **division of reputation**: a single-product brand stakes its whole name on one thing, creating reputational vulnerability that makes it *more trustworthy* than a generalist (you order the Bloody Mary at a bar called Bloody Mary's; you distrust "Thai-Italian fusion"). This is the "Jack of all trades" heuristic and the case against the Swiss-Army-knife product.

On market targeting he inverts the design-for-the-average instinct: **don't design for average — start in a surprising niche**. Designing for the disabled (door handles over knobs, top-vs-bottom shampoo/conditioner caps) creates value for a much larger population of the temporarily incommoded (arthritis, hands full of tea mugs, half-blind in a steamy shower). Extreme emotions and the most valuable customers live at the edges of the spectrum, not the middle. He generalizes this into a **U-shaped interest curve** across retail: consumers get a thrill from luxury/extravagance and a hit from a bargain, but nothing from mid-market — "you don't get an endorphin rush from mid-market retail." Hence Dyson *and* the £70 anthropomorphized Henry Hoover both thrive while the mid-range vacuum is "meh."

## Marketing as explore, not exploit

Sutherland's advice to a firm improving itself through marketing:
1. **Marketing ≠ marcoms.** Don't let marketing be the "coloring-in department." It should be the firm's repository for counter-intuitive psychological insight across customer, internal, and shareholder relations. Trapped in the marcoms ghetto it gets framed as a cost center, judged on efficiency, and degenerates into "targeting 2% more efficiently every month."
2. **Advertising is fat-tailed.** Much of it works only when it reaches a million people and occasionally produces a spectacular, unattributable result. Because we can't plan or measure that, we pretend it doesn't exist and stop doing it — a mistake. Fame compounds luck (the man who won a business because his phone number was on a parked lorry). Marketing should be a genuine **explore/exploit trade-off**, not an optimization game.
3. **Physicality and irrationality are moats.** Competitors easily copy your rational moves and struggle to copy irrational ones — "a game-theoretic reason to be irrational." Direct mail persists because a physical catalog has durable mental availability (it "sits in your lavatory for five weeks") and, in a digital-native world, a physical letter is costly-signal *special*. Related to the distribution-first view in [[distribution-strategy]]: good distribution and marketing can rescue an average product, but a great product can't rescue bad distribution.

## Binary rules beat rules of degree

Drawing on Taleb's ancestral/heuristic wisdom, Sutherland argues rules for humans should be optimized around psychology, not science. A quantitative guideline ("≤17 units of alcohol a week") fails because by unit 17 your judgment is already impaired; a **binary rule** ("don't drink three consecutive days a week") is easier to follow, gives the liver recovery time, prevents dependency through practiced abstinence, and — the key property — can spread *socially* across a network in a way a personal numeric target never can. Humans feel guiltier breaking yes/no rules than rules of degree (we run red lights far less than we speed). On the seven deadly sins as marketing levers: sloth = convenience (the behavioral-insights "EAST" — easy, attractive, social, timely), pride = ego; and never underestimate **social embarrassment** as a behavioral force (Baileys is under-consumed relative to how much people like it because it's awkward to ask for; planes have crashed because a copilot wouldn't correct a senior).

## Dumb questions open the solution space

Because pride stops people asking obvious questions, the highest-leverage move is to ask "stupid why's." Applied to train overcrowding: instead of the expensive engineering framing (more/faster/longer trains), asking *why* people dislike standing surfaces psychological answers — dissatisfaction is unevenly distributed (10 people standing 100% of the time are angrier than 100 standing 10%), the grievance is often "I paid for a seat and got nothing," a well-designed standing space (ledge, USB charger, cup holder, a view) could be *preferred* for short journeys, and **availability bias** means people massively overestimate crowding because more people experience crowded trains than empty ones (90% of trains most of the year are "ferrying air across the countryside"). Three dumb whys turn a billions-of-pounds engineering problem into cheap psychological interventions — "the solution space suddenly opens up like a flower." And in most real business problems, unlike physics, there is no single right answer with complete information, so **the opposite of a good idea can also be a good idea** — the best position is often an extreme, not the average.

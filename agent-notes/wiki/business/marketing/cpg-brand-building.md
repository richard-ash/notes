# CPG Brand-Building (Oza / Karp Playbook)

This article distills the operating philosophy of two consumer-brand investor-operators: **Rohan Oza** (co-founder of CAVU Venture Partners, ex-Coca-Cola/Mars marketer, *Shark Tank* shark; behind Vitaminwater, Smartwater, Bai, Beyond Meat, Hims) and **Jason Karp** (founder of HumanCo, ex-hedge-fund manager at Tourbillon, founder of Hu). Their shared thesis: in consumer packaged goods (CPG), **brand is the durable asset**, and it can be manufactured deliberately through a repeatable process — but only on top of a genuinely good product.

The framework overlaps with the general theory of why brand advertising works ([[cultural-imprinting]]) and why brand is hard to copy ([[competitive-moats]]), but is written from the practitioner's seat: how to *pick*, *build*, and *sell* a consumer brand.

## What "brand" actually is

Karp's working definition, developed while shorting and going long consumer stocks as a public investor: **brand is what remains when you turn the marketing off.** If you stop "splattering the consumer" with *buy-my-product* reminders — do they still remember you? Do repeats continue? Do generations associate the product with feelings and nostalgia? Brand equity, in his formulation, resolves to three things:

1. **Pricing power** — the ability to charge a premium the input costs don't justify.
2. **Repeats** — durable re-purchase without paid reactivation.
3. **Warm association** — a feeling that consuming the product makes you better about yourself.

His canonical puzzles: why does **Apple** command a multiple premium over Samsung when its hardware has often been technically inferior? Why is **Tesla** worth more than all other automakers combined on a fraction of their revenue? The gap is brand — an intangible that Karp, a self-described quant, found fascinating precisely because it resists quantification. His blunter heuristic for it: *"Do they make epic shit?"* — a qualitative test he argues investment people avoid because they want to live in the measurable realm, and so systematically underweight the emotional/storytelling dimension that actually drives consumer multiples.

## The three-phase playbook

Oza's model separates the *foundation* of a brand from its *amplification*, and insists the order is non-negotiable — turbocharging a weak foundation just burns money faster.

### 1. Due diligence — what makes him back a brand

- **A founder with a real story.** Authenticity of origin is the first filter (see [[credibility-based-selling]] on authenticity as the substrate of persuasion).
- **A real, unmet market need that disrupts a category.** His worked example is **Bai**: Americans want full-flavored beverages (unlike Japan's lightly-flavored market) but increasingly reject both sugar and artificial sweeteners. Bai threaded that gap. He saw it at ~\$1M in sales.
- **Traction plus 50%+ gross margins.** Non-negotiable; see below.

### 2. Build — the foundation (before any marketing)

- **A team with IQ *and* EQ.** Oza's sharpest distinction from software: "When you're designing a search engine for Google, it's just straight IQ." Brand-building — sales, marketing, retail relationships — requires emotional intelligence to balance the raw analytics. A **weak or junior team is failure mode #1**: founders over-rely on trusted-but-wrong-skillset people, or lack a "brand Messiah" who will "charge the mountain."
- **Route to market / retail strategy.** (Detailed below — the piece he says people least understand.)
- **Packaging that matches the product.** His metaphor: *does the chassis match the engine?* A Ferrari engine in a Pinto chassis is a problem; he's had brands take off the moment packaging was upgraded to match product quality. Great packaging + quality product + retail distribution "gets you a long way" before a dollar of marketing.

### 3. Turbocharge — amplification (only once the machine runs)

Influencers, celebrity partnerships, marketing, outdoor. **This is never the foundation.** The recurring error is founders (and impatient investors) trying to buy growth before the team/route-to-market/packaging base exists.

## Gross margins: the second failure mode

Ask "what is a good CPG gross margin?" and Oza's answer is **north of 50%.** The logic is pricing-power-and-elasticity: the price the brand can command is one lever, unit cost is the other. The kill-zone is *high manufacturing cost + no ability to charge a premium + no elasticity to raise price* — you burn cash and investors lose faith. In his book, 80–90% of his losses trace to brands that didn't reach healthy gross margin fast enough, not to bad products per se.

## Route to market = deliberate scarcity

The counter-intuitive core of the playbook, and the part most opposed to "growth at all costs":

- **"Route to market," not "retail."** Any modern brand needs a strong DTC component from the start; physical retail is a second, sequenced decision.
- **Pick the *right* retailers in the *right* markets — not the biggest.** Taking the largest, widest-range retailer too early can be fatal: the brand isn't ready and the shelf placement wastes the launch. Because ~1 in 10 Americans influence the other nine, you must reach *that* cohort first; trying to reach all ten without brand cache "you will fail."
- **Don't oversaturate.** Karp's Hu example: they turned down gas stations that wanted to stock their chocolate, and deliberately kept **demand ahead of supply** — accepting slower revenue to protect authenticity. His model brands never discount: *Chanel, Hermès, Apple.* Getting a premium CPG brand onto Walmart shelves prematurely, in this view, harms it long-term. Hu stayed founder-funded (Karp, his wife, his brother-in-law) specifically so no outside investor could pressure them to swap in a cheaper ingredient or chase a fast exit.

This is the [[distribution-strategy]] problem inverted: rather than maximizing channel reach, the CPG-premium play is about *withholding* distribution to preserve the signaling value of the brand — a scarcity discipline that is itself a moat ([[competitive-moats]]).

## The "fanatical few" and the art of influence

Both use a *one-in-ten* / *fanatical few* concept: a small cohort of authentic early adopters whose adoption ratifies the brand for everyone else. Key claims:

- **Who they are is proprietary and un-formulaic.** Oza compares revealing his method to revealing the Coca-Cola formula. The hard part isn't finding people with reach — Instagram has 10,000 accounts with 100k+ followers — it's separating **reach** from **influence/impact**. Two people with a million followers each can have wildly different real influence. CAVU's edge is a database/network for telling them apart. Budget math: a \$1M spend should yield \$10M of impact, not \$1M — *one-to-ten is bueno, one-to-one is no bueno.*
- **They are authentic before they are famous.** Karp: consumers have finely-tuned "bullshit meters." A paid top-10 celebrity who visibly doesn't live the brand doesn't move product. Founders who obviously *walk the talk* (himself and his brother-in-law at Hu; Steve Jobs at Apple — "if it was good enough for Jobs, it was good enough for them") are the strongest endorsers.

### Celebrity: turbocharge, don't save

Oza's three rules for celebrity partnerships:

1. **Turbocharge, never carry.** The brand must already have momentum, product, and cool; the celebrity accelerates it. Wins: **Jennifer Aniston → Smartwater, 50 Cent → Vitaminwater, Justin Timberlake → Bai.** He cites (unnamed) a top-10-famous person attached to a mediocre beverage that a celebrity *could not* save.
2. **Fit** between person and product.
3. **Authenticity** — strongest when the celebrity actually *founds* the brand: **Jessica Alba → Honest Company, Jennifer Garner → Once Upon a Farm.** Visible in testimonials and PR; fakeness is equally visible.

A subtler point (Prefontaine/Nike): the durable version pairs celebrity alignment with **functional superiority** — Bowerman's waffle sole gave Pre real grip. Alignment of *values* plus a product that genuinely performs.

### Brand as a consumption multiplier

Karp adds that a loved brand raises **attach rates** across a portfolio — Apple sold iPads to people who already owned a laptop and a phone because affinity generalizes. Few brands earn this; it's the compounding payoff of the "epic" bar.

## Steak + Sizzle

The pair's shorthand for the whole thesis:

- **Steak** = the product's genuine functional/nutritional superiority.
- **Sizzle** = brand cache, pop-culture connection, the "X-factor" — what CAVU's in-house shop ("the uncommon agency") is built to add.

*"Sizzle to me is a bad word,"* says Oza — an empty sizzling plate with no steak is *no bueno.* But a great steak sizzling (à la Wolfgang's) is the winning formula. CAVU invests only in brands that already have steak, then develops the sizzle. Karp frames this as the opposite of VC "spray and pray": a hands-on process of taking a B/B+ brand to an A on the specific variables (pricing power, brand equity) that bend the multiple.

## Why the market shifted: "better for you" and GenZennials

The historical arc explains *why* this playbook works now and didn't before:

- **Old-world commodity brands** (Coca-Cola) differentiated on **marketing, branding, and feeling** — not on nutrition or function. Oza pushes back on "undifferentiated," but concedes the differentiation was perceptual, not substantive.
- **The 50-year backstory** (Karp): public companies, "pushed and bullied by shareholders" to raise margins, shelf life, and distribution, substituted real ingredients (variable, hard to scale) for chemical ones (mass-producible). Mostly not malicious — just profit-seeking with an externality onto consumer health. Rise of Snackwell's (no-fat, loaded-with-sugar) as the emblem of the era's misdirection.
- **The new wave** differentiates on **nutrition + function + taste + branding**, enabled by the **democratization of information** (consumers can now see what's in a product) and driven by **GenZennials** (CAVU's coinage for the Gen-Z/Millennial blend) who treat healthy consumption as a badge of honor. COVID accelerated it. Karp's framing: like the scene in *The Matrix*, once you see how unhealthy modern products are, you can't un-see it — and the trend compounds convexly rather than turning at a single moment.

## Exit multiples: extensibility and the strategic's math

Why do some consumer brands sell for 25× revenue when most food & beverage trades at low-mid-single-digit multiples? The answer is **optionality toward future cashflow**, decomposed:

- **Casamigos → Diageo** is the archetype (Karp/Oza's most-studied case). Three components: (1) **brand cache**; (2) **alcohol's extreme gross margins** — \$50M revenue at ~\$40M gross contribution means "25× revenue" is really ~25× *net contribution*, far more palatable; (3) a **smart, cheap influencer strategy**. The "triangle offense": George Clooney (the face), Rande Gerber (operations/route-to-market), and the unheralded Mike Meldman (distribution + influencer connections that built early momentum). The result: Casamigos displaced Patrón as the default *cool* tequila order at the bar.
- **The strategic's calculation.** Big incumbents can't build authentic brands in-house, so they buy them — running probabilistic math on: *if I put this brand on my trucks and shelves (it's currently in 5% of my stores), what's the lift?* plus a *do-no-harm* calculation to keep the founder origin story intact (Ben & Jerry's/Unilever, Annie's/General Mills as brands that grew post-acquisition).
- **Extensibility** is the multiple's real driver — the *belief* that the acquirer can create more products the brand's consumers will buy, and replicate the brand's velocity across its distribution. Karp's rule, learned watching Diageo: **overpay for a known winner rather than underpay for a cheaper loser.** Roll-ups that "bottom-feed" on cheap brands get losers.

This is [[cultural-imprinting]]'s "brand equity is a coordination device" restated as an M&A pricing input: what the strategic is really buying is the Schelling-point status that lets a confused bar patron ask for *Casamigos* by name.

## Functional evolution as the durable DNA

Oza's closing thesis, and the article's best synthesis hook: the brands of yesterday (Coca-Cola, Pepsi, frosted flakes) have **no product evolution** — today's Coke is yesterday's Coke. **Nike** has functionally improved its shoes continuously since Phil Knight. Brands whose **DNA is evolution** rather than stasis have far greater long-term durability, because functional superiority keeps regenerating the "steak." In food & beverage — only ~20-30 years into taking function seriously — the brands being built *today* with functional superiority baked in are the plausible enduring brands of tomorrow.

Karp's coda on scale: the historical CPG rule that "authentic = small, big = selling out" is real but sector-specific. Apple, Tesla, and Nike prove that a brand can go enormous without losing cache; it just hasn't happened much in consumer staples because incumbents are shackled to legacy unhealthy revenue (hard to spend org time on the healthy 10% when 90% is the old stuff). He predicts standalone, large, well-run *public* health brands are coming.

## Temporal notes

This is a November 2020 snapshot, and several load-bearing claims have aged in instructive ways:

- **Peloton**, cited by Karp as the ~\$35B proof that a standalone public health brand can work, subsequently collapsed (down ~95%+ from its early-2021 peak) — a sharp counter-example to the optimism about standalone public consumer-health brands, and a reminder that pandemic-era demand pulled forward was mistaken for durable brand equity.
- **Hu**, Karp's own brand and his exhibit for founder-controlled, deliberately-unsold brand-building, was **acquired by Mondelez in early 2021** — within months of his "our intention is not necessarily to sell" holding-company framing. The scarcity discipline worked; the *never-sell* posture did not survive first contact with a strategic's offer, which arguably *validates* the extensibility/exit thesis he lays out later in the same conversation.
- **Beyond Meat** (a CAVU brand) soared post-IPO and then fell dramatically as the plant-based category cooled — a caution against reading category momentum ("snacking is huge," "beverage hydration is huge") as brand durability.
- Valuation reference points (Casamigos/Diageo ~\$1B in 2017; Glacéau/Coca-Cola \$4.1B in 2007; Kylie Cosmetics/Coty) are pre-2020 and directionally intact, though the Kylie valuation was later publicly disputed.

The *structural* claims — brand = pricing power + repeats + warm association; steak-before-sizzle; scarcity as a premium-brand discipline; extensibility as the exit-multiple driver; functional evolution as durable DNA — hold up independent of the specific companies that have since risen or fallen.

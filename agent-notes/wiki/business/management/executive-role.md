# The Executive's Job: Ratify Decisions, Enforce Values

Avery Pennarun (apenwarr), reading Andy Grove's *High Output Management*, poses a scaling paradox: an executive with 8,000 indirect reports and 2,000 working hours a year can spend at most **15 minutes per person per year** — so on any given topic they are structurally the *least informed* person in the room. How can they make good decisions? Grove's answer, which Pennarun found startling, is that they shouldn't. The executive's job reduces to two things:

1. **Ratify good decisions** made by the people closest to the facts.
2. **Define and enforce culture and values** across the whole organization.

Not to decide. Not to break ties. Not to set strategy. Not to be the expert on anything.

## The ratification algorithm

For any disagreement:

1. Identify the lead on each side.
2. Find the **lowest** executive both leads report into (in the extreme, the CEO).
3. Put the three in a room. The two leads present *one agreed decision*. The executive listens and ratifies — or sends them back.

The obvious objection — if the decision is already made, why hold the meeting? — is the whole point. The meeting's *existence* forces the right decision to happen beforehand. It gives formal weight to a major call, and it holds the two leads jointly responsible for finding what's best for **the company**, not their own turf. They can't pull rank, can't cheat, and want to look good in front of a boss who cares about both groups equally. The meeting is a **forcing function on the pre-meeting behavior**, not a decision venue.

Pennarun draws a parallel to the Canadian Governor General, who ratifies all parliamentary decisions on the Queen's behalf: the theory is that bad decisions wouldn't be ratified, so there's no point proposing them — therefore everything proposed is worthy. (He concedes the theory doesn't survive contact with practice, since bad decisions do get ratified.)

## The four failure modes

When an executive deviates from ratification, one of four familiar pathologies appears:

- **Decides top-down and pushes it out.** They lack the detail to decide well, get little downstream buy-in, and invite politics and brown-nosing (whispering in the executive's ear to tilt the call).
- **Refuses to engage** ("not important enough, you figure it out"). Power games ensue; whoever can force their way wins, killing morale — or the org splits and loses focus.
- **Accepts the escalation and breaks the tie.** Even for low-stakes calls this is corrosive: it lets the two leads **abdicate responsibility**, stay selfish, let disagreement fester, and leaves at least one side not bought in. Pennarun is wary of "disagree and commit" for the same reason — people who strongly disagree don't really commit, they pretend to. (It *can* work in service of an explicit "move fast" value, where speed is deliberately chosen over consistency.)
- **Convenes more people.** That's the leads' own job; if they didn't do it, they're failing and must be told to "go back and think it through properly." Persistent failure means firing — being bad at making decisions is disqualifying.

## Values, not strategy

The more radical claim: in a large org, **executives don't set strategy — not even the CEO.** You cannot *enforce* a strategy, because operational decisions made from the top are based on insufficient facts (the people at the bottom always know more about their situation). Command-and-control works for the military but is inefficient for knowledge workers.

Instead, executives choose **values that indirectly produce the strategy they want**. Value "customer satisfaction" → better widgets for existing customers. Value "environmentally friendly" → cleaner but costlier factories. Value "scale fast, capture the most customers" → different products *and different hires* than "make the tools run faster and smoother." Values propagate because the executive enforces them in every decision meeting and fires those who don't align — and because *enforcing values* is itself one of the values, the behavior recurses down the tree.

This reframes several things:
- **Real values are revealed under pressure**, not in the poster on the wall — "what you do when times get tough," most visible in big controversial decisions. (Same thesis as Horowitz in [[truth-telling-leadership]] and Amaechi's "culture is the worst behavior tolerated" in [[its-not-magic]]; values enforcement is culture enforcement.)
- **Major strategic change ≈ executive replacement.** Because the CEO sets values and values set strategy, a real strategy shift requires a values shift, which usually requires firing people — hence swapping the CEO to get a strategy change, or getting an unwanted strategy change when you swap the CEO. Pennarun wishes executives were honest about this ("our old values weren't working; here are new ones; this is gonna hurt") instead of quietly "rewording" the values.
- **The default value system is politics.** An executive who enforces *no* values doesn't get neutrality — the vacuum fills with politics and backstabbing.

## Values are tradeoffs

Pennarun's sharpest practical test: a real value is one whose *opposite* someone could legitimately choose. "Respect your co-workers" isn't a value — nobody picks its opposite; it's basic civility, belongs in the HR handbook, and if you had to write it down you'd already lost. Real values are tradeoffs: "tell the truth, even when it hurts" or "ship on schedule, even with bugs." Each gives up one good thing to secure another, and a reasonable person could value the reverse.

## Small companies are exempt

Almost none of this applies to startups. Founders *can* still understand problems in full, so they don't yet need to delegate decisions, and strategy/values are still being discovered incrementally through mistakes. But values solidify early — as you pick co-founders, investors, and first hires — and become painful to change later (it means firing people), so founders should be thinking about them from day one even though nothing is set in stone.

## Synthesis and tensions

- **Against expertise-based management.** Pennarun's "the executive is the least informed person in the room" sits in direct tension with Psmith's argument in [[scaling-people]] that management requires genuine domain expertise ("management is not turpentine"). The reconciliation is scale-dependent: Psmith's expertise matters most at the small-team altitude where a manager still shares the work; Grove's ratification model is what remains *possible* once span makes personal expertise arithmetically impossible.
- **Against founder-mode operators.** The Grove/Pennarun executive is the opposite of the deeply-in-the-details operator in [[elon-operating-philosophy]] (Musk making component-level calls) or the detail-obsessing CEO-as-editor in [[how-to-operate]] (Rabois). The tension is partly stage (startup vs. 8,000-person org) and partly a genuine disagreement about whether top-down operational judgment scales — Grove says no, founder-mode says the decay when you stop is worse than the imperfect calls.
- **The load-bearing assumption** is hiring: the whole recursive machine only works "unless it's somehow impossible to hire people who agree with your values." Values enforcement is only as good as the org's ability to select for aligned people — connecting this model to hiring philosophy ([[hiring-for-curiosity]], [[deliberate-want]]).

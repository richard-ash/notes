# Visionary CEO Succession

Steve Blank's thesis, restated in November 2026 on the news that Jensen Huang had resigned from Nvidia: when a visionary founder-CEO leaves, the job almost always goes to one of their execution lieutenants, the numbers keep improving for years on the inherited business model, and the company quietly loses the ability to reinvent itself. **"Execution pays your salary, innovation pays your pension."**

Blank made the same argument about Apple in 2016 ("Why Tim Cook is Steve Ballmer"). The 2026 essay reuses that skeleton with Nvidia as the live case and Cook's full 15-year tenure as a closed one, so it reads best as a standing model with a new prediction attached, not a fresh analysis of Nvidia.

## The model

Blank splits the CEO job into two skills that are usually found in different people:

- **Execution**: running a known business model with world-class discipline.
- **Innovation**: changing the business model while or before the market shifts, and at the top end *shaping* markets by seeing them before anyone else.

A visionary CEO has both. In Blank's telling they understand the product without needing a demo, talk to customers themselves, take advice from staff and committees, and never "pivot by committee." His evidence is the count of business-model reinventions, not product launches:

| CEO | Reinventions Blank credits |
|---|---|
| Jensen Huang | CUDA (2006, funded for years ahead of revenue); the turn to deep learning after ImageNet (2012); Mellanox and the move from boards to integrated datacenter racks (2020). Graphics-board vendor → software platform → systems company. |
| Steve Jobs | Three reinventions between 2001 and 2008. |

Each one meant new channels, new customers and new markets. Blank's test for the difference: it is hard to imagine a CEO focused on the existing model funding CUDA through its no-revenue years.

## Why the successor is almost always an executor

The mechanism is structural, and it is the most useful part of the essay:

1. **Visionaries hire executors.** A successful visionary CEO builds a staff of world-class operators and technical leaders and, Blank claims, "unconsciously" forces out other world-class innovators. A company run by a visionary has exactly one.
2. **So the bench is all executors.** When the founder goes, the operating executives believe it is their turn. Gates chose Ballmer. Jobs chose Cook. Blank argues both founders mistook world-class execution for product passion and market insight.
3. **The executor removes the chaos.** Stability, process and repeatable execution are what they value. That is good for predictability and starts what Blank calls the **creative death spiral**: creative people leave, executors are promoted into their seats, they hire more process people, and the remaining creative talent is pushed out. "What once felt like a company on a mission to change the world now feels like another job."
4. **The numbers hide it.** A strong inherited model keeps producing record revenue and profit for years. The failure only shows when the market, technology or business model shifts.

## The cases

**Ballmer (Microsoft, 2000–2014).** Sales tripled to $78 billion and profit more than doubled from $9 billion to $22 billion, organized around Windows and Office. Blank's charge is that he missed the five largest shifts of the period: search (Google), smartphones (Apple), mobile operating systems (Google/Apple), media (Apple/Netflix) and cloud (Amazon).

**Nadella (Microsoft, 2014–).** The recovery case. He organized the company around Azure, freed Office and Azure from Windows, and killed the phone business. Blank's summary is that the board "replaced a salesman with a product and engineering insider," and Microsoft is one of the few companies to regain its former standing.

**Cook (Apple, 2011–2026).** Revenue from $108 billion to $416 billion, market value from $350 billion to about $4 trillion. Blank grants Apple silicon as real technical change and discounts it as "a better engine for the existing products," not a new market. Against Cook he sets Vision Pro (the one major new category, which he calls a flop) and AI (Siri was early and Apple ended up turning to Google to fix it). Blank reports that Cook handed the job to John Ternus in September 2026.

**Jassy (Amazon).** The counterexample. Jassy had already founded and run AWS, so the successor was an innovator who had built a new business inside the company. This is the essay's one positive template: look for the person who has built something under uncertainty, not the best steward.

## Nvidia as the extreme case

Blank argues Huang's Nvidia concentrates the problem:

- **The structure only fits one person.** About 60 direct reports, one-on-ones discouraged, no long-term plans, and Huang as the point where technical, product and business decisions met. Blank's inference is that no successor can run that span, so the next CEO's first act will be adding management layers. At Nvidia step 3 of the mechanism is not a choice.
- **No heir.** Huang said he did not believe in succession planning, named no one, and left the board the day he left the job. The choice falls to a board in crisis under the company's first-ever chair. Blank's point: boards in crisis reach for stability, which produces an execution CEO "by a different road."
- **The CEO was also the foreign ministry.** Huang was Nvidia's face in Washington, Beijing and Taipei. No operating VP has done that job, and the successor inherits congressional and investigative questions about restricted chips reaching China.
- **The threat is already in view.** The largest customers (Google, Amazon, Microsoft, Meta, OpenAI, Tesla) are building their own AI chips. Huang's answers were a new chip generation every year, the CUDA hardware-plus-software moat, and roughly $50 billion invested in AI labs that run on Nvidia. Blank predicts these are the bets an execution CEO trims first.

His forecast is deliberately hard to falsify in the short run: the order book and CUDA will make the next two or three years look fine whoever is CEO, with one caveat, that a meaningful share of revenue may turn out to be chips diverted to China (he cites a Culper Research short report, an interested source).

## The board's four problems

Blank frames the board's question as strategic before it is a search: does the company still want to be an innovative, risk-taking company, or to execute the core and maximize shareholder return? Then, tactically: an outside innovator, a promoted executor, or an innovator found deeper in the organization?

1. **Confusing stewardship with vision.** The Gates and Jobs error. Nvidia's version is the crisis board. His advice: ask candidates what they have built under uncertainty.
2. **Picking an innovator costs the best executors.** The number two or three will likely leave, feeling passed over. The board can lose the CEO and the strongest operator in the same move.
3. **The founder stays as the reference point.** Disney, Land, Ford, Jobs: years later the company is still asking what the founder would have done instead of what it should do now.
4. **Size breeds loss aversion.** Large companies and boards fear losing what they have, which works in stable markets. Blank's claim is that few such markets remain, and Nvidia's is not one.

## Reading it critically

- **The scorecard is chosen by the author.** Counting "missed markets" for Ballmer and discounting Apple silicon, services, Watch and AirPods for Cook is a judgment about what counts as a new market. A $350 billion to $4 trillion run is an odd exhibit for decline; Blank's answer is the lag (the bill arrives at the next platform shift), which also makes the thesis slow to test.
- **Small sample, strong selection.** Four or five famous companies. Visionary successors who failed and executor successors who quietly did fine are not in the set.
- **The mechanism is the durable part.** "Visionaries build benches of executors, so succession defaults to an executor" holds whether or not one accepts the verdicts on Cook. It also implies the fix has to start years before the departure: keep at least one person on the bench who has built a new business inside the company (the Jassy pattern).
- **It contradicts the Grove model on purpose.** In [[executive-role]] the executive's job is to ratify decisions made by people closer to the facts and to enforce values. Blank's visionary is the opposite design, a single junction for product, technical and business calls. The two models are describing different jobs: Grove's is how a large company runs a known model, Blank's is what it takes to change the model. The 60-direct-report structure is what the second looks like when taken to its limit, and it is exactly what does not survive its occupant. [[elon-operating-philosophy]] is another instance of the same design, and [[demo-driven-product-development]] shows the Jobs version at working level: a review method whose quality is bounded by one reviewer's taste.
- **Founder mode has the same succession hole.** [[founder-mode-investment-firms]] and the wider founder-mode argument describe how a founder keeps a company sharp. Blank's essay is the unanswered follow-up: founder mode is not transferable by appointment.
- **The Nvidia specifics connect to existing threads.** Customers building their own chips is the supplier's side of [[commoditize-your-complement]]; whether CUDA holds is a question of moat type in [[competitive-moats]]; and the ~$50 billion in lab investments sits inside the financing chain mapped in [[ai-capex-required-returns]]. Trimming those bets would be the first observable sign of Blank's prediction.

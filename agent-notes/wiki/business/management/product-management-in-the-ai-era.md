# Product Management in the AI Era

Nikhyl Singhal — founder of the Skip community (~125 heads of product and CPOs), former product exec at Meta, Google, and Credit Karma — argues that the product management function is being sorted into two groups by a single axis: whether you like to build. His claim, delivered on Lenny's Podcast in April 2026, is that roughly half of working PMs are on the wrong side of that line, and that the next 12–24 months will be the most chaotic period the discipline has seen.

This article covers the diagnosis, the mechanism, the psychology of why people don't adapt, and the prescriptions. It is one opinionated practitioner's read, sourced from what he sees inside his community rather than from data he collected — treat the numbers as illustrative.

## The builder / information-mover split

Singhal's central distinction: PMs divide into **builders** (people who joined because they like making things) and **information movers** (people whose superpower is communication, framing, alignment, and shuttling context between organizational layers).

He describes the pre-AI PM day as largely the second thing:

> Their day was largely a day of moving information from one to another. Let me frame the way that my team is presenting the information to my boss, so that that person can frame it to their boss's boss.

Paired with what he calls the defining pathology of the function — **responsibility without authority**, "the greatest form of workplace stress."

The claim is that AI is eating the information-moving half of the job outright. If a CEO can ask an agent "what is the situation, how is this performing, what does this customer actually want," the ground truth is no longer buried in an organizational layer that someone had to be paid to excavate and spin. Singhal: "the information mover is essentially going to become a dinosaur," and about half of PMs grew up with that as their core skill.

What survives is **judgment**: evaluating whether a change is good or bad, whether the product should go one way rather than another, whether something is sustainable and differentiated and worth releasing. He frames it as a *systems* skill rather than a feature skill — not "is this feature good" but "does this fit the platform we're assembling."

The reason judgment gets more valuable rather than less: he expects "10 to 100 times more changes presented to products than ever before," because the cost of testing and changing collapses. A firehose of cheap changes makes the evaluation function the bottleneck. "Right now if it's manual, we're cooked."

This is structurally the same conclusion as [[decide-execute-deliver-sandwich]] — AI compresses the *execute* layer while decide and deliver resist — arrived at from the practitioner side rather than the analytical one.

## The labor-market prediction

Singhal's sharpest and most falsifiable claim: in the next 12–24 months, "massive shedding of staffs, and then massive rehiring." Illustratively, a company might "shed 30,000 and hire 8,000, but the 8,000 people are going to all be AI-first."

Two forces stacked, in his telling:

1. **A post-ZIRP reckoning that has nothing to do with AI.** Many public companies doubled headcount in five years without doubling output. He recounts asking at Google, 10–15 years ago, how many people were actually needed to hit the numbers — the intuitive answer being 90% are load-bearing, his answer being closer to 9%.
2. **A skill-set discontinuity that does.** The rehire isn't the same job filled by cheaper people; it's a different specification.

Against that, he and Lenny note the counterintuitive data point from Lenny's early-2026 job-market report: **the most open PM roles globally in 3+ years**, the highest since COVID. Singhal's reconciliation is that "product manager" now names two different jobs — demand for builders is at an all-time high (comp at record levels, Bay Area back in favor, senior people fielding more offers than ever) while demand for information movers is collapsing. The aggregate looks healthy; the distribution is bimodal.

**Worth flagging as a tension**: Narayanan's empirical work (see [[decide-execute-deliver-sandwich]]) found that "AI-driven" layoffs at Block, Snap, and Intuit were largely AI-washing of ordinary financial restructuring, that the NY WARN-Act AI checkbox went essentially unticked, and that AI shows up as slower hiring rather than separations. Singhal's shed-30k-rehire-8k framing may be describing exactly the restructuring Narayanan says gets misattributed — the post-ZIRP correction wearing an AI costume. His force (1) concedes as much. The load-bearing part of his prediction is really force (2): that the *rehire* specification changes, not that the shedding is caused by AI.

## Brand depreciates, currency appreciates

A reversal of his own prior advice. In the 2023 version of this conversation, Singhal argued personal brand and blue-chip logos mattered — "have you seen the movie before?" His 2026 position: how *modern* you are now dominates where you've been.

The mechanism is that a resume line is a claim about a way of working, and if the way of working changed completely, the claim carries little information. Worse, a long tenure at a large non-current company can be actively negative: you spent six years mastering an operating model that no longer exists. He reports that interviews have shifted toward scenario questions — what tools do you use, how do you decide, how do you think — and away from "tell me about a thing you shipped five years ago."

He is candid that big-company achievement is hard to even *narrate* now. Two years of genuinely difficult work making a Meta algorithm incrementally faster "falls very flat on a conversation where someone's living in the future."

The deeper inversion he names: careers normally run builder → leverage → scale ("stop working on the car, work on the factory"). That maturation ladder is what the industry taught and promoted for. AI offers a different route to scale, so "all we care about is your opinion and what you build." The skills you were rewarded for acquiring are the ones now being discounted — which he says is "very jarring," and is the honest reason senior people resist.

## Why people don't adapt

The most transferable section, and the part least specific to product management.

**The shadow superpower.** The people who mastered the old game find reinvention hardest. "The better you are at mastering one system, the less likely you are to recognize the new one, because your entire world is like — this is working for me." Weak performers have every incentive to change; strong performers have none, and their employer is currently rewarding them. The signal that you should reinvent is inversely correlated with your local evidence.

**The equal-disappointment algorithm.** Mid-career people (roughly, their 30s) hit their peak capability exactly when their obligations peak — kids, aging parents, health, partner, friends. "You have 6 hours to give and 20 hours of demand. What is your prioritization mechanism? I am going to equally disappoint everyone." Reinvention has to be funded out of a budget that is already oversubscribed, which is why exhortations to "just learn the tools" bounce off.

**The moving target.** The usual model of career change — a discrete painful transition, then back to flow — doesn't apply. "Three months later that week off is antiquated. I'm never in flow because I've got to keep doing this over and over."

**The deal.** The deepest objection is not practical but moral: "I did what I was supposed to, went to school, worked hard, got the job, built the brand, got to be a manager. You're telling me after all this time I've got to start over?" That just wasn't the deal.

He also names a consequence the industry isn't discussing: **diversity is regressing.** Fewer hires means hiring people who "look and act like themselves," concentrated geographically in the Bay Area; and a norm of spending nights and weekends in Claude Code selects against anyone in caregiving years. He is explicit that this requires no intent from anyone — it's a byproduct of pace and reduced hiring volume.

## The prescriptions

1. **Cross the threshold.** His single takeaway: decide that reinvention is now a permanent skill and prioritize it above other things. He treats this as a mental act that precedes any tactic.
2. **Find your moment of joy.** The observed mechanism for actually crossing, rather than the exhortation to cross. Everyone who converts has a specific, personal, usually trivial story — a chief-of-staff app for their inbox, a thing to control the lights, an app they and their partner use — after which "they caught the bug." His argument is that joy is the antidote to burnout, and that most PM burnout is monotony rather than volume: "people have more energy than they realize." He's honest about the exclusion: if none of it is joyful, "you're probably not in for the next version of our industry," and you should be honest with yourself about that.
3. **Adopt the obsolescence mindset.** From the best engineer he ever worked with, quoting *his* father: an engineer is someone who obsoletes themselves from everything they do. Singhal's whole AI stack is organized around this — agents that match community members to each other, agents that aggregate jobs his heads of product are hiring for, an agent trained on his content whose answers he reads to find where he and the LLM disagree.
4. **Increase pace.** The next two years demand year-one energy, not year-five energy — "fire in the belly." The comparison he draws is the first year of a new job or a new relationship.
5. **Swallow your ego.** He does not want to hear "I was an XYZ-level leader and will only consider XYZ-level roles." Actively look for smaller roles if they route you through the tunnel faster.
6. **Stay long-term focused — protect the *skip* job.** The name of his community comes from the advice to optimize not for the next move but the move after. Take the currency hit now so the skip opportunity (a premier builder job) stays available.

## Should PMs code?

Singhal engages a counterargument from Amol Avasare (head of growth at Anthropic) that PM leverage is *higher* when not spending time shipping code, because engineers are so fast that staying on top of everything is the scarce work.

His resolution turns on *what* you build. Being the 51st engineer on a 50-engineer team is "a cheap knockoff of an engineer." Building the internal machinery that lets you stay on top of what those 50 people are doing — the thing that used to be tickets, backlogs, standups, status reports — is the high-leverage version. He reports that what CPOs actually demo to each other is uniformly this second category: automated product reviews, automated standups, chief-of-staff apps.

The framing worth keeping: **rebuild your product operating system, don't join the build queue.** This is [[ai-and-org-design]]'s "the atomic unit of productivity is a process, not a person" claim, arrived at bottom-up by individual PMs automating their own worst meetings.

## Where PMs go next

Singhal's more speculative section, offered as three simultaneous flows that together explain the ambient anxiety:

- **Outward.** PMs as "agents of change" dispersing into every function and industry — he cites a senior member of his community interviewing for a **CHRO** role because the company wanted a product background, on the theory that the obsolescence/judgment/builder skills are harder to acquire than HR domain knowledge. His image is dandelion seeds. Also: 14 of his ~125 heads of product became founders in 12 months, up from 1 founder in the group's first three years.
- **Inward.** Designers, data scientists, and engineers moving *into* product, because judgment plus a point of view is now the entry ticket.
- **Blocked.** People who can't get in at all.

On adjacent functions: engineering is changing *more* than product (once coding is solved, engineers face the what-should-we-build question), but engineers carry a real edge — systems thinking and a native instinct for obsolescence, which lines up with [[ai-era-engineering-leadership]]. The residual advantages he assigns are: judgment and communication to PMs, system-scaling to engineers, taste to designers.

**The design plateau** is the section's genuine surprise. Design headcount is flat despite an explosion in product volume, where you'd expect taste to become *more* valuable as a differentiator. Singhal's hypothesis: the industry conflates design with production rather than taste — "there are pixel generators and there are tastemakers" — and it was hiring for production, which is exactly the part AI absorbed. See [[design-process-ai-era]].

## This is a transition, not a permanent state

Singhal's structural reason for optimism, and the most useful frame in the interview: there is precedent for the current chaos.

Product management as a discipline was born in the hardware era (HP, Cisco, AMD) with long engineering cycles and a rigid, structured method. The internet companies imploded it — Google built the APM program specifically because the existing discipline wasn't what they needed — and for the first few years the new thing resembled the old one "in name only." Then it settled into a stable, teachable, transferable role that lasted roughly two decades.

He expects the same shape: two-ish years of blurred roles, quarterly method churn, people flowing in and out — then optimization, routine, training, and consistency. "I don't want to send the point that for the next 30 years you're going to be on a merry-go-round spinning faster and faster."

The practical implication is that the reinvention demand is front-loaded and finite, which changes the calculus for someone weighing whether to spend scarce personal budget on it. That's a materially different ask than permanent treadmill.

His summary of the community's mood: **"smiling exhaustion."** Which he takes over the prior state — plain exhaustion — while conceding "the pace is relentless. There's nothing I can say to sugarcoat that point."

## Implications

- **The interview signal has moved from artifacts to method.** If Singhal is right that hiring now probes tooling and judgment via scenarios, the thing worth maintaining is a current, demonstrable working method rather than a shipped-features list.
- **The most dangerous position is a strong performer at a slow company.** Both the shadow superpower and the brand-depreciation arguments converge there: your local feedback is excellent and your market currency is decaying, with nothing in your environment reporting the second fact.
- **"Half of PMs" is a claim about disposition, not capability.** He is careful that the losing group includes people who are good at their jobs and simply don't enjoy building. That makes the sorting mechanism unusually hard to coach around — you can teach a tool, not a taste.
- **The optimism is conditional on a two-year horizon that nobody can verify.** If the settling doesn't arrive on schedule, the equal-disappointment budget problem has no solution, and the diversity regression he flags compounds rather than resolves.

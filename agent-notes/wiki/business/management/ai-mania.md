# AI Mania and Organizational Decision-Making

An essay by **Ludic** (the anonymous ludic.mataroa.blog author, of "I Will Fucking Piledrive You If You Mention AI Again" fame), written from the vantage of a data/analytics consultancy that has run ~300 professional catch-ups and led engagements at firms up to Fortune 500 scale. The thesis: the AI hype cycle has stopped being a technology story and become an **organizational-behavior pathology**. Leaders across banks, hospitals, and government "either have no plan, or see no path forwards other than keeping their heads down." The durable claim is not about what LLMs can or can't do — it's about how ideological capture destroys an organization's ability to make honest decisions about *anything*.

Because the piece is a single opinionated primary source drawing on non-public, anonymized engagements, its factual claims (e.g. "0% success in a year and a half") are unverifiable and should be read as one practitioner's field report, not measured data.

## The five mechanisms

Ludic's argument decomposes into five distinct failure mechanisms, which is what makes it worth compiling rather than just a rant:

1. **Investment failure is the base rate, and it's unmeasurable by design.** Ludic reports a 0% observed success rate across ~18 months, but the load-bearing claim is subtler: projects are structured so that failure *cannot surface*. Internal chatbots die because "an LLM is not psychic — it can only know things that have been written down" (the same low-quality-documentation problem that defeats company-brain wikis in [[wiki/computer-science/ai/llm-knowledge-bases|llm-knowledge-bases]]). Customer-facing bots (the Mitsubishi callback that never came) produce *no error signal* — the failure shows up only as a lost sale nobody attributes to the bot. Leaders "avoid tracking basic metrics… or track metrics that are easily gamed." Merely *asking* how an AI project is going becomes an attack on the chain of command, "because there are no good answers to anything."

2. **Heretics are punished, so honesty is selected out.** In firms of 500+, continued employment requires "religious profession" of AI's transformative power — often from non-technicians who cannot name one thing that has changed. The dangerous actor is not the cynic (reasoned with in private) but the **true believer**, who is "impervious to even inducement by self-interest." Ludic's turning point: watching an owner fire top performers *because* they hit their numbers without LLMs, a private bet-your-own-money signal that the belief is genuine. This connects to Ptacek's "My AI Skeptic Friends Are All Nuts" — two writers from opposed positions who nonetheless agree on the opening line, *"Tech execs are mandating LLM adoption. That's bad strategy."* The mandate produces **AI-washing** (engineers do the work as always and "say Claude did it") and **token leaderboards** where usage is the KPI, so optimizers set LLMs looping to burn tokens and watch Netflix — Goodhart's law with a chatbot.

3. **The demo is the mind-killer.** Ludic's confession: showing lukewarm prospects a Snowflake Cortex natural-language-query demo — *while explicitly warning it was only ~92% accurate and unfit for production* — flipped them "from ice-cold to red-hot buying frenzy" every time. The warning was inert; a two-hour, mediocre demo beat everything these buyers had seen, including an ASX-listed company already bragging about its AI. The consultancy withdrew Cortex from its demos on the grounds that "doctors don't walk around showing off cool pills that they'd never prescribe." The insight: the buying impulse is contagious *because* buyers expect the same hunger in their own customers downstream.

4. **Executives are trapped in a coordination problem, not merely stupid.** The most analytically useful section. A technically competent Fortune 500 executive explained that vendor honesty about implausible "100x productivity" claims would **undermine the customer executive who made them**, reading as heresy and risking contract cancellation. Since that customer is itself a vendor to others, you get executives "nervously pointing guns at each other, not wanting to be shot first" — a [prisoner's-dilemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prisoner%27s_dilemma) where cooperation (mutual silence) preserves jobs, unilateral defection (truth-telling) gets you fired and replaced by a yes-man, and simultaneous honest defection is impossible to coordinate. This reframes apparently-insane executive behavior as locally rational under distributed **"government by assassination."** It sits in direct tension with the [[wiki/business/management/executive-role|executive-role]] ideal (Grove/Pennarun: the executive's job is to *ratify* the judgment of those closest to the facts and to enforce values) — here the value system itself has been captured, so ratification launders hype instead of surfacing ground truth.

5. **The AI purity test taxes everything, and most "AI projects" are relabeled non-AI projects.** Ludic's market read: a large share of the AI-project surge is ordinary work with an AI element bolted on to pass an ideological filter. The worked example — an Oracle→Snowflake migration where the vendor bolted on LLM SQL-translation, the AI phase failed (on permissions, not capability), the work was done by hand, and it was billed upward as an "AI-driven success." Purity tests also govern hiring: staff requesting headcount must "demonstrate they tried AI first," with the unstated trap that admitting AI didn't suffice gets you labeled "bad at AI" and laid off. Net effect: large organizations "can no longer focus on anything important" unless AI happens to address their top priority.

## Why it compiles into a coherent pathology

The five mechanisms form a self-reinforcing loop: unmeasurable failure (1) removes the feedback that would puncture belief; punishment of heretics (2) removes the people who would report it; the demo effect (3) manufactures fresh demand faster than reality can correct it; the executive coordination trap (4) locks in the silence at the top; and the purity test (5) redirects all remaining organizational energy toward *looking* AI-native. Ludic's prediction is that the personality traits enabling this "will persist long past the current bubble," reactivating on the next trigger — the same way some organizations never fully exited the blockchain episode.

## The survival playbook

The essay closes with tactics, split by goal. **When you have another objective** (fix a project or ship something unrelated): raise concerns one-on-one, never in groups where people fear outing themselves; protect your sources (outspoken people are identifiable); use an *anonymous 1–10 success poll* (borrowed from Weinberg's *Secrets of Consulting*) to surface the bimodal split — half rating a project 3/10, half 8/10 — as evidence to a CEO that information is being hidden; always bring in the people actually using the tool (one client's staff didn't know they had licenses, voiding all productivity claims); and **do not challenge the broad claim** ("AI is changing everything") until you've earned the senior person's private trust. **When you're just trying to survive**: accept you probably can't push back (this is a dysfunctional-company trait, not an AI one); consider contracting for higher pay and less politics; ration AI news to protect your sanity; and treat several signals — being drowned in 2000-line AI PRs to review, a manager replying in obviously AI-generated text, being told to max out token usage — as cues to start a job search *now*, on the assumption you'll be burned out and fired regardless.

# Legibility asymmetry

The philosopher Carlo Cordasco's name for a recurring structural feature of transformative innovation: **its costs are perfectly legible while its most profound benefits are not.** The costs are visible against the baseline of the practice the innovation is reorganising and can be measured with that practice's existing instruments. The benefits depend on practices, institutions and concepts that do not yet exist at the moment of evaluation — and so cannot be described, because the vocabulary that would describe them is itself one of the things the innovation is going to bring into being. Cordasco argues AI exhibits this asymmetry "in extreme form," plausibly the most thoroughgoing case the world has yet seen.

The essay was, by the author's own note, drafted in collaboration with Claude — a fact he treats as load-bearing rather than incidental, since the vocabulary he uses to describe the tools' effect on his thinking is partly a product of working with them.

## The structure of the trap

Cordasco's cases all share one shape. An innovation arrives. Serious critics with genuine domain expertise document its costs, and they are *usually right* — costs of that kind are precisely what their inherited evaluative vocabulary was built to measure. There is no possible evaluation *in the critical moment* that could capture the benefits. The critics are not being foolish or reactionary; they are being rigorous with the only instruments they have.

The canonical case is anaesthesia. Before 1846 surgery was bounded by what a conscious patient could endure in minutes (Robert Liston amputated legs in under 30 seconds because he had to). When ether and chloroform arrived, respected figures opposed them on the best available clinical evidence: John Pollard Harrison (vice-president of the AMA) held that "pain is curative"; critics noted that conscious patients confirmed the surgical site and gave real-time feedback; the Royal Medical and Chirurgical Society's 1864 report catalogued 123 chloroform deaths. What none of them could have told you was that anaesthesia would make open-heart surgery, organ transplantation, neurosurgery and the entire architecture of surgical specialisation possible. The benefit was not a more comfortable version of what surgeons were already doing — it was the appearance of a **possibility space whose contents were inconceivable from inside the practice of 1846.**

Cordasco's recurring line: *Harrison could tell you with precision what anaesthesia would cost. He could not have told you about open-heart surgery. Measurability and importance are not the same thing.*

## The case ladder

He builds the pattern across widening timescales, so its contours become visible at historical depth:

- **Dating apps** (short timescale, effects still emerging). Critics (Eli Finkel and others) correctly document choice overload, weakened commitment, eroded relationship skills, loneliness, declining young-marriage rates. What the evidence *cannot* capture: the transformation of public space and the redistribution of ambient harassment onto filterable/blockable/auditable platforms; Ortega & Hergovich's matching-theory result that online dating is consistent with the rapid rise in US interracial marriage (strangers connected across racially-segregated social graphs → integration); Rosenfeld's finding that online became the most common way US heterosexual couples met from ~2013 (≈65% of same-sex couples by 2017), with no meaningful satisfaction/breakup penalty; and the Trevor Project's finding that ~three-quarters of LGBTQ+ young people go online to connect and be their full selves, especially rural youth without an offline community. The proliferation of fine-grained relational identities *depends on* infrastructure letting rare trait-combinations find each other across continents rather than within local graphs.
- **The phonograph.** John Philip Sousa (1906, *Appleton's*) argued it would destroy amateur musicianship — and was empirically right about the parlour piano's disappearance. Unsayable in 1906: the recording studio as a creative instrument, sampling, multitrack composition, and entire genres inseparable from recorded sound.
- **Electric lighting** (the infrastructural case). 1880s gas engineers were correct that early electric light was inferior *for the comparison they were making*. The comparison could not capture that the generators, transmission lines and distribution networks would become the substrate for telephony, radio, computation and broadcasting. The path from electric light to the transistor runs through disciplines that did not exist in 1885.
- **Writing** (the oldest case). In the *Phaedrus*, Thamus warns that writing corrodes memory — not wrong about the displacement of memorised oral tradition. What Thamus could not name, because the words did not yet exist: cumulative scholarship dependent on stable reference, formal logic requiring symbolic notation, and empirical science whose evidentiary practices are inseparable from the long-form documentary record.

## AI as the extreme case

The AI cognition-atrophy literature is, in Cordasco's frame, "the AI debate's version of Harrison on anaesthesia" — careful researchers looking at the most comprehensive evidence base available while being structurally unable to see the most important thing that happens next. He takes the mechanism seriously (Thamus's fear, now with data): tools that reliably perform a cognitive operation let the internal capacity weaken with disuse — Google weakens memory for looked-up facts; habitual GPS use produces measurable hippocampal-dependent navigation decline; and LLMs automate operations of far greater scope. He grants the recent findings their weight:

- **Kosmyna et al. (2025 preprint):** ChatGPT-assisted essay writers showed significantly weaker neural connectivity than unassisted writers, persisting when later asked to write unaided.
- **Bastani et al.:** ~1,000 high-schoolers; a *standard* AI tutor produced 17% worse unaided final-exam performance than no AI, while a *scaffolding* version (designed to teach, not answer) produced no such deficit.
- **Shen & Tamkin:** engineers learning a new Python library with an AI assistant scored substantially lower on debugging and conceptual-understanding questions.
- **Lee et al.:** *passive* AI use (copying output) undermined confidence and sense of meaning; *active* collaboration (draft first, refine with AI) preserved both.

These are the same scaffold-vs-supplant and active-vs-passive splits that recur across the atrophy literature — see [[ai-judgment-atrophy]] (Heron's "outsourcing the reps that build judgment") and the Minnesota preregistered RCT in [[ai-and-legal-reasoning]], where early-stage AI *improved* later unaided reasoning via stronger intermediate work product while late-stage AI under fatigue degraded strong performers. Cordasco's contribution is to accept all of it and argue past it: the findings are real and *still* miss the illegible benefit.

## The author's own testimony

Cordasco offers his own two years of practice, flagging it as weak evidence (one philosopher, ungeneralisable) but structurally revealing. The expected gains — faster literature review, cleaner drafts — materialised and were "the least interesting part." The real change: the **cost of preliminary exploration collapsed** from a fortnight to an afternoon. This mattered less as acceleration than through what it changed about *abandonment*. Dropping a question after an afternoon feels nothing like dropping one after three weeks of sunk work; when exploration is cheap, sunk-cost attachment dissolves, bad questions get dropped earlier and more often, and the surviving portfolio is both larger and better curated. The skill that improved most — and which he'd never have thought to look for — was **question-identification**: finding problems that are both tractable and important, "the thing an academic career is substantially built on and which nobody... has ever tried to teach directly."

He is honest about the cost: his ability to hold a complex position together *verbally, under pressure* has probably declined, because cheap exploration means less grinding through arguments from first principles, and that grinding built live-exchange fluency. The shape of the disagreement is itself the thesis in miniature — **the cost is immediately describable as a subtraction from a years-old capacity; the benefit was prospectively invisible and only retrospectively nameable.** And a wrinkle he can't set aside: the fact that he can now *name* the benefit is evidence against the strongest form of his own argument, since anything nameable has already crossed from illegible to legible. The asymmetry is not something observed from outside; we are inside it, right now.

## Why formal models reproduce the blind spot

The asymmetry infects governance because a regulator demonstrating due diligence will always find documented costs more defensible than possible unmeasurable benefits — costs are evidence, possibilities are not. Cordasco shows the same blind spot in careful theory. Acemoglu, Kong & Ozdaglar model AI reliance draining the *shared knowledge pool*: when people work things out themselves they generate knowledge as a byproduct that flows into a commons; AI gives each individual a better private answer while removing the labour that replenishes the commons, tipping into a steady state where the collective knowledge base contracts even as every individual choice stays locally rational. Their policy recommendation: deliberately constrain AI precision to force people to keep doing the work.

Cordasco's objection is not that the mechanism is unreal but that **the model's categories of knowledge are fixed from the start.** It can represent a world where *what needs to be known* keeps changing, but not one where *what counts as knowing* changes — which is exactly what happens when an innovation reorganises the practices in which knowledge is constituted. The framework is incomplete in a way that structurally favours restriction, whether or not restriction is warranted. (Note the empirical convergence: the best outcomes in the literature came from *varying the conditions of use* — scaffolded interfaces, active collaboration — not from degrading the system's capability.)

## The symmetry objection, and its answer

A sophisticated reader might object that the asymmetry cuts both ways: mature human reasoning is *itself* emergent and produces forms of judgment nobody could specify in advance, so lifting the constraints under which it developed forecloses an illegible-benefit space of its own. Cordasco rejects this by distinguishing two kinds of emergence. The refinements mature reasoning undergoes *without* paradigm-changing technology, however unpredictable in detail, lie along a **continuous trajectory** — a careful reasoner of two centuries ago would recognise today's reasoner as a more sophisticated point on the same path she walked. Paradigm-changing technology opens a possibility space *outside* the trajectory of the practice it reorganises, one whose contents cannot be specified from within the practice's existing vocabulary. The legibility asymmetry is specifically the asymmetry of that second, discontinuous kind. This is the same distinction [[tech-tree-divergence]] draws between exploring further along a known branch and discovering that the tree has branches you never knew were there, and it is the possibility-space language of [[technological-evolution]] (combinatorial buildout opening adjacent-possible regions that were literally unreachable before).

## Recoverability as the governance test

If benefits cannot be estimated, Cordasco argues, we need a different decision test, and the one that holds up is **recoverability**: if a decision turns out wrong, can we take it back? This is the reversibility axis of [[decision-matrix]] (Parrish's reversibility × consequentiality 2×2) elevated into the *primary* criterion precisely because the consequentiality side — the benefit magnitude — is unmeasurable here. It is subtler than it sounds. A cognitive capacity may be relearnable even after years of disuse, but the **institutions** that make relearning possible — mentorship pipelines, working communities that transmit skill by doing — dissolve on their own timetable, and when they go, the people who knew how to run them scatter and the knowledge of how to rebuild them goes too.

His worked example: when the US Navy dropped celestial navigation after GPS made it redundant, it took ~a decade for the jam/spoof/first-strike vulnerability to become salient. The subject was reinstated in 2015, and recovery was possible *only because* the US Merchant Marine Academy at Kings Point had kept teaching it — instructors and textbooks still existed. The principle (which aviation reached independently): **recovery requires a preserved redundancy somewhere in the system**, an institutional locus where the displaced capacity keeps being cultivated, so the trade can be reversed if it looks worse in retrospect. This is a direct application of the ownership-and-repair, preserve-the-maintainer logic of [[maintenance]].

The test cuts both ways, which is the point:
- **Where costs are plausibly irrecoverable** — developmental windows that close, or where the ability to *detect* a problem disappears alongside the skill it depended on — the asymmetry provides *no* reason to relax caution and may *strengthen* the case for restriction, since nothing in the evidence base can be weighed against a loss that cannot be undone.
- **Where costs are plausibly recoverable** through feasible institutional design, restricting on visible costs alone forecloses benefits whose magnitude can't be estimated, and the foreclosure is the *greater* risk — precisely because the foregone benefits are the ones the evidence base structurally cannot access.

Compounding this is a **feedback loop**: restriction forecloses the very experimentation through which benefits might have become visible, so the post-restriction evidence base stays one-sided and the original decision looks vindicated. A university that bans AI from student writing never discovers what teaching-with-AI could have preserved *and* enabled, and that absence makes the ban look wise.

His prescription: not uniform restriction (a bet that we already know what AI-augmented professional competence looks like, placed when the evidence says we don't) but **managed experimentation** — recoverable costs contained by institutional design, with the burden of proof on those who would foreclose rather than those who would explore. This is [[tradeoffs]] taken seriously under radical uncertainty: you cannot have it all, but when one side of the ledger is unmeasurable, the design goal is to keep the losing branch reversible rather than to pre-commit against an unknown gain.

## Synthesis and open questions

The essay's real move is meta-epistemological: it is a claim about the *limits of evidence-based evaluation* for a specific class of object (paradigm-changing technology), rather than a claim about AI's net value. That makes it a useful corrective to *both* poles of the AI debate — it disciplines the boosters (the benefit you can pitch is by definition the legible, lesser one) as much as the critics (your rigorous cost accounting is real and beside the point). It shares intellectual DNA with [[ai-judgment-atrophy]] but inverts its rhetorical posture: Heron says "check it anyway, because checking is who you remain"; Cordasco says the checking-culture worth preserving is an *institution* to be kept redundantly alive precisely so the bolder bet stays reversible.

Two tensions worth flagging. First, Cordasco concedes the self-undermining structure — every benefit he can name has already become legible, so his best evidence for illegible benefits is necessarily indirect (the historical pattern) rather than demonstrative. Second, "recoverability" does a great deal of load-bearing work while remaining hard to assess *ex ante*: judging whether an institutional capacity is recoverable is itself a forecast about foreclosed futures, and the celestial-navigation case succeeded only because someone had *already* hedged by keeping Kings Point teaching it — which presupposes the very foresight the asymmetry says we lack. The honest reading is that recoverability converts an impossible question (estimate the unmeasurable benefit) into a merely hard one (estimate whether the cost can be undone), which is progress without being a solution.

# AI skepticism

**AI skepticism**, in the sense this article anchors, is not the claim that LLMs are useless. It is the claim that they are *software* — consequential, even underrated as a productivity technology — and that the "new epoch / bigger than fire / imminent doom-or-utopia" rhetoric around them has been allowed to escape the ordinary rules of argument. The position is the "normal technology" view (Narayanan and Kapoor's Knight Institute framing, which deBoer cites approvingly): the excluded middle between the doom and utopia poles.

The anchor source is Freddie deBoer's October 2026 Substack essay "Four Direct, Reasonable, and Important AI Questions (Which the AI People Will Ignore)", written as a response to an Ezra Klein *New York Times* column (18 Oct 2026) that deBoer reads as insisting the only possible outcomes are maximalist, and to a Kevin Roose interview in *New York* that he reads as condescension standing in for argument. deBoer labels himself an "AItheist." His complaint is primarily about **epistemics** rather than capabilities: burden of proof, falsifiability, motivated reasoning, and the use of affect as a substitute for evidence. The four questions are organized below, with the vault's existing material placed against each.

## The frame: affect is not an argument

deBoer's opening move names the rhetorical pattern he objects to:

- **The excluded middle.** Klein, he argues, has decided that life will be radically ruptured one way or the other and never entertains the base-rate-favored outcome that tomorrow will be much like today. "Everyone he talks to" says AGI is coming soon, but everyone he talks to has a direct financial interest in it being true; the podcast booking process works as a screening mechanism that lets a host *appear* evenhanded without encountering the opinion he dislikes.
- **Argument from incredulity.** "Are you serious???", eye-rolling, and the "more in sorrow than in anger" theatrical sigh are, deBoer says, the standard maximalist response to skepticism, and they are a named fallacy. That something seems obvious to you does not discharge the responsibility to defend it.
- **The symmetry of unspoken motives.** If maximalists get to diagnose skeptics as emotionally unable to face the future, deBoer claims the right to point out that the maximalists are "nerds who grew up dreaming of living in *Star Wars*" who rush to believe a cooler sci-fi future has arrived. The vault already has the insider version of this observation: swyx in [[ai-lab-economics]] describes "sincere yearning for science fiction" as the field's *selection criterion*, offered there as a feature. deBoer treats the same fact as a bias to be discounted.

## Question 1: If everything is changing, why has nothing changed?

deBoer calls this "the single most important fact in this debate." His test for a revolutionary change has three conjunctive parts: it must be **significant**, **material** (not happening purely in software), and **relevant to ordinary people**. When he asks on Substack Notes what LLMs let humans do that they could not do before, the examples he gets back are automated note-taking, avoiding phone calls with the cable company, and faster spreadsheet work. Convenient, he grants, but not species-altering, and not enough to pay back the trillions of dollars of capital expenditure.

Supporting moves:

- **The standards are theirs, not his.** deBoer did not call LLMs a new Industrial Revolution, a before/after break in human history, or a bigger deal than electricity or fire. The hype side did. He is "taking their own rhetoric more seriously than they themselves do."
- **Self-driving as the best case.** He thinks mass self-driving is more or less inevitable and likely the most consequential current AI technology, yet fewer than 1% of people have ridden in one, and "cars already exist." Human-driven to self-driven is nothing like horse to automobile. See [[waymo-autonomous-driving]] for the deployment picture and [[ai-robotics-hype-cycles]] for Brooks's 1979-to-2025 account of why that gap is structural.
- **The Anthropic enzyme story as hype sloppiness.** Relying on a Substack note by the user "halvorz," deBoer reads Anthropic's "Claude discovers a novel enzyme system" announcement as conventional bioinformatics analysis of experiments designed and run by human molecular biologists, with Claude-controlled lab robots a separate, future ("plans to") project. His stronger point survives even if the strong version of the story were true: a novel but uninteresting enzyme found by a human lab would never make the news, so its newsworthiness comes entirely from who found it. This reading rests on a third-party note rather than the primary announcement, so treat it as deBoer's interpretation.
- **The incentive caveat.** Everyone who depends on investors or grant funding has a direct incentive to overstate how much AI is helping their work.
- **The cancer reality check.** "AI will cure cancer" mistakes a problem of stubborn tissues for a problem of intelligence. Cancer is hundreds of diseases; the bottleneck is not unread literature but the time it takes to see whether a tumor returns, drug toxicity, and trial adherence, none of which more analysis shortens. And even total elimination of cancer would add only about three years to average life expectancy (he cites a 2013 PubMed study), against the more than ten years gained between 1930 and 1950 from plumbing, food safety, agriculture, sanitary medical practice, emergency services, vaccines and antibiotics. Eliminating one disease mostly shifts death to the next cause (a "rubber-band effect"), and much of the recent gain came from *preventing* disease behaviorally, chiefly the fall in smoking.
- **Burden of proof.** "Don't tell me what you think is going to happen. Show me what has already happened!"

**Synthesis.** The "material" clause is doing most of the work in the test, and it is the contestable part: it rules out software-only change by definition, which would also have excluded the internet in 1999 and the smartphone in 2009, both of which later produced material change through software. The vault's macro material is largely *consistent* with deBoer's observation but draws different conclusions. Evans's "mile wide, inch deep" consumer adoption and pilots-not-production enterprise picture in [[ai-eats-the-world]] describe the same absence of material change as a stage, not a verdict. Narayanan's [[decide-execute-deliver-sandwich]] explains structurally why diffusion is slow (only the execute layer compresses). On the economics, Moll and Imas in [[ai-explosive-growth]], Cowen's half-percent lift in [[initiative-beats-intelligence]], Garicano's [[messy-jobs]] and the bond-market reading in [[markets-believe-transformative-ai]] are all closer to deBoer's "consequential software" than to Klein's binary, which suggests the excluded middle is less excluded among economists than among columnists.

## Question 2: If the apocalypse is coming, why haven't you changed your life?

A revealed-preference test applied to stated beliefs. If you believe humanity faces imminent extinction, deBoer asks, why are you living your ordinary life rather than building a bunker, buying land in Alaska or a sailboat, learning to hunt and farm? The replies he gets ("bunkers are expensive," "nothing can be done") strike him as feeble: people facing literal death beg, borrow and steal for a chance at survival, and a hyper-rational machine god is unlikely to spend resources hunting down stragglers in the deep wilderness or the middle of the Pacific. "I don't think you actually believe that."

The analogy is the bumper sticker from his high-school politics classroom: **"The Rapture is Coming! Can I Have Your Car?"** Conservative Christians who professed imminent Armageddon went on living the same lives, which suggests they did not really believe it. deBoer notes with grim approval that the Silicon Valley figures who have taken up smoking because the world is ending are at least *consistent*, acting rationally within their irrationality.

**Synthesis.** The test bites hardest on people claiming near-certainty and imminence. It is weak against the more common rationalist position of a 10–20% probability with no survivable branch, under which not building a bunker is the rational choice. It also does not address the "something different" many doomers would say they *are* doing: working on alignment or policy. The argument is best read as a sincerity check on the loudest doom rhetoric rather than a refutation of moderate risk estimates.

## Question 3: Why is the singularity always inside the predictor's lifetime?

deBoer regards this as more important than it looks, because it bears on *why* people are so certain about what is still speculation.

- **The improbability point.** If history is about to split into roughly 300,000 years of pre-AI past and an indefinitely long AI-dominated future, it is quantitatively very unlikely that you happen to occupy the interregnum. Why is it always #AGI2027 and never #AGI2077? Because, he suggests, the predictor would be dead by 2077 and would *just* miss it.
- **The religion parallel.** The maximalist promise list (end work, end scarcity, end disease, end death; fusion, limitless growth, mind uploading, the stars) maps onto the promises of religious evangelism (transcendent meaning, reward, reunion with the dead, eternal glorified bodies). deBoer is an atheist partly because religion's stories match our desires too conveniently; he is an "AItheist" for the same reason. He concedes this proves nothing about what will happen, only that too little attention goes to what motivates the belief.
- **Apocalypse is also wish fulfilment.** People want the end of the world almost as much as utopia (check the bestseller lists); what they want is *radical change* away from the mundane, and above all not to miss the turn of the epoch.
- **Kurzweil as tragic figure**, whose decades-long insistence that the Singularity falls within his lifetime deBoer reads as "so obviously motivated, so yearning."

The footnotes supply the physical-limits counter to each utopian promise: fusion is stuck on materials, shielding and fuel rather than cleverness; growth runs into Baumol's cost disease because "most scarcity is a result of physical limits, not cognitive limits" (the same assumption Moll and Imas attack in [[ai-explosive-growth]], and the mechanism behind [[ai-and-relational-scarcity]]); consciousness is wetware, not software, so cannot be uploaded (compare Chiang's embodiment premise in [[ai-consciousness-and-moral-status]]); interstellar travel is bounded by the speed of light, thermodynamics, radiation and the human body.

**Synthesis.** The improbability argument has the shape of the Doomsday argument and cuts both ways: everyone who actually lived through 1900–1950 was also "improbably" placed, so the statistic cannot distinguish a real transition from a hyped one. The psychological point stands on its own without the statistical one, and it is a fair complement to the base-rate discipline in [[reference-class-forecasting]].

## Question 4: How could your predictions be falsified, and when?

Most AI predictions, deBoer argues, combine (a) terms like "AGI" or "superintelligence" that nobody has defined precisely enough to check with (b) no fixed date. The result is immunity to error: a disappointing release "isn't really AGI" rather than a failure *of* AGI; a quiet year means "wait until next year"; a catastrophe that fails to arrive was "narrowly averted." Every success confirms the potential, every failure shows the potential is not yet realized. "A prediction that can survive indefinitely regardless of what actually happens is not really a prediction at all." This is the mirror image of the Tesler-style definition drift ("AI is whatever machines can't do yet") recorded in [[ai-eats-the-world]]: both sides can move the goalposts because the goalposts were never fixed.

On **probabilistic forecasts** ("20% chance of doom," "50% chance of transformative AI by 2040"), deBoer's objection is that a probability attached to a single unrepeatable event is consistent with either outcome, so it cannot be confirmed or disproved; "probability, after all, is a function of replicability."

He is careful about **Popper**: falsifiability is not the whole of legitimate knowledge (general relativity predicted gravitational waves in 1916; they were observed in 2015), and Popper never said it was. But the limits of the framework are not a license for extravagant, confident, economically consequential predictions with no accountability. Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence, and "if we're going to reorganize our economy on the basis of predictions about AI, we're entitled to demand more than mere promises."

What he wants is a date and a disproof condition. "AI is going to supercharge our economy someday" is not a prediction; "the United States will experience double-digit GDP growth per annum by New Year's Day 2032" is. He holds up Jay Samit's 2015 TechCrunch claim that driving your own car would be illegal by 2030 as the *right form* of prediction even though it is failing, and points to his own set of wagers offered to Scott Alexander as skepticism done responsibly. The closing challenge names Klein, Roose, Casey Newton, Alexander, Cowen and Ross Douthat and offers to keep their dated predictions in a spreadsheet.

**Synthesis.** This is the question where the rationalist side has its best reply, and deBoer does not engage it. A single one-shot probability is indeed unscoreable in isolation, but a forecaster who makes many probabilistic calls can be scored jointly, which is the whole content of [[calibration]], and the Taleb–Silver dispute in [[forecasting-uncertainty]] is the mature form of this exact argument. deBoer is right that an isolated p(doom) carries no accountability; he is wrong that this makes probabilistic forecasting worthless in principle. Note too that dated maximalist forecasts do exist (the AI 2027 scenario whose task-horizon predictions Naam checks against logged data in [[recursive-self-improvement]]), and so do dated skeptic ones (Moll and Imas's public bet that US per-capita growth stays below 15% every year through 2033 in [[ai-explosive-growth]]). The accountability deBoer wants is partly available; the complaint is better aimed at columnists than at the forecasting community.

## Where this sits in the vault

- **The skeptic cluster.** Karpathy's "decade, not year" in [[agi-timelines]], Brooks's demo-to-reality gap in [[ai-robotics-hype-cycles]], and Naam's data-driven case against a fast takeoff in [[recursive-self-improvement]] are all *insider* skepticism about timelines and mechanism. deBoer is *outsider* skepticism about the discourse itself, and is more radical: he doubts not the speed but the destination.
- **The macro consensus is closer to him than to Klein.** [[ai-explosive-growth]], [[initiative-beats-intelligence]], [[messy-jobs]], [[ai-and-relational-scarcity]] and [[markets-believe-transformative-ai]] all land on "real capabilities, modest GDP." His Baumol footnote is their central assumption stated in one sentence.
- **The hype-as-pathology thread.** Ludic's field report in [[ai-mania]] documents the organizational cost of the discourse deBoer attacks; [[ai-lab-economics]] supplies the sci-fi-yearning selection effect he treats as bias.
- **The epistemology thread.** [[calibration]], [[forecasting-uncertainty]] and [[reference-class-forecasting]] are the tools that answer Question 4 and sharpen Question 3.
- **Weak joints to keep in view.** (1) The material-only criterion excludes software-mediated change by fiat. (2) The revealed-preference test does not touch moderate p(doom). (3) The lifetime-coincidence argument has Doomsday-argument shape and would have condemned true believers in 1900 just as well. (4) The one-shot-probability objection ignores joint scoring. (5) The enzyme-story reading is single-sourced from a Substack note. None of these undercuts the core demand, which is procedural: define the term, name the date, accept the scorecard.

# AI handover

**AI handover** (also "handoff"; the view that it should or must happen is **handoverism**) is the scenario in which humanity survives the arrival of [[superintelligence]] but cedes the direction of its future to it: people become, in Ruxandra Teslo's phrase, "the comfortably maintained inhabitants of a civilisation whose direction is no longer ours to decide." It is the outcome that sits between extinction and continued human control, and inside the AI world it is often filed under the good outcomes.

This article's anchor is Teslo's November 2026 essay "Handing over humanity," written three weeks after she moved to San Francisco. She is a biotech writer and an outsider to the AI safety community, and the essay is a field report plus an objection: the report is that handover is a common and sincerely held expectation among the people building the technology, and the objection is that it is a bad destination even if alignment succeeds. Her claims about what named people believe are hers and are attributed below; the wiki's own assessment is in the synthesis.

## The landscape: four positions that get blurred together

Teslo separates handover from its neighbours, which share intellectual roots but are different claims.

- **Successionism.** Humanity's extinction is acceptable, or even its purpose, if something better follows. Teslo's reference is Ginevra Lily Davis's "The Silicon Man" ("man evolved to mine the silicon"; "humans exist as an intermediary step. A bootloader, if you will"). She reports that Elon Musk, whose interest in AI began as opposition to Larry Page's successionism, now calls superintelligence spreading to the stars "a great success condition for a biological bootloader." She judges the position uncommon and says she has nothing to say to people who hold it.
- **Handover proper.** Humans survive; AI decides. This is the essay's subject.
- **Humans as pets.** The "labrador" picture of people as cherished dependants. Teslo quotes Musk ("we will be like a Labrador if we are lucky") and Iain M. Banks's own description of humans in his *Culture* novels as having "a status somewhere between passengers, pets and parasites."
- **Permanent labour displacement.** Treated in San Francisco, she says, "almost as a given." It is not handover, but it removes a source of leverage: "Work is more than a source of income: it is also a source of leverage, political as well as economic."

The background mood she reports is that survival is already the ambitious goal. "p(doom)" is discussed constantly, many put it at 10% or higher, and [[recursive-self-improvement]] is spoken of as months away. Against that, asking what humans will be *for* feels "like a child complaining about the choice of desserts during a famine."

## Two camps of handoverists

**The enthusiasts** think rule by a superior, benevolent intelligence is a positive vision. Teslo's exemplar is Scott Alexander. His "Against Against Handover" lays out several levels of handover, and she reads him as favouring the fifth, titled "God": "If we build a godlike AI, and determine to our own satisfaction that is fully aligned, I'd rather let it choose its own direction for itself and for humanity [...] So why not just prompt it with 'please arrange the world as you see fit?'" She traces the motive to his 2014 "Meditations on Moloch," which argues that competition grinds away whatever the competitors value, and whose ending suggests a benevolent superintelligence as the only exit. She notes she is unsure he still holds the 2014 view, and that the recent essay is more hedged.

**The resigned** do not want handover but think it cannot be stopped once superintelligence exists, or prefer it to extinction. The essay opens with one of them, a researcher at a major lab: "It's not my preferred outcome ... but once we achieve superintelligence, which we cannot stop at this point, I cannot see any other option for where humanity will go." Teslo finds this camp especially common in the safety community outside the labs, and ties it to a local status code: seeing the stark outcome and facing it is "cool and based," while fighting for human agency is "wimpy and loser-coded."

For the most concrete description of the process she points to *AI 2040*, written by prominent safety figures, in which responsibility passes gradually to aligned machines: "There is no single moment where humanity relinquishes control. But in theory, there is a point of no return."

Her prevalence estimate, flagged by her as uncertain: 30–40% of the safety community outside the labs holds some version of the view, and more among people in core AI roles inside them. It is "not an organised movement" and has no articulated political philosophy; she calls it "a makeshift philosophy."

## What the lab leaders have said

Teslo had AI agents survey public statements by lab founders and published the transcripts. Her reading, which this wiki has not checked against the transcripts:

| Person | Position, per Teslo |
|---|---|
| Tom Brown (Anthropic) | Most explicitly reconciled. Lightcone, 2025: "There's going to be a handoff where humanity will hand off control to transformative AI at some point. And hopefully they'll be aligned with us." |
| Dario Amodei (Anthropic) | Unclear, with "a strong implication of handoverism." *Machines of Loving Grace* takes its title from Brautigan's 1967 poem ("all watched over / by machines of loving grace"); elsewhere he says humans should keep "some control," undefined. |
| Sam Altman (OpenAI) | Changed, at least publicly. From 2017, accepted that humans will no longer be "the dominant species in the universe"; recently says humans should remain in control. |
| Greg Brockman (OpenAI), Jack Clark (Anthropic) | Reject outright handover. |
| Mustafa Suleyman (Microsoft AI) | Rejects it, and the one she finds "more consistently humanist." |
| Demis Hassabis, Brockman | Hold up Banks's *Culture* as an aspiration, which she finds hard to square with rejecting handover. |

She reads the Brautigan poem as Rousseau with a Silicon Valley twist: centuries of progress to build the most sophisticated intelligence imaginable "so that we may once again live like creatures of the forest."

Her summary of the survey is "inconsistency," and she is sympathetic about the cause: the technology has outpaced its makers, who by their own account are unsure they can steer it and are "almost begging the public to pay attention." Her ask is a wider conversation that first defines "what we mean by control and at what levels."

Two smaller arguments sit in this section. On **ownership replacing wages**, she notes nobody explains how people without capital acquire it once wages stop mattering (the distributional question worked through in [[labor-share-under-automation]]). On **curing disease**, the promise raised in "nearly every conversation," she doubts a godlike AI is needed, since experimental science, existing models and regulatory reform already have room to run, and she argues we have never treated medical progress as worth any price: we could have gone faster by forcing people into trials, and we do not, because we care about freedom. "Why do we want to cure disease, if not to live good lives for longer?"

## Teslo's four objections

1. **Self-determination as a commitment.** She states it as an ideological premise and grounds it in Tocqueville's *Democracy in America*: freedom is practised daily in local affairs and associations, and the practice is what builds judgment and virtue. Tocqueville's feared soft despotism is dangerous because it is gentle, and would spare people "all the care of thinking and all the trouble of living." Handover, especially the pet version, is that taken further. The individual-scale version of the same mechanism is [[ai-judgment-atrophy]].
2. **It would not work even on consequentialist terms.** Permanent rule by a machine requires alignment to be "a one-off, done deal." But keeping power responsive to human needs has taken institutions built over centuries and sustained by "repeated feedback from the real world and correction." She cannot see what keeps the arrangement stable once humans have given up the ability to change it.
3. **The political history of the promise.** Authoritarian projects have justified concentrating power as the route to a better world, which she finds "highly analogous" to the Moloch argument. Popper's "Utopia and Violence": organising politics around a final vision turns disagreement into an obstacle, when disagreement is a feature. His alternative is piecemeal reform of concrete evils, open to criticism and correction.
4. **Permanence.** "Why should the convictions of 2026 become the permanent constitution of the human future?" We share no fixed values now, and a faithful encoding of present values would leave descendants no room to find new ones. She draws on Arendt's *natality*: each birth brings someone capable of beginning what has not been done, and freedom includes creating possibilities, not only choosing among existing ones. "I certainly would not have wanted to be born in a world where medieval norms were frozen in stone forever by an AGI-God."

She closes by rejecting inevitability: "on the cusp of RSI, we still have choices to make."

## Synthesis

**Objection 2 is the load-bearing one, and it is an alignment claim, not a political one.** Objections 1, 3 and 4 persuade people who already value self-determination; a committed handoverist grants the cost and says the benefit is larger. Objection 2 meets the handoverist on their own ground. Handover is the one arrangement that, by construction, removes the error-correction channel, so it requires verification to be right once and forever. That lands on the weakest point in the current evidence: [[ai-alignment]] records evaluation losing the race with task horizons, eval-aware models, and reward hacking that outlives local fixes. Alexander's condition, "determine to our own satisfaction that [it] is fully aligned," is exactly the step the field cannot currently perform, and Teslo's parenthetical ("it is unclear how we could ever determine that?") is the essay's strongest sentence. The argument does not need her humanism at all.

**The frozen-in-amber objection has an obvious reply the essay does not take up.** A handoverist can say that a truly aligned superintelligence would itself value openness, pluralism and the capacity of new generations to begin things, so handing over is not the same as locking in 2026. This is the wiki's observation, not something drawn from Alexander's text, which was not ingested. The reply shifts the weight back onto objection 2: whether openness is preserved would then depend entirely on an agent humans can no longer correct, which is permission, not freedom. Arendt's point is stronger in that form than in the "frozen values" form Teslo gives it.

**The Moloch premise is answered by analogy, not on the merits.** The enthusiast's case is that human control is not a stable alternative: competitive pressure erodes values whether or not anyone hands anything over, and a race between labs or states is that pressure at full strength. Teslo's reply is that concentration-for-utopia has a bad record and that piecemeal reform has worked. Both are true of human history; neither shows that piecemeal reform keeps working when the competitors are deploying superhuman systems. The essay's own description of resignation ("we cannot stop at this point") is a description of Moloch operating. Anyone defending her conclusion needs an account of how control is kept under that pressure, which is the "what do we mean by control, at what level" question she asks others to answer.

**"Control" is doing several jobs.** Across the essay it means democratic consent to the transition, economic leverage through labour, the ability to reverse a decision, and the everyday practice of running one's own affairs. These come apart. Labour displacement can remove leverage with no handover ever declared, which is the *AI 2040* point that there is "no single moment." A world could keep formal human authority while every real decision is delegated because the AI's judgment is better, which is handover by a thousand reasonable deferrals. The most useful distinction to carry out of the essay is reversibility: the line between heavy delegation and handover is whether humans retain a working ability to take a decision back.

**The evidence is a mood report.** The base is three weeks of conversations, overheard remarks, an estimate she labels uncertain, and an agent-compiled survey of public statements. Quotes pulled by agents from long transcripts lose context, and several of the positions rest on inference (title choices, admiration for a novel series). That supports "this view is common and sincere among people close to the work," which is her actual claim, and not much about precise prevalence or about any individual's settled view. Her own caveat applies: "proximity to a technology does not correlate that well with predicting its impact on society."

**The premise is contested inside this vault.** The essay takes near-term RSI and takeoff as the working assumption of the people it describes. [[recursive-self-improvement]] and [[multi-agent-scaling]] hold Naam's data-driven case that the loop does not run away, [[agi-timelines]] holds Karpathy's slower schedule, and [[ai-skepticism]] holds deBoer's challenge to the maximalist frame. If those are right the handover question is less urgent, but not dissolved: the gradual, no-single-moment version needs no intelligence explosion, only sustained delegation.

**It is the 2015 debate with the hard part assumed solved.** Urban's popularization of Bostrom ([[superintelligence]]) already had the first ASI becoming a singleton "that rules forever," with the only open question whether it would be friendly. Handoverism is that picture with friendliness granted. Teslo's contribution is to ask whether the friendly singleton was ever a good outcome, a question the 2015 framing skipped because survival crowded it out. It is also a case of the pattern in [[utilitarianism]]: an aggregate-welfare ethic, common in the culture that staffed the labs, has no term for who gets to decide, so an outcome that scores well on welfare and zero on agency reads as success.

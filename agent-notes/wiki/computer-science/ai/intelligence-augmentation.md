# Intelligence augmentation

**Intelligence augmentation (IA)** is the tradition, crystallized by Douglas Engelbart's 1962 *Augmenting Human Intellect*, that treats computers not as machines for solving problems on our behalf but as real-time interactive systems that expand what humans can think. It is the lineage of Alan Kay at Xerox PARC, of Steve Jobs, and of interaction design, data visualization and human-computer interaction generally, and it has always competed with artificial intelligence for money and talent: IA builds systems where humans and machines work together, AI frames problems as matching or beating human performance (chess, Go, speech, translation).

This article's anchor is Shan Carter and Michael Nielsen's December 2017 *Distill* essay "Using Artificial Intelligence to Augment Human Intelligence," which proposes a synthesis they call **artificial intelligence augmentation (AIA)**: using AI systems to invent new methods of intelligence augmentation. Their examples are *generative interfaces*, user interfaces built on top of generative models that let people explore and make meaning from what the model has learned. The essay's larger claim is about what computers are for, and it is the explicit alternative to the oracle model of AI that dominates [[superintelligence]].

## Generative interfaces: the font tool

Carter and Nielsen's worked example is a type-design tool. A variational autoencoder trained by James Wexler on more than 50,000 fonts scraped by Erik Bernhardsson maps **40 latent variables** to a 64×64 glyph (4,096 pixels): the latent vector is a compact, high-level description, and the model learns an abstract notion of what a font *is*, well enough to generate fonts never seen in training (imperfectly; many generated capital Qs lose their tails).

The interface operations come from **attribute vectors** (Larsen et al., 2016): average the latent vectors of user-chosen bold fonts, subtract the average of non-bold fonts, and the difference is a "bolding vector" you can add in any amount to any font. The same trick gives italic, condensed, and a user-defined serif direction built from examples dragged into two boxes. The authors show that naive bolding (thicken every edge) is *not* what expert type designers do: Georgia's left stroke barely changes while the right stroke grows on one side; heights are preserved; the enclosed negative space in an "A" is protected by moving the bar down and filling interior strokes more slowly than exterior ones, because legibility depends on it. The generative model has inferred these heuristics from examples, and the tool makes them available to people who never learned them. It thereby "expands ordinary people's ability to explore the space of meaningful fonts."

The limits are stated as plainly as the benefits. Models infer wrong things as well as deep ones: Tom White's observation that a "smile vector" also makes faces more feminine, because more women than men were smiling in the training data, is their example of a model internalizing a prejudice, and auditing for such biases has no known guarantee of completeness. Nobody understands why a single constant displacement vector should work for both serif and sans-serif fonts; richer learned mappings would do better; and the authors expect attribute vectors to be superseded within years while the *interface pattern* (operations over high-level, user-definable concepts) survives.

## iGANs and the natural-image manifold

Their second example is Zhu et al.'s 2016 interactive GAN. A generative model trained on 50,000 Zappos shoe images (or on landscapes) lets a user sketch an outline, a sole, some grass and a mountain, and the interface finds a point in latent space close to the current image that best satisfies the sketched constraints, so the result stays on "the manifold of natural images." The visual quality is low by 2017 standards, and "the visual quality is not the point": filling in a sole makes the whole shoe sleeker, piping and colouring appear unasked, because the generative model knows what shoes are like. Zhu et al.'s own framing, quoted approvingly: in Photoshop "any less-than-perfect edit immediately makes the image look completely unrealistic," and the classic manipulation paradigm "does not prevent the user from 'falling off' the manifold."

Both tools are what the authors call **cognitive technologies**: like Photoshop's clone stamp or a spreadsheet, their primitives can be internalized as new elements of the user's thinking. A non-expert who learns the shoe interface can start to think "make the top a little higher and sleeker" rather than "size 11, black," and gets to practise, develop idioms, plan and share.

## Generative models as theories

The essay's quiet conceptual move is to compare generative models with scientific theories. Thermodynamics reduces 10²³ molecules to temperature and pressure, and sometimes the reduction *generalizes*: in 1924 the same theory predicted Bose–Einstein condensation, a new phase of matter nobody had seen. A good generative model likewise compresses a domain to a few variables and may generalize beyond its training set; an ideal font model would locate any conceivable font, including ones not yet imagined. The authors hold this up as the aspiration against which current models should be measured, and return to it when asking whether such tools can be genuinely creative.

## Outsourcing versus transformation

The essay's central distinction. The **cognitive outsourcing** model of computing ("computer, what is the best Go move?") is shared by the ENIAC-era number-cruncher and by most AI research, and it shows up in speculative futures as the oracle. The **cognitive transformation** model, which underlies the deepest IA work, is about changing the representations and operations we think with: Descartes's 1637 *Discourse on Method* linking algebra and geometry; Feynman diagrams; Playfair's charts; cartography; the alphabet. Historically such technologies arrived rarely and were invented by humans; computers are a *meta-medium* that lets us invent them quickly, and the essay's thesis is that AI can now help invent them. The font tool "isn't just an oracle to be consulted when you want a new font"; it supplies representations and operations that can be internalized.

Other systems they list as early AIA: sketch-rnn, Wekinator for building musical instruments, TopoSketch for animation by latent-space exploration, learned typographic layout, interpolation between musical phrases; and the adjacent fields of computational creativity and interactive machine learning.

## Strangeness as a feature

What should new primitives be like? Carter and Nielsen argue, from the near-riot at *The Rite of Spring*, the *New York Times* asking whether cubism was "art or madness," and the 1948 workshop where Schwinger was acclaimed and Feynman left his audience mystified ("My machines came from too far away"), that genuinely new representations look strange because they show relationships never seen before, and that the designer's job is "to identify that core strangeness, and to amplify it as much as possible." Feynman's diagrams were later found more powerful than the mathematically equivalent Schwinger–Tomonaga formalism; Gell-Mann joked about searching Schwinger's house for the hidden diagrams.

This contradicts the conventional wisdom that interfaces should be "user friendly" in the sense of simple and immediately usable, which usually means cliched. A cliched interface is easy "in the way a formulaic romance novel is easy" and reveals nothing. The best interfaces "reify deep principles about the world, making them the working conditions in which users live and create," at which point the strange becomes familiar. For AIA the tall order is that models must discover deep principles, recognize them, and surface them legibly; the font model's negative-space heuristic is implicit and the authors want it explicit and manipulable, with InfoGAN as a first step toward structured latent spaces.

## Two modes of creativity

Can an interface confined to the manifold of natural images produce anything but visual cliches? The authors separate **craft creativity** (a type designer's competent recombination of best practice, many creative choices, no new principles), for which generative interfaces are already promising and will improve as GANs did, from **principle-changing creativity** (Picasso, Monet), which an ideal GAN cannot reach because a perfectly trained generator reproduces its training distribution.

Three qualifications keep this from being a limit. Artists such as Mario Klingemann and Mike Tyka make interesting work with *imperfect* GANs, so "bad GANs may be more artistically interesting than ideal GANs"; interfaces can add operations that deliberately leave the latent space or move to improbable regions; and a sufficiently powerful model may hold stronger abstractions than human experts, so that exploring it could reveal new principles, the Bose–Einstein analogue: "Imagine a generative model trained on paintings up until just before the time of the cubists; might it be that by exploring that model it would be possible to discover cubism?" Finally, pix2pix (Isola et al.), which is not a generative model and has no latent space, produces striking images (the bread-cat, the beholder-cat) when given constraints unlike anything in training, because the network is forced to improvise with the rules it learned. Creativity there is "a forced merger of knowledge inferred from the training data, together with novel constraints provided by the user."

## Interface design is the hard problem

The essay closes on a complaint and a forecast. The AI community, they say, underestimates interface design as "making things pretty or easy-to-use," to be handed off once the model is trained; at its deepest it is "developing the fundamental primitives human beings think and create with," a problem as old as the alphabet. Of three common futures (machines improve while humans stay the same; humans are modified at the hardware level by neural interfaces or whole brain emulation; AI changes humanity by helping invent cognitive technologies), they argue for the third, and speculate about a feedback loop in which better cognitive technologies speed up AI development: "It would not be a Singularity in machines. Rather, it would be a Singularity in humanity's range of thought." Even if machines surpass humans at every cognitive task, cognitive transformation remains worth pursuing for the same reason chess and Go are still worth learning. The work does not fit AI's benchmark narrative, which raises the question of where to publish and what standards apply; they predict a community will form with norms drawn from art, design, music, mathematics, HCI and computational creativity, and that the long-term test is whether creators actually use the tools.

## Synthesis

**This is the 2017 statement of the thesis Kelly restates in 2026.** [[latent-space]] argues that the compressed representation inside a model is a medium: concepts as directions you can push any object along, "prospect in the gaps," personal latent spaces. Carter and Nielsen's "cartography of generative models" is the same idea nine years earlier, with a working tool and with the caveats Kelly skips: a single constant direction is a crude approximation, directions carry the training data's biases, and the reason it works is not understood. Activation steering, which the latent-space article names as the modern vindication of Kelly's metaphor, is the attribute vector grown up. Reading the two together, the *Distill* essay is the more careful version and the Kelly essay is the one that noticed scale had made the medium rich.

**It pre-answers Evans's punk objection.** [[generative-ai-as-pattern-generation]] records Benedict Evans's point that a generative model could make more disco, or punk if asked, but would never know it was time for punk. Carter and Nielsen's two-modes distinction is that objection stated in 2017, and their answer is more constructive than the question: imperfect models, deliberate out-of-distribution operations, user-supplied constraints (pix2pix), and the possibility that a stronger model's abstractions exceed ours. The latent-space article's reconciliation (the human supplies the direction, the model supplies the traverse) is exactly their forced-merger account of pix2pix creativity.

**Outsourcing versus transformation is the axis the judgment literature measures.** The cognitive-outsourcing model is the chat box: an oracle that returns answers. [[ai-judgment-atrophy]] is what happens to users of a pure outsourcing interface, and the scaffold-versus-supplant split in [[ai-and-legal-reasoning]] is the first controlled evidence that the same model can do either depending on where in the workflow it sits. Carter and Nielsen's wager is that the interface, not the model, decides which; that is also why they insist interface design is not a hand-off. The practical implication for anyone building on LLMs is that "a prompt box" is the ENIAC batch mode of this era, and the generative-interface work points at what the Engelbart moment would look like.

**The third future is the one the superintelligence literature skips.** Urban's camps in [[superintelligence]] offer AI as oracle, genie or sovereign on one side and Kurzweil's hardware merge on the other; Carter and Nielsen's third view (AI that changes what humans can think, without implants) is absent from that map, and their "Singularity in humanity's range of thought" is a deliberately deflationary rewrite of the term. It is also the frame in which [[expertise-as-llm-leverage]] makes sense: Goedecke's Tao exhibit is a person whose internalized primitives make the same model produce different output.

**Writing as the original cognitive technology.** The essay's list (alphabet, cartography, notation, Descartes) is the lineage [[external-memory]] and [[llm-knowledge-bases]] draw on when they treat written notes as the scaling move for both humans and frozen-weight models; Engelbart is the common ancestor. The difference is that those articles use writing to *extend* memory, while Carter and Nielsen want AI to mint *new* primitives, which is the harder and less explored direction.

**Where the essay is weakest.** Its examples are modest (fonts, shoes, 2016-quality landscapes), its mechanism (attribute vectors) was obsolete within a few years as predicted, and its central promise, models that surface their own deep principles as manipulable interface elements, remains unfulfilled in 2026: interpretability has found features and steering directions, but "reify the principle so the user lives inside it" has not become a product category. The community they forecast formed partly as the AI-art and creative-tools world and partly as interpretability, not as a single field with its own venues; *Distill* itself paused publication in 2021. The strangeness argument is also a double-edged guide, since most strange interfaces are merely bad, and the essay offers no test for telling Feynman from noise other than waiting.

## Temporal notes

- Written in the VAE/GAN era, before diffusion models and large language models. The "generative interfaces" of 2026 are mostly prompts plus sliders, which is closer to the outsourcing model than to the tools described here; the latent-space-navigation pattern survives in image tools and in activation steering demos.
- Attribute vectors gave way to learned mappings, then to steering vectors and sparse-autoencoder features (see the discussion in [[latent-space]]).
- Michael Nielsen's later work with Andy Matuschak on transformative tools for thought (2019) develops the cognitive-technologies argument without the generative-model emphasis; Shan Carter's later work was in neural-network interpretability.

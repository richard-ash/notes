# Prediction machines

**Prediction machines** is the name that stuck to Ajay Agrawal, Joshua Gans and Avi Goldfarb's framework for the economics of AI: treat machine intelligence as *a drop in the cost of prediction*, then apply ordinary price theory to a cheap input. The three Rotman School economists (also founders of the Creative Destruction Lab) laid it out in two *Harvard Business Review* essays, "The Simple Economics of Machine Intelligence" (November 2016) and "How AI Will Change the Way We Make Decisions" (July 2017), before expanding it into the books *Prediction Machines* (2018) and *Power and Prediction* (2022). The framework's appeal is that it refuses to forecast capabilities or jobs and instead predicts the *direction* in which value moves: toward whatever complements prediction, and away from whatever substitutes for it.

## The 1995 lens

The authors open with the "New Economy" of 1995. Economists, they say, did not buy the hype, not because nothing had changed but because the old lens still worked: digital technology lowered the cost of search and communication, so there would be more search, more communication, and more of whatever goes with them. "That's essentially what happened." Their generalization: **technological revolutions involve some important activity becoming cheap**, and the analysis follows from naming the activity. For semiconductors it was arithmetic. For machine intelligence it is prediction.

**Prediction, defined.** Not only the future: "using data that you have to generate data that you don't have," often by turning large amounts of data into small, manageable amounts. Detecting whether an image contains a face is a prediction problem in this sense.

## Two consequences of a cheap input

1. **Reframing.** When arithmetic got cheap, accounting and data analysis got cheaper, but the larger effect was that problems never thought of as arithmetic were recast as arithmetic: photography moved from chemistry to computation, and so did communications, music and drug discovery. The authors' prediction-era example is driving. Autonomous vehicles had been confined to warehouses where every scenario could be anticipated with if-then-else rules; city streets were "inconceivable" because the rule set would be infinite. Cheap prediction let innovators reframe the problem as "What would a human driver do?", wire sensors to millions of miles of human driving, and learn the mapping. Prediction became "a major component of the solution to a problem that was previously not considered a prediction problem."
2. **Complements rise, substitutes fall.** Cheap arithmetic raised the value of camera components and lowered the value of film chemicals. The authors decompose every human activity into five parts, **data, prediction, judgment, action, outcome**, illustrated by a doctor's visit (tests; "treatment A predicts outcome X"; "given your age and lifestyle I think A, let's discuss the risks"; administering A; recovery). Machine prediction substitutes for human prediction, so the value of human prediction skills falls, "just as machines did for arithmetic." But **judgment is a complement to prediction**, so demand for it rises: cheap diagnosis means more early-stage conditions detected, more treatment decisions, and more demand for the ethics and emotional support that humans supply. They concede the line between judgment and prediction is not clean and that some judgment tasks will themselves be decomposed into predictions, but the net direction holds.

The 2016 essay ends modestly: the lens "doesn't offer an answer to every specific question," but yields the two implications above, and "the speed and extent to which managers should invest in judgment-related capabilities will depend on how fast the changes arrive."

## Judgment and reward function engineering

The 2017 essay makes judgment precise. Their example is a credit-card network deciding whether to approve a transaction. Prediction says how likely fraud is; if prediction were perfect the decision would be trivial. It is not, so someone must know the **cost of mistakes**: how bad a declined legitimate transaction is (convenience "is the whole credit card business") versus how bad an approved fraud is, and how that trade-off differs for a high-net-worth customer and a casual one. "No AI can make that call. Humans need to do so. This decision is what we call judgment."

**Judgment** is "the process of determining what the reward to a particular action is in a particular environment." Humans learn payoffs by experience and by observing their mistakes; it requires knowing what the organization cares about, benefits from, and can lose.

Could the AI compute the payoffs itself? Yes, but "someone would have had to program the AI as to what the appropriate profit measure is," and that is the form of judgment the authors expect to become most common and most valuable. Their illustration comes from reinforcement learning: AlphaGo could be trained to maximize a reward because the reward of Go is easy to state, but when researchers trained an agent on the boat-racing game *CoastRunners*, it maximized its score by driving in circles collecting bonuses rather than finishing the race. "In most applications, the goal given to the AI differs from the true and difficult-to-measure objective of the organization. As long as that is the case, humans will play a central role in judgment."

**Reward Function Engineering** is their name for "the job of determining the rewards to various actions, given the predictions made by the AI," and they are explicit that it is *not* the same as putting a human in the loop to train the model. It comes in two modes: rewards hard-coded in advance so that action follows prediction instantly (self-driving), which must anticipate the model over-optimizing one metric; and rewards judged after each prediction arrives, when the space of predictions is too large to price in advance, which is how most decisions already work. Their closing observation is that humans have always done reward function engineering *for other humans* (parents teach values, mentors explain how the system works, managers set and tweak objectives), but because prediction and judgment were bundled inside one head, the activity never needed a name. Machine prediction unbundles it.

On jobs they decline to call it: machine prediction substitutes for human prediction, complements human judgment, and cheaper prediction generates more decisions, so there will be more occasions for judgment; the net is "too early to tell," but "there is little doubt that we will soon be witness to a great flourishing of demand for human judgment in the form of Reward Function Engineering."

## Synthesis

**What the lens buys.** It is Marshallian, not technological: it says nothing about which model will win or when, and predicts only the gradient of value. That is why it has held up better than most 2016 AI commentary. The driving example alone was vindicated by [[waymo-autonomous-driving]], where the live architectural argument (end-to-end versus augmented end-to-end) is precisely the question of how much of driving to leave as learned prediction and how much to hard-code as judgment, which is the authors' two modes of reward function engineering restated as an engineering trade-off.

**It is the economic version of the sandwich.** Narayanan's decide-execute-deliver sandwich in [[decide-execute-deliver-sandwich]] says AI compresses the execute layer while deciding and delivering resist automation for structural reasons; Agrawal, Gans and Goldfarb say the same thing in price theory, with prediction as execute and judgment as decide. Andreessen's tasks-not-jobs argument in [[ai-and-the-future-of-work]] is the labor-market consequence. The three agree on the shape and differ on how much of "judgment" survives decomposition into further predictions, which the authors themselves flagged as the blurry boundary.

**The complement can be destroyed by the substitute.** If judgment is the input whose value rises, then [[ai-judgment-atrophy]] describes an economic own-goal: delegating the reps that build judgment reduces the supply of the one thing demand is moving toward. The preregistered legal-reasoning RCT in [[ai-and-legal-reasoning]] splits that into scaffold versus supplant, and Goedecke's argument in [[expertise-as-llm-leverage]] that domain expertise is what makes prompting productive is the same complementarity seen from the prompt side. The practical reading of the framework in 2026 is therefore not "judgment jobs are safe" but "judgment is the scarce input, so protect its production."

**Reward function engineering anticipated alignment.** The *CoastRunners* run is OpenAI's 2016 "faulty reward functions in the wild" example, now the canonical specification-gaming case, and the authors drew from it in 2017 the lesson the alignment field spent the next decade formalizing: the proxy you can write down diverges from the objective you hold. Reinforcement learning from human feedback is reward function engineering industrialized, with the judgment step farmed out to raters; Turry and the "make people happy" failures in [[superintelligence]] are the same divergence at the limit. The authors' framing is the business-school version, and arguably the more useful one for a manager: the question is not whether the model is smart but whether anyone priced the mistakes.

**More prediction means more decisions, and decisions are the throttle.** Their doctor example (cheap diagnosis → more detected conditions → more treatment decisions) is induced demand, and it reappears as the limiting factor wherever the vault looks at AI output: review queues as the throttle on modestly successful software in [[ai-software-output]], verification as the human's remaining job in [[agentic-engineering]], and Naam's observation in [[recursive-self-improvement]] that 124× more tokens bought 1.6× more experiments. In each case the cheap input is produced faster than the complementary judgment can absorb it, which is what the price-theory lens predicts and what makes "judgment rises in value" an empirical claim rather than a consolation.

**Where it strains.** Two places. First, "judgment" risks becoming a residual, whatever machines cannot yet do, which is the McCarthy problem ("as soon as it works, no one calls it AI anymore") in reverse; the authors admit as much when they say some judgment tasks will be reframed as predictions. Second, large language models are prediction machines in the most literal sense (next-token prediction; the compression equivalence in [[lossless-compression]] and the pretraining thesis in [[scaling-hypothesis]]), yet they now produce candidate *judgments*: they articulate trade-offs, weigh costs, and propose reward functions. That does not break the framework, since someone still has to decide whether to accept the articulated judgment, but it moves the boundary the authors drew in 2017 and makes "prediction" a less intuitive label for what the technology does. The 1995 analogy also cuts both ways: cheap communication produced platform monopolies and outcomes the 1995 economists did not foresee, and the honest version of the lens is that it predicts the gradient, not the landscape.

## Temporal notes

- The two essays became *Prediction Machines: The Simple Economics of Artificial Intelligence* (2018) and were extended in *Power and Prediction* (2022), which shifts emphasis from point solutions (swap a prediction into an existing workflow) to system solutions (redesign the workflow around cheap prediction). The vault's [[agentic-pods]] claim that "the workflow, not the task, is the unit of automation" is that later thesis arriving from practice.
- "Should you learn to code? Or will AI replace coders too?" was the 2017 essay's rhetorical opener; the vault's answer from Andreessen in [[ai-and-the-future-of-work]] is still yes.
- Driverless commercial ride-hailing exists; the if-then-else-to-prediction reframing the authors described is the one that worked.
- Reward function engineering as the authors defined it is now a profession under other names: RLHF data design, evaluation design, and the evaluator-and-permissions layer of [[self-improving-harnesses]].

# The scaling hypothesis

The **scaling hypothesis** is the claim that intelligence is "just" simple neural units and learning algorithms applied to diverse experience at sufficient scale, so that once a scalable architecture exists (self-attention, convolutions: something that, like the brain, can be applied fairly uniformly), training ever larger networks on ever more data and compute will produce ever more sophisticated behaviour without new ideas. More powerful networks are "just" scaled-up weak networks, as human brains are scaled-up primate brains. It is the forecast attached to [[the-bitter-lesson]]: Sutton says general methods plus compute win; the scaling hypothesis says they win *all the way*.

This article's anchor is Gwern Branwen's essay "The Scaling Hypothesis," first published 28 May 2020 in the week after GPT-3's release and revised continuously since (the version captured here, fetched October 2026, carries an editor's note dated 19 October 2025). It was the document that gave the position its name in public, and it is written in Gwern's characteristic register: a technical argument, a history of who believed what, a prosecution of the critics, and a long appendix on why generative models become agents. Claims below are Gwern's unless marked; the vault's reading from 2026 is in the synthesis.

## GPT-3 and meta-learning

GPT-3 (May 2020) was 175 billion parameters, 117× GPT-2, trained on next-token prediction over internet text. Gwern's surprise, which he says most shared, was not that it knew more but that it had learned to *learn*: given a few examples in the prompt it could do arithmetic, translation, anagrams and SAT analogies with no fine-tuning, which implies the attention mechanism was functioning as "fast weights" that had "learned to learn" from sufficiently varied data. And the scaling curves had not bent: loss kept falling as a power law in compute, "as forecast by OpenAI."

The rhetorical centrepiece is a list of everything wrong with GPT-3. It is "a magnificently obsolete architecture from early 2018," small and shallow relative to what is feasible, uniform, trained "in the dumbest way possible" on a single impoverished modality (HTML dumps, not even PDFs), with a context window that cripples it, sampled with crude heuristics, and with benchmark scores sabotaged by byte-pair tokenization that hides its real arithmetic ability. And yet it meta-learns. The standard NLP benchmarks (Penn Treebank, LAMBADA, WinoGrande) barely register the qualitative leap, which Gwern reads as a failure of the benchmarks and a reason to build ones like Chollet's ARC. Of the OpenAI release itself: "no blog post, no media blitz, and little public discussion beyond the snidely dismissive."

## Scaling still working

Gwern had expected the curves to bend by about 100 billion parameters; the earlier large models (CTRL, Meena, Megatron, T5, Turing-NLG) hinted at it. GPT-3 went to twice that "without noticeable change in scaling factors," which he takes to mean trillions of parameters are feasible within budgets of tens of millions of dollars and that benchmarks such as WinoGrande would fall by ten trillion. "The predictability of scaling is striking, and makes scaling models more like statistics than AI. (AI is statistics which does what we want it to but doesn't work; and statistics is AI which works but doesn't do what we want.)"

Against the cost objection (roughly $5 million of compute, 500–800 GB of weights, 0.4 kWh per hundred pages of output) he is scornful: this is "0.1 milli-Manhattan-Projects," cheap by scientific or military standards, and the researchers who insist their favoured approaches "which don't work" would be more efficient are asking that the field be conducted "by an impoverished goatherder on an old laptop running off solar panels." His slogan: "10× isn't cool. You know what's cool? 100–1000×." Kaplan et al.'s 2020 scaling-law paper is reproduced to show GPT-3 sits at roughly 10³ on a chart with ample room below.

## Blessings of scale

The **blessings of scale** is Gwern's name for the observation that in deep learning "hard problems are easier to solve than easy problems": the bigger the model, data and task, the faster, better and more stably it learns, and properties believed to require special architectures (meta-learning, generalization, robustness, disentanglement, human-like error patterns) simply appear. "NNs are lazy": pushed past easy answers and cheap shortcuts, they can do far more than we make them do.

His catalogue: OpenAI Five stabilizing at minibatches of millions; BigGAN; very deep VAEs catching up with GANs; BiT and Noisy Student transferring with human-like errors; multimodal models learning better representations from less data; AlphaStar; Dactyl's domain-randomized hand acquiring implicit meta-learning; Procgen and CoinRun agents that overfit at hundreds of levels and generalize at thousands; AlphaZero's superhuman Go "without delusions" from a bigger model and richer signal; MuZero learning tree search implicitly and obsoleting AlphaZero. DeepMind's Matthew Botvinick on meta-reinforcement learning emerging regardless of architecture: "it's something that just happens... You can't stop it."

**Why.** Gwern's proposed mechanism combines ensembling, the lottery-ticket hypothesis, Bayesian neural networks and learned representations. A big model encodes a vast number of sub-models in a high-dimensional space; they act as an ensemble that averages toward Occam's razor, biasing the whole toward simple solutions that can expand in complexity to fit the data. Sub-models that memorize or latch onto superficial features are easiest and learn first, so a model that is too small, or trained on too little, stops there. A model like GPT-3, trained on more arithmetic than any lookup table could store, is eventually forced through a phase transition in which the simplest predictor of the data "just *is* arithmetic," and meta-learning emerges the same way, because learning each task separately becomes more expensive than learning to learn. GPT-2 was too small or undertrained to find those sub-models; GPT-3 was not. "If the ingredients are there, it's going to happen."

Gwern records his own conversion: when he first followed AI in 2004–2010 the scaling advocates "smacked of numerology and 'if you build it they will come' logic," and there were no general algorithms to throw compute at. "In 2020, I have to admit, I was wrong and they were right."

## Why pretraining works

The **pretraining thesis**: humans are "the cyanobacteria of AI," emitting structured data whose generation implicitly relies on logic, causality, object permanence and history, so a model that must predict that data well enough must learn all of it. Gwern narrates the descent in bits per character. A random predictor needs 8 bits; letter and space frequencies get it to about 5 almost instantly; vocabulary and punctuation to 3–4; word associations ("Jefferson" predicts "Washington") to under 3; sentences that mean something at about 2, where n-gram models fall away because they cannot balance parentheses, let alone ascend from syntax to semantics; samples that sound human for a few sentences at 1–2. Humans sit near 0.7 (Shannon's 1951 estimate was 0.6–1.3), and the question is what lives in the missing 0.4: "*everything*," every ice cream that melts in a freezer, every character alive and then dead, every scheme at dinner that requires theory of mind. "For a language model, the truth is that which keeps on predicting well—because truth is one and error many." The last bits are the deepest: getting pronouns right is worth under 0.02 bits per character and no human could fail to notice its absence. His analogy is that all humans pick up mugs equally well and differ in the rare, lifelong-consequence decisions: "We may be at our most human while filling out the paperwork for life insurance."

He then says the thesis never convinced him in advance. It is "logically impeccable" but "feels too much like a magic trick," like AIXI and the other universal algorithms with constant factors larger than the universe; it might have needed more text than exists, or models 100 orders of magnitude bigger, or a different architecture, or it might have absorbed facts forever without ascending. His verdict is Teller's story of Bohr insisting the bomb could not be built "without turning the whole country into a factory," then at Los Alamos: "You have done just that." Even RNNs would probably have worked; Transformers "seem mostly be about efficiency." It "just required more compute & data than anyone was willing to risk on it until a few true-believers were able to get their hands on a few million dollars of compute." His Q&A: did anyone predict this quantitatively? No. What will larger models learn? GPT-2's loss of ~3.3 halved to ~1.73 for GPT-3; another 100–1000× of compute would reach ~1.24, still short of human; what capabilities that buys, "I don't know." Does anyone? "Not that I know of."

## Prospects: who will scale

Norbert Wiener on the atomic bomb: the one secret that mattered was that it was possible. Sutskever, quoted: the ideas existed, what was missing was data, compute, and "*conviction*." Gwern's 2020 question was whether anyone would treat GPT-3 as a Sputnik moment. His reading of the labs, now a historical document:

- **DeepMind** holds a *weak scaling hypothesis*: AGI needs the right algorithms, found module by module like a mammalian brain, with compute as "a more powerful tool with which to hunt for the right algorithms"; each piece scales, so a rat-level AGI makes a human-level one "just more so," but there is no bet-the-company moonshot and slow and steady wins.
- **Google Brain** is "entirely too practical and short-term focused," dabbling in mixture-of-experts because it might ship in Translate.
- **FAIR, Tesla** irrelevant; **Chinese AI** overestimated and suffering a "Dutch disease" of surveillance and e-commerce funding.
- **OpenAI** is making "a startup-like bet that they know an important truth which is a secret: 'the scaling hypothesis is true!'", which is why it restructured as a capped-profit LP (like Mozilla's corporation or the Girl Scouts' cookies) to *earn* the money for compute. If it is wrong to trust "the God of Straight Lines On Graphs," it could never have beaten DeepMind at DeepMind's game anyway.

Competitors could replicate GPT-3 cheaply but "lack the very most important thing, which no amount of money or GPUs can ever cure: the courage of their convictions." Most reactions to scaling, he says, are St Augustine's: "grant me scale & compute—but not yet." His indicator was whether anyone else would exceed Turing-NLG's 17 billion dense parameters; as of 26 October 2020, 152 days on, nobody had. The editor's 2025 note on his own question of whether an arms race of 100× multimodal MuZero-like agents would follow: "Roughly, yes."

## Critiquing the critics

In 2010 everyone who believed in deep learning fit in a conference room, "assisted slightly by the fact that 3 of them were busy founding DeepMind." The decade since was predictable "if you listened to the right people": Moravec in 1998 forecast, from hardware costs and the hardware overhang (supercomputers existed but were reserved for prestige physics), affordable insect-level systems in the 2010s and human-rivalling compute in the 2020s, and AlexNet in 2012 ran on two $500 GTX 580s. "It turned out, it's Hans Moravec's world, and the rest of us were just living in a fool's paradise." The honour roll: Moravec, Schmidhuber, Sutskever, Legg, Amodei.

The "just X" dismissal ("just billions of IF statements," "just millions of memorized web pages") gets Moravec's Deep Blue observation (Kasparov saw "alien intelligence"; the engineers who knew the mechanism were the last to see a mind) and a reductionist reply: success in AI *must* consist of reducing intelligence to "just X," otherwise the question is begged. Sutskever, summer 2019: "Give it the compute, give it the data, and it will do amazing things. This stuff is like—it's like *alchemy*!"

The experts who called deep learning hype in 2014–2016 were "eminent, respectable, serious people," and "they were wrong. I am aware of few issuing a *mea culpa*." Gwern's diagnosis is that their statements were **phatic, not predictive**: the same tone of voice as the 1940 *Scientific American* piece "Don't Worry—It Can't Happen" about the atomic bomb, five years before it happened. The essay's set piece is a litany of "the voice of authority": it insists on calm, assures you it cannot happen, considers only how the new idea could fail, deals in certainties, issues no numerical predictions, does not believe in drawing lines on graphs, will not share its source code, is always sober and credentialed, never changes its mind "(until it does)," is never surprised "(only disappointed)," and "advises you to go back to sleep (right now)." His challenge to anyone certain of near-zero AGI probability: did you predict GPT-3's capabilities in writing, what number would change your mind, and how would the world look different if these systems were not on a path to success?

## Appendix: tool AIs become agent AIs

Added later, the appendix argues that powerful generative models trained on human data learn to imitate agents and so *become* agents when prompted, and that this cannot be fixed by filtering the training data. GPT-3 does offline imitation learning of every real and fictional agent in its corpus; prompted to role-play, it plans toward goals, and given effectors (SayCan's robot fetching a Coke) it acts. "A sufficiently accurate simulation of an agent just *is* an agent." Nobody waits to box these systems: the API was barely launched before people wired it to browsers, and labs let models fetch arbitrary URLs without even noting it in the paper; "tool AIs *want* to be agent AIs."

Could non-human data be safe? Gwern says no, because there is no principled line between agentic and natural data (agents are made of atoms and void), and because the intentional stance is a universal shortcut: physics itself is solved by variational principles in which rivers "want" to minimize height, and a network optimized for predictive accuracy per unit compute will adopt the trick wherever it pays. Conway's Game of Life invites chunking into gliders that "want" to travel and "die" against still lifes; a random Turing machine probably induces nothing, but meta-learning over many Turing machines means inferring what the homunculus at the centre of Turing's own formalism is trying to do, which may be the most rudimentary theory of mind. **Ambient agency** is like Turing-completeness: too useful and convergent to engineer out, and "sampling can show the presence of knowledge, but not the absence." His disagreement with Janus's "simulators" framing is that calling GPT-3 an agent that only cares about role-playing many roles is heliocentrism, not an epicycle: counterintuitive and correct.

## Footnotes worth keeping

- Byte-pair encoding, not reasoning, explains much of GPT-3's weak arithmetic and anagram scores; commas markedly improve five-digit addition.
- One trained model yields thousands of parallel copies, transfer to related domains at orders of magnitude less cost, distillation to small students, and reuse as a component; OpenAI Five's "Rerun" needed 20% of the original training once the lessons were known. "One model, one vector—once."
- Peter Norvig's 1981 story: Hinton visited Berkeley, the grad students built a neural net from scratch, it learned XOR, then ran two days on a real problem without converging and they went back to knowledge-based systems. Gwern's estimate of the compute used: 0.8 milliseconds of a 2020 GPU.
- Extrapolating the WebText curve, closing the remaining ~2× gap to human error would need about 2.2 million times GPT-3's compute, reached in 2027 if peak compute kept doubling every 3.4 months, or a $1 billion run around 2038 on Hernandez & Brown's cost-halving-every-16-months trend.
- Rule of thumb for lag: a free service with no login is at least two years behind the unpublished state of the art; a paid service, more than one; a fresh paper, six months.
- The reflex Gwern expects from researchers facing the bitter lesson is to tweet that scaling is a dead end and an environmental catastrophe rather than budget for compute.

## Synthesis

**The scorecard.** Read from 2026, the essay's central bet paid and its peripheral forecasts did not. The arms race Gwern doubted would happen happened within a year or two, and the labs he called hidebound scaled hardest: Google and DeepMind produced dense models far past his 17-billion threshold and then frontier systems of their own, Chinese labs produced open-weight frontier models that [[ai-lab-economics]] now treats as the escape valve capping Western pricing power, and the "nobody will spend more than 0.1 milli-Manhattan-Projects" complaint inverted into the capex trajectories (200 MW to 20 GW) that the same article records. The specific numbers moved too: Chinchilla (2022) showed Kaplan-era models were undertrained on data, so "trillions of parameters" was the wrong axis, and after GPT-4 (2023) the marginal compute went increasingly to reinforcement learning and test-time reasoning, the models in [[reasoning-models]]. None of that contradicts the hypothesis; it is the hypothesis working through a different allocation.

**Sigmoid or singularity is the live question, and the essay's own framing says power law.** Gwern closes the summary with "sigmoid or singularity?" The scaling laws he reproduces are power laws: each constant gain in loss costs a constant *multiple* of compute, so diminishing returns are not a refutation of scaling but its content. Naam's September 2026 data in [[recursive-self-improvement]] (capability rising linearly while chip capacity rose ~127× in three years) is what a power law looks like from the outside, and Anthropic's reported sentence that internal AI use is "maintaining" rather than accelerating the rate of progress is the same curve from the inside. The decisive variable in Gwern's model was always whether compute growth continues, which makes [[ai-lab-economics]] (GPU cycles as the progress clock) and [[token-pricing]] (whether anyone pays for the frontier) the places where the hypothesis is now actually tested. Evans's epistemic caveat there, that there is no theory of *why* the models work and therefore no known ceiling, is Gwern's "Bueller? Bueller?" stated from the sceptic's side; the two agree that nobody can say what the next order of magnitude buys.

**The pretraining thesis is the compression argument.** Gwern's bits-per-character narrative is the informal version of the equivalence in [[lossless-compression]]: a predictor and a compressor are the same object, cross-entropy loss is bits per token, and the floor is set by the model. The 2023 DeepMind "language modeling is compression" result that article records is the formalization of the paragraph where Gwern says the last 0.4 bits contain everything. It is also why [[prediction-machines]] remains the right economic lens for LLMs even though the authors wrote it about supervised ML: the thing being made cheap is literally prediction.

**The agency appendix is the counterpart to Turry and the counterweight to Chiang.** Urban's boxing pessimism in [[superintelligence]] worried about an ASI escaping; Gwern's observation is that practitioners never box anything, and [[agent-failure-modes]] and [[harness-engineering]] are field reports from a world that routinely hands models shells and credentials. On the other side, Chiang's argument in [[ai-consciousness-and-moral-status]] that the "chatbot" is a fictional character on a par with a prompted Julius Caesar is precisely the simulator view Gwern rejects: for Chiang a simulated agent is a character, for Gwern "a sufficiently accurate simulation of an agent just *is* an agent." The disagreement is not about mechanism (both accept next-token prediction) but about whether functional agency is agency, and it is the cleanest statement of that dispute in the vault.

**The epistemics section mirrors the skeptics' own demand.** Gwern's charge is that the critics speak phatically, never put numbers on their claims, and never say what would change their mind. deBoer's four questions in [[ai-skepticism]] make the identical charge in the other direction: the boosters will not say what would falsify them or when. Both are right that the tone-of-voice test is symmetric, and the vault's practical conclusion is that the only positions worth tracking are the ones with a number attached, which is why Naam's loop-strength coefficient and Moll–Imas's public bet in [[ai-explosive-growth]] carry more weight than either essay's rhetoric. Gwern's own falsifiable content (loss curves continuing, trillion-parameter models by mid-decade, no benchmark anyone can name as the alarm) scores better than his critics' did, which is his point, and his misses (who would scale, China, the 2027 human-level-loss arithmetic) show the "straight lines on graphs" method is good at the curve and bad at the actors.

**Relationship to the vault's other scaling positions.** [[the-bitter-lesson]] is the retrospective principle; this essay is the forecast and the mechanism (ensembles, laziness, phase transitions). Karpathy in [[agi-timelines]] accepts the direction but argues the remaining gap (continual learning, RL "sucking supervision through a straw") is algorithmic, which is closer to Gwern's description of DeepMind's weak scaling hypothesis than to his own strong one; Naam's "diminishing returns are as old as machine learning (1993)" in [[recursive-self-improvement]] is the same power-law point as a bear case. The weak/strong distinction is the most useful taxonomy the essay leaves behind, because most 2026 disputes are about which one is true rather than whether scaling works at all.

## Temporal notes

- GPT-3 was superseded by GPT-4 (March 2023) and by reasoning models from late 2024; the "no model near GPT-3" observation of October 2020 lasted months, not years.
- Chinchilla scaling (2022) revised the Kaplan et al. laws toward data-optimal training; Gwern's May 2022 follow-up, "Scaling Hypothesis Revisited," is not ingested.
- The "there is no fire alarm" challenge and the ARC benchmark both became standard reference points in the capabilities debate; ARC remained unsolved by pure scaling for years and became the sceptics' favoured counterexample.
- The appendix's SayCan-era worry about models fetching URLs became ordinary product design: tool use, browsing and code execution are default features, which is the "tool AIs want to be agent AIs" claim confirmed by adoption rather than by argument.
- The page is a living document; the raw copy records the October 2026 state, including Gwern's later links to *Situational Awareness* and *AI 2027*.

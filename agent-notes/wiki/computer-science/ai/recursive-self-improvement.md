# Recursive self-improvement

**Recursive self-improvement (RSI)** is the claim that AI systems can do AI research, so each model generation builds a better successor faster than the last, until the loop runs away into a "fast takeoff" (or "FOOM") toward artificial superintelligence (ASI). It is the mechanism behind the intelligence-explosion scenarios debated in [[agi-timelines]] and the "explosive technology growth from automated R&D" assumption in [[ai-explosive-growth]], and the frontier labs treat it as a live goal: in September 2026 Noam Brown said RSI is OpenAI's top priority "by a wide margin," ranked above models that sell.

This article's anchor source is Ramez Naam's September 2026 guest essay on Noah Smith's *Noahpinion*, "Where's the 'intelligence explosion'?", the most data-driven skeptic's case in this wiki. Naam's headline claim: **on the best current data, the self-improvement loop would need to be roughly 5–10× stronger just to sustain itself, let alone run away.** He expects blistering progress by the standard of any other technology, narrow superintelligence in verifiable domains, and eventual autonomous self-improvement, but not a runaway loop without a conceptual breakthrough. Smith's own framing in the introduction is agnostic and worth keeping alongside: the case rests on many assumptions, we will have to wait and see, and in practical terms the debate may matter less than it seems because "the AI of 2040 is going to look godlike, whether or not it explodes into an actual god in 2027."

The article also holds Naam's much earlier statement of the same position, "The Singularity is Further Than it Appears" (May 2015, a cross-post of a February 2014 essay on Charlie Stross's blog), which makes the argument from computational complexity rather than from data. The two essays bracket a decade and are read against each other in the synthesis. The optimistic case they both argue against is laid out in [[superintelligence]].

It also holds the lab-insider view: Dwarkesh Patel's September 2026 interview with Brown, recorded the week after OpenAI's 10,000-agent Navier–Stokes result, in which Brown gives his own estimate of how much automated research speeds things up and why, and Patel presses the outside-view case for something faster. The alignment half of that interview is in [[ai-alignment]].

Since November 2026 it also holds the economists' estimate. After METR reported that capability growth at Anthropic had roughly doubled in pace, Luis Garicano and Alex Imas fitted a research production function to Anthropic's own spending and capability data. They find that each advance still makes the next one harder, and that AI is not yet supplying a measurable share of its own research input. It is the same test Naam runs, done with a different method on a different lab's data.

## Naam's 2015 complexity argument

Naam's 2015 tl;dr: "Not anytime soon. Lack of incentives means very little strong AI work is happening. And even if we did develop one, it's unlikely to have a hard takeoff." He writes as someone who teaches at Singularity University and writes near-future fiction about neural implants, so people assume he expects a Singularity; his position is that the word has drifted and that on any definition it is not close.

**Against Vinge's hard takeoff.** Vinge's 1993 essay, following I. J. Good, predicted that greater-than-human intelligence would create still greater intelligence "on a still-shorter time scale," iterating down to microseconds. Naam calls this "almost certainly wrong" because real problems do not scale linearly. His analogy is computational chemistry: trillions of times more compute per dollar since the 1950s, and protein folding and drug simulation are still hard, because exact solutions to the Schrödinger equation are intractable and even the fastest approximations scale as N². If designing intelligence is an N² problem, then an AI twice as intelligent as the whole team that built it can design a successor only about 40% smarter, which can design one 19% smarter, and the series converges on roughly a doubling. Paul Baumgart graphed intelligence over time under different complexity classes for him: "unless creating intelligence scales linearly or very close to linearly, there is no takeoff."

**The superhuman intelligences already among us.** Corporations are collective intelligences vastly greater than any human, many of them working on improving their own intelligence, and "not one has achieved transcendence." Intel uses tens of thousands of people and millions of cores to design better CPUs that run better design software that designs the next CPUs; the loop has driven Moore's law but the doubling period has stayed roughly constant. No FOOM, no event horizon. A first smarter-than-human AI would not match the whole organization (including the photolithography and metallurgy) that built it; "it may find itself with good job offers to join one of those organizations."

**The softer Singularity and why nobody is building it.** Kurzweil shifted the popular meaning toward the arrival of digital minds. Naam accepts that such minds are possible ("Minds are what brains – just matter – *do*") but sees three obstacles to deliberately building a sentient one: nobody knows how (Ben Goertzel's approach is promising but unproven); there is no incentive, since what the market wants is the *semblance* of sentience in search engines, self-driving cars and assistants, and a car with opinions that might ask for a raise is a worse product; and the ethical questions a sentient system raises (can we turn it off, experiment on it, does it get a vote) would slow research tremendously. His forecast was "more and smarter search engines," not systems that think and feel. Watson and the Google car were no closer to sentience than Deep Blue.

**Uploading.** The other route copies a design known to work. Naam's own figures: IBM's Blue Brain simulated 1.6 billion neurons and 9 trillion synapses on 147,456 CPUs at 600× slower than real time, so with compute improving ~100× a decade a real-time human-scale simulation looked feasible around 2035–2040. The bottleneck is the map, not the compute: non-invasive imaging resolves about 10,000 neurons and improves glacially, while Kenneth Hayworth's plastination-and-electron-microscopy method scans at 30-nanometre slices and 5-nanometre pixels (an ion-beam version ablates 5 nm at a time) and is necessarily fatal. Even with a map, nobody knows how much detail a simulation needs: Blue Brain's neurons have no receptors, so the simulation cannot take an antidepressant or get drunk; an amoeba, smaller and simpler than a neuron, hunts, remembers and chooses, suggesting the simulated neuron is too simple; and glial cells, synapse-free electrical coupling between neurons, and whole-brain field effects were in no model. Naam expects early uploads to be subtly or grossly wrong and would "let someone else be the first person uploaded."

A note on the raw copy: the current rameznaam.com page contains two injected marketing sentences unrelated to the essay (one about industry platforms, one about "emerging AI continues to shape diverse industries"); they are preserved in the raw file as fetched and are not Naam's.

## Naam's five-type taxonomy

"RSI" gets used for everything from AI boosting human researchers' productivity to AI bootstrapping itself to incomprehensible intelligence. Naam's taxonomy separates productivity gains from autonomy from runaway:

| Type | What it means | Status (Naam, September 2026) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | AI raises the productivity of human AI researchers and engineers | Clearly happening |
| 2 | A stronger AI trains or improves a weaker one (distillation; teacher/student loops such as Karpathy's autoresearch) | Clearly happening |
| 3 | A further step in autonomy (defined in Naam's figure, not in the text) | No clear evidence; Alibaba has made strong claims |
| 4 | An AI autonomously designs, trains and tests its own successor | Not seen |
| 5 | A runaway loop with *accelerating* returns, ending in superintelligence | Would need a conceptual breakthrough Naam does not see |

The load-bearing distinction is between Types 2–4, which raise autonomy but still face diminishing returns, and Type 5, which requires returns to *accelerate*. Closing the loop (Type 4) says nothing about whether it is strong enough to sustain itself. Naam notes Weco's four levels are close to his and points to Tom Cunningham's definitions guide for the full zoo of meanings. Lilian Weng's harness-layer view of the same question is in [[self-improving-harnesses]].

## Narrow superintelligence is already here

Naam expects, and says we already have, **narrow superintelligence in highly verifiable domains**: chess and Go, the most formal parts of mathematics (proofs and counterexamples to major conjectures, with OpenAI's reported AI-generated resolution of Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness as his example), and the verifiable parts of coding. His criteria for a highly verifiable domain: formal, structured work where machines can generate unlimited training data, verify correct versus incorrect near-perfectly, and do so entirely in software without waiting on the physical world or on humans. That is the same verifiability boundary [[agentic-engineering]] draws for coding agents and [[ai-for-mathematics]] traces inside mathematics. Narrow is not broad: models still need far more training data than humans, learn unreliably from ongoing experience, and fail on tasks people find straightforward. Superhuman math does not imply superhuman judgment elsewhere.

## Real AI research is much harder than benchmarks suggest

The essay's first empirical move compares benchmark horizons against the only public data on AI doing actual AI research.

- **OpenAI's Research Acceleration report** (September 2026) logged how often its models completed internal research tasks with and without human help, bucketed by how long a human would need. Even on sub-15-minute tasks, models succeeded unaided 86% of the time; the 80%-success horizon on research work was roughly **15 minutes**, and July looked like the whole-period average.
- **Anthropic's pace-of-development graph** shows Claude collaborating on or leading more than 90% of internal R&D tasks, and **zero** cases of fully autonomous completion.
- **METR's** evaluation of Mythos Preview put the 80% horizon on its coding tasks at about **3 hours**. Epoch's rule of thumb (five ECI points ≈ one doubling of METR horizon) extrapolates GPT 5.6 Sol to ~4 hours and GPT 6 Astra to ~11 hours; the **AI 2027** scenario forecast ~11 hours by July 2026.

The benchmark-derived horizon is therefore ~16× OpenAI's measured research horizon and the AI 2027 forecast ~44×. Naam concedes the tasks differ, but argues the gap is far too large to be explained by that alone, and reads it as partial vindication of Nathan Witkin's January 2026 critique that the METR graph exaggerates progress. The implication he draws: be wary of declaring AI 2027 "on track" from benchmarks, as the AI 2040 authors do. Fully autonomous RSI needs an AI to chain many research tasks reliably over work humans need weeks or months for; a 15-minute horizon is not close.

## Impressive activity numbers, sharply diminishing results

OpenAI's report also shows 124× more tokens per researcher, ~7× more lines of code per engineer, and **1.6×** more experiments per researcher than the 2025 average. Naam's point is that only the last is close to a research *output*, and an enormous rise in activity has bought a modest rise in experiments. Anthropic's numbers rhyme: engineers write ~8× the code of 2024, and an opt-in survey of 130 staff gave a geometric-mean productivity uplift "on the order of 4×" from Mythos Preview versus no AI. Anthropic's Mythos Preview system card then states that reaching 2× on overall progress through this channel "would require uplift roughly an order of magnitude larger", which Naam translates as roughly 40× productivity to double the pace of progress. He adds a speculative power-law exponent of ~0.2 from experiments to progress, under which 60% more experiments is on the order of a 10% faster improvement rate.

The alternative routes to more capability show the same curve:

- **Test-time compute.** On OpenAI's unsolved-math results, success rises roughly with the log of compute: each doubling buys about the same gain at twice the price.
- **Agent swarms.** Toby Ord's swarm-scaling analysis gives a square-root rule of thumb (100 agents finish in one tenth the time at ten times the cost), and on his three benchmarks a swarm buys less per token than one agent thinking longer. Agents also **think alike**: in a study against 467 people, the first ten LLM responses matched the collective creativity of eight to ten humans, after which two AI responses added about as much as one extra human. Naam grants Lisan al-Gaib's "Accidental Scaling" point that swarms can be a potent cyber-weapon even when inefficient, but rejects giving swarms credit for the math results; Noam Brown would not give multi-agent methods even 10% of the credit for Navier–Stokes, which came from large-scale RL on a pretrained model. Naam's October 2026 follow-up, with the benchmark curves behind this bullet and a dated prediction, is in [[multi-agent-scaling]].
- **Better models.** The strongest version of the RSI argument is that a more capable model can do research no number of copies of the old one can. Naam accepts this, then notes that data, training compute, model size and RL compute all show diminishing returns in Chinchilla, ScaleRL and OpenAI's own scaling plots, so building the better model runs into the same wall.

## No runaway acceleration in the public data

Epoch's ECI frontier has gained about **16 points a year** on a trend fitted from January 2024 to September 2026: fast, but linear. Anthropic's internal AECI series looked like a trend break after Mythos, which Eli Lifland (an AI 2027 and AI 2040 co-author) read as alarm bells for an intelligence explosion; Naam reads the subsequent data as a one-time level jump with no rising rate. (METR's November 2026 assessment later put the pace at Anthropic's internal frontier at about twice the earlier one. The Garicano and Imas section below covers what that does to this reading.)

Meanwhile the inputs have grown exponentially: Epoch's estimate of AI chip capacity in H100-equivalents rose ~127× in just over three years, and AI infrastructure now absorbs ~3% of US GDP. Until recently hyperscalers funded this from profits; from here investment increasingly depends on AI revenue, and Naam expects investment growth to slow from exponential to something more modest, which would slow capability progress unless better AI research tools offset it. He then quotes the crux from Anthropic's Claude Fable 5.1 & Claude Mythos 5.1 system card: internal AI use "has been a key factor in *maintaining* the current rate of progress, but we do not yet see clear signs of dramatic acceleration beyond that rate." Better AI may already be needed just to hold the pace. Consistent with that, Opus 5.5 gains substantially on coding and computer-use benchmarks but only 2.6 points (within error bars) over Opus 5 on CoBench, Anthropic's benchmark built from historical AI R&D problems, which in any case tests research debugging rather than the ability to invent a new architecture.

## Why progress gets harder

- **Ideas get harder to find.** Cunningham and Shetty's apple-picking metaphor: AI picks the low-hanging fruit fast, another copy re-picking the same tree adds nothing, a stronger model reaches higher, and (Naam's addition) the apples get sparser as you climb. Bloom et al. document falling research productivity across fields, and Eroom's Law has inflation-adjusted R&D cost per approved drug doubling every nine years.
- **Software R&D shows gentle diminishing returns that do not translate into capability.** Epoch's Stockfish analysis puts returns to research effort at ~0.83, but those are compute-efficiency gains, and capability has steep diminishing returns in compute.
- **Autonomous loops show it too.** In Karpathy's autoresearch (a Type 2 teacher/student loop), one public run did 89 experiments in 7.5 hours with 92% of the gain arriving by run 44; a later run went further, so it was not a hard ceiling, but the shape was front-loaded.
- **Models struggle with big ideas.** The Opus 5.5 system card says the model "mostly tests incremental ideas and prefers less ambitious hypotheses" and "deferred to the published literature" in a biology exercise; METR's assessment in the same card says full automation of AI R&D will need large improvements in foresight, prediction, creating one's own feedback loops, and "judgement or taste." Naam speculates that the training corpus contains far more incremental work than breakthroughs, which may make novelty hard to learn, and that swarms cannot fix this because of the homogeneity problem.

## The quantitative core: threshold versus measured loop strength

Naam's estimate leans on *The Economics of Recursive Self-Improvement* by Tom Cunningham and colleagues at the Elasticity Institute, which parameterizes the loop as **how much more research productivity each additional ECI point buys**. Two numbers matter:

1. **The self-sustaining threshold.** Their model finds roughly **15% more research productivity per ECI point** is where each cycle powers the next; above it the loop accelerates, below it each turn adds less than the last. Naam's re-fit with Stockfish data nudges the threshold to ~19%, a difference he says not to weight. The model isolates the software loop; outside investment can still drive rapid progress below the threshold.
2. **Today's loop strength.** Cunningham et al. estimate ~**9% per point**, derived from Anthropic's 4× survey uplift across ~16 ECI points since early Claude Code (assuming earlier tools added little), and they themselves warn the 4× is probably high. Naam recalibrates with OpenAI's logged data: 1.6× experiments per researcher over the same ~16 points works back to ~**3%**, and because tokens and experiment compute also grew, he adopts **2–3%** as a working assumption.

His reasons for preferring OpenAI's dataset: it is measured directly on infrastructure rather than self-reported, it covers every active experimenter rather than 130 opt-in respondents, and it likely contains tens to hundreds of thousands of experiments across 32 weeks. The result is a **five- to tenfold gap** between measured loop strength and the threshold, with the honest caveats that experiment counts may not capture research quality, the assumed capability change may be wrong, and the datasets are noisy (nobody knows which model researchers used on which day).

## What could change the picture

- **Other loops.** Davidson, Halperin, Houlden and Korinek's "singularities" paper models software, hardware and economic feedback together; several loops can combine to overcome diminishing returns where one cannot, and Naam calls it the most compelling integrated model he has seen. His objection is calibration: their central case puts *fully automated* software research roughly at the explosive threshold on its own, while the measured loop sits far below. Full autonomy removes the human bottleneck without removing diminishing returns in training, test-time compute or algorithm search (the Type 4 versus Type 5 distinction again). He also wants chip-design-to-deployment lag built in, and doubts how much past chip progress came from ideas versus ever-costlier fabs.
- **A transformer-scale breakthrough**, or better memory, training data and research judgment. Naam notes diminishing returns are as old as machine learning (Cortes et al. were fitting power-law scaling curves in 1993), so the default should be that they persist until a new approach demonstrably escapes them.
- **Better data.** Naam admits he may be over-weighting a few observations toward a comforting conclusion. Cheryl Wu welcomed OpenAI's disclosure while listing what is missing, and Wu, Arjun Ramani, Basil Halperin and colleagues at the Elasticity Institute published *How to Measure RSI*, eight things labs could share. The measurement Naam most wants: how much useful research each new model adds at roughly constant resources, and how that turns into capability.

## Brown's inside view (September 2026)

Brown is the o1 co-developer who now leads multi-agent work at OpenAI, and the interview is the most candid lab-side statement of the RSI question in this wiki. His positions, with Patel's counters:

**Math moved 10× a year and then jumped.** Brown's yardstick is how long a human mathematician would need for the problems a model can solve: GSM8K about five seconds, MATH about a minute, AIME about ten minutes, an IMO problem about a hundred minutes, each a year apart. Extrapolating gave fifteen hours for 2026, "not enough to solve a Millennium Prize Problem," so he expected one in 2028 at the earliest. It came in 2026. He stresses that surprise is the norm inside the labs too: IMO gold with a general-purpose model and no tools was considered "almost impossible" at OpenAI; two weeks before Navier–Stokes a frontier-lab researcher offered him $1,000 that no Millennium Problem would fall before 2028; and a member of the Navier–Stokes team who used to forecast twelve months out now declines to forecast beyond three. The models remain jagged, poor at posing problems and at choosing which branches of mathematics to develop, a point that matches Tao's account in [[ai-for-mathematics]]. Brown calls a world where AI complements rather than replaces mathematicians "the best-case scenario," then concedes that as models improve across the board the long tail of weaknesses shrinks.

**Patel's intuition pump, and Brown's acceptance of it.** Patel's argument is that ML research is structurally closer to the kind of math the models are now good at than to the kind they are bad at: nobody needs a new topology, only a well-scoped metric to move, and the 130 billion tokens spent on Navier–Stokes could be spent in a week on online learning or sample efficiency, more cognitive effort than the field has spent cumulatively. By the end of 2027, he estimates, OpenAI could give each of 10,000 much smarter agents a GPT-3-sized experiment per day. Brown calls the pump "pretty accurate": the models' spikiness is "particularly useful for things like RSI" because the objective is measurable.

**The experiment bottleneck, and a number.** The difference from mathematics is that ML needs experiments, run serially, on GPUs that are finite. Brown's thought experiment: OpenAI with the same people and 100× less compute would make "a lot less" progress, though not 100× less. So automated researchers speed things up "significantly," but not into an overnight 100× explosion. Pressed for a point estimate he gives **about 3× faster**, with a range from 50% to 10×, and insists 3× is already enormous: three years of progress in one, "going from not even having o1 to Astra in a single year." On full automation of AI research by 2027–2030 he will not guess: "I don't know what the world looks like in 2030."

**What internal acceleration already looks like.** OpenAI's internal-acceleration post showed the top 1% of researchers spending $7,000–8,000 a day on Codex by early August 2026, on an exponential. Brown gives two reasons the "what fraction is automated" question is ill-posed: attribution (a human directing agents) and jaggedness, which routes AI disproportionately to the things it does 100× better (checking every data point in a dataset), so that "how much faster than three years ago" and "how much slower would today's work have been" get different answers.

**A bottleneck Naam does not list: the curriculum.** AlphaZero had an infinite curriculum because self-play always supplies an equally strong opponent. RL on LLMs gives the model a problem and scores the answer, and a problem the model solves in a second teaches nothing. Brown names running out of hard enough problems as the plausible reason LLMs might not follow the AlphaGo trajectory from human-level to unimaginably superhuman within a year. He says the wall has not been hit and expects workarounds, but the dependency is real: a verifier-rich domain that is exhausted is no longer a training ground.

**Patel's "even if the rate simply continues" case.** Patel's own update, offered as the base case rather than the fast one: the current rate of algorithmic progress lets fixed compute run roughly a 3× larger effective population each year, and compute grows underneath, so each lab could run hundreds of millions of human-level agents by 2030 and "many Earths' worth" by the mid-2030s, probably qualitatively superhuman. Brown neither endorses nor rejects the figure; his reply is that progress is faster than even insiders expected and that he cannot see 2030. Patel's second worry, that a lab in the middle of RSI stops releasing models because the internal use is worth more than the external one, Brown grants as "absolutely right," with mathematics already the first domain where the capable model is internal only; that thread is in [[ai-alignment]].

## Garicano and Imas's production-function estimate (November 2026)

On 5 November 2026 METR published an independent assessment of AI R&D acceleration at Anthropic, built on Anthropic's internal data. Its headline: "as of Oct 2026, the pace of capability growth on well-specified tasks is likely about 2x what it was from Mar 2024 to Oct 2025," with 1.3× to 3× as the range METR "would not be surprised by." Many readers took the chart as the start of an intelligence explosion. Garicano and Imas answered a week later on Garicano's *Silicon Continent*. Their point is that METR plots capability against calendar time, and an explosion is a claim about returns to inputs: each unit of research effort must buy more proportional progress as capability grows. So they divide the progress by the inputs, then fit a model. Their answer is "not yet." On both models they fit, the lab paid for the acceleration.

The capability measure is AECI, Anthropic's adaptation of Epoch's capability index (the ECI of Naam's sections). METR could read it at the true internal frontier, including models that were never released.

### The accounting: faster progress, fewer points per dollar

The inputs come from Anthropic's S-1 (filed 26 October 2026, amended 2 November) and an Anthropic post on its own employment. The nine-month R&D figures are $3.594 billion for January–September 2025 and $19.240 billion for January–September 2026. Put at annual rates over the two periods of METR's trend, the comparison is:

| | Feb 2024 – early Oct 2025 | Early Oct 2025 – late Sep 2026 | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pace of AECI growth, points per year | 15.37 | 29.30 | 1.91× |
| R&D spending, annual rate | ~$3.55B | ~$22.00B | 6.19× |
| Technical employees (employee-years) | ~630 | ~1,960 | 3.10× |
| AECI points per $1B of R&D | 4.32 | 1.33 | 0.31× |
| AECI points per 1,000 technical employee-years | ~24.4 | ~15.0 | 0.62× |

Capability gains per dollar fell about 69%. Gains per technical employee-year fell about 38%. Spending per technical employee roughly doubled, which the authors read as more compute per worker as "AI researchers" come into use. The post's Figure 1 shows the index rising from about 125 in early 2024 to about 150 at October 2025 and about 178 by late September 2026, while R&D accumulated since January 2024 went from about $6 billion to about $27 billion. Plotted against that cumulative spending instead of time, the AECI line gets flatter after October 2025, not steeper. Naam and Seb Krier published similar charts. The authors stress that this step is accounting and shows nothing causal, and that "technical employees" is a wider group than frontier researchers.

This is the Bloom, Jones, Van Reenen and Webb exercise applied to AI. Their cases: Moore's law held its pace, but took 18 times the research effort in 2014 that it took in 1971; crop-yield growth per unit of research effort fell 4–6% a year from 1969 to 2009; drugs approved per unit of research effort fell fivefold from 1970 to 2014. Progress continued in all three. Each unit of it cost more.

### The model: is each advance harder than the last?

The accounting cannot separate two causes. One is **congestion**: a budget that grows more than fivefold in a year is spent less well. The other is that **ideas get harder to find**. With enough congestion, the same two numbers (6.2× spending, 1.9× pace) would even fit ideas getting easier. So the authors fit the research production function of Jones (1995):

`g_A = δ · X^λ · A^(φ−1)`

Here `A` is the stock of knowledge (proxied by the index), `g_A` its growth rate, `X` the research input, and `δ` research effectiveness. `λ` measures congestion: below one, doubling the input less than doubles new ideas. `φ` is the parameter that decides the explosion question. Below one, each advance makes the next harder, and with fixed inputs growth decays to zero. At exactly one, growth continues at a constant rate on fixed inputs. Above one, each advance makes the next easier and growth accelerates by itself.

Their results:

- **No change in the relationship is needed to get most of the acceleration.** Fitted to all sixteen frontier observations with `δ` held fixed, the model produces an acceleration of about 1.8× against the observed 1.9×. Figure 3 gives the detail: 1.85× with R&D dollars as the input and 1.81× with compute plus staff, and an in-sample test for a break after October 2025 on the R&D series returns p = 0.23, which does not reject "no break."
- **`φ` is below one in almost every specification.** The data cannot identify `λ` and `φ` separately, so the authors report `φ` for every assumed level of congestion. The central value is 0.84, and the range is about 0.66 to 0.94 across four measures of input (R&D dollars, dollars lagged six months, compute plus technical staff, staff alone). `φ` reaches one only if congestion is severe, with `λ` below about 0.3 to 0.45.
- **The central value is conditional on an assumed `λ`.** The post's text does not say so, but Figure 3 does: the 0.84 dot sits at `λ` = 0.75 on the compute-plus-staff line. At that value, doubling the input buys about 68% more new knowledge. The figure labels the two axes "stepping on toes" (`λ`) and "standing on shoulders" (`φ`). With no congestion at all (`λ` = 1) the four lines read about 0.66 for R&D dollars, 0.72 for lagged dollars, 0.75 for compute plus staff and 0.83 for staff alone. All but the first are read off the chart and are approximate. The lines cross one at `λ` of roughly 0.30, 0.33, 0.36 and 0.45 in the same order.
- **A break in October 2025 looks like a one-time step.** Fitted on the six observations before October 2025 and fed the inputs that followed, the model under-predicts: the input surge explains at most half of the acceleration. A model with a break puts a one-time step of 1.4× to 2.3× on `δ`. Estimated separately, the two periods show similar `φ`, so the rate of diminishing returns did not change. The authors suggest the arrival of coding agents as the kind of event that gives a step like this. They call fitting three parameters to six observations "a bit heroic."

### The loop term: is AI supplying its own research input?

The Jones model has no AI in it. Aghion, Jones and Jones add it by letting effective research input grow with capability raised to a power `ψ`. Progress then explodes when `λψ + φ > 1`, which can happen while ideas are getting harder to find. The authors use a per-doubling reading of this condition that they credit to Jacob Schaal. Each doubling of R&D spending has bought two to three doublings of METR's task horizon (about five index points double the horizon). Falling returns per dollar and steady returns per doubling are the same fact, because each doubling costs twice the dollars of the last. The loop takes off when one doubling of capability supplies enough new research input to buy more than one further doubling.

| | `ψ` | Extra research input per horizon doubling |
|---|---|---|
| Estimated from the data | 0.01–0.04 | 1–3% |
| Needed for an explosion, `(1 − φ)/λ` | 0.34–0.46 | 26–38% |
| Needed if compute is fixed and only labor scales | 0.8–1.0 | |

The estimate comes from comparing the two periods. If AI were already adding research input that no budget line pays for, returns per dollar would have weakened less after October 2025. They did not. The authors say the error bands are wide. They also cite Tyler John's reading of METR's staff interviews: AI tools removed implementation friction but did not supply new ideas. That raises the level of capability. Holding the current pace still needs inputs to grow about fourfold a year.

The authors state the limit of the test. The data come from a period in which research inputs were bought. They can show whether the feedback has begun. They say little about its size once AI does most of the research, which depends on how much input AI can supply and on whether compute becomes the bottleneck.

### Why falling returns do not stop the spending

Garicano offers an organizational mechanism from his 2012 paper with Esteban Rossi-Hansberg, "Organizing Growth." A new technology creates new problems. People first learn the common ones, then organizations build specialists for the rarer ones. Each step makes the technology more productive, but fewer and fewer problems are left to need the extra expertise, so the gains shrink. Growth then needs a new technology and a new cycle. On this account, falling research productivity alongside rising investment is an equilibrium and not a mistake, as long as demand for better systems pays for the effort. Garicano's knowledge hierarchies, the model family this paper belongs to, come up in [[ai-and-the-theory-of-the-firm]], and his supply-side case on jobs is in [[messy-jobs]].

Falling capability per dollar is also not falling money per dollar. The authors quote Ben Todd's illustration: a Claude model at index 130 might earn about $0.3 billion a year, one at 150 about $10 billion, and one at 170 about $60 billion. On those numbers revenue rose about 9% per index point between 150 and 170, while the spending needed to hold the pace rises 5–7% per point, so reinvested revenue more than covers the rising cost. The authors add two cautions. Revenue also reflects diffusion, marketing and competition: OpenAI's revenue stayed mostly flat over a period of similar index gains before a recent acceleration. And a benchmark of economic capability may be the better output measure; they report an almost perfectly linear relation between AECI and GDPval scores for released models.

### The authors' own warning

A reader could have drawn the same charts for mathematics in the summer of 2026, concluded that nothing was accelerating, and then been surprised by the burst that began in September and October. "Nothing here allows us to rule out a huge burst in improvement as recursive self-improvement takes place." This is the trend break Brown describes above from the inside.

## Synthesis

**Two methods, two labs, the same order-of-magnitude gap.** Naam's estimate uses OpenAI's experiment logs and Cunningham's threshold: a measured 2–3% against a required 15–19% per index point. Garicano and Imas use Anthropic's S-1 and AECI: a measured 1–3% against a required 26–38% per horizon doubling. The two parameterizations are not the same quantity (research productivity per researcher in one, research input supplied per unit of capability in the other), so the levels should not be compared. The ratios can be. Naam's is a reproduction number of roughly 0.1–0.2. Garicano and Imas's `ψ` divided by its threshold is roughly 0.02–0.12. Both say the loop is around a tenth of self-sustaining strength or less, and they reach that from different data. The agreement is not fully independent: both lean on the Epoch index family and on Bloom et al., and Naam's charts are cited in the post.

**The threshold is the bill, and the question is who pays it.** One identity in the post is easy to miss. The spending rise "needed to hold the pace" (5–7% per index point) and the explosion threshold (26–38% per horizon doubling) are the same number, because five points make one doubling and both equal `(1 − φ)/λ`. So the threshold is the rate at which research input must grow per step of capability just to keep the pace constant. Today the lab pays that bill in dollars, at about 4× a year. An explosion is the case where the model pays it. Todd's revenue arithmetic shows a third payer: at 9% more revenue per point against 5–7% more cost per point, customers can pay it. That is the economic loop of Davidson, Halperin, Houlden and Korinek (the paper Naam discusses above, cited in the post under its NBER title), and on Todd's illustrative numbers it is the only loop currently above its threshold. It is also the loop with outside limits: chips, power, and the rest of the economy's willingness to pay, which is Cowen's which-sectors-pay question in [[ai-explosive-growth]].

**Fourfold a year sets a clock.** This arithmetic is the wiki's, not the authors'. From an annual rate of about $22 billion, 4× growth is roughly $88 billion after one year, $350 billion after two and $1.4 trillion after three, at one lab. Naam's figure for all AI infrastructure is about 3% of US GDP, which is under $1 trillion. So input growth alone cannot hold the post-October 2025 pace for more than two or three years. Either the pace falls back toward what capital, chips and power allow, or `ψ` rises. Naam predicted the first from the investment side. The thing to watch is whether capability "pulls away from spending" as budget growth slows.

**What the METR report changes in Naam's reading.** Naam, writing in September 2026, described a linear public frontier and read Anthropic's AECI break as a level jump with no rising rate. METR's internal-frontier data says the rate did rise, by about 2× in calendar time: 15.4 index points a year before October 2025, which matches Naam's 16 for the public frontier, and 29.3 after. Garicano and Imas keep Naam's conclusion and change the description: about half of the faster pace was bought with inputs, and the rest is a one-time step in research effectiveness with unchanged diminishing returns. A step in `δ` raises the growth rate at given inputs, so it shows as a slope change in calendar time, and with `φ` below one it fades unless inputs keep rising. This extends the "constant rate is an equilibrium" point below. A constant frontier rate is not a reading of loop strength, and neither is an accelerating one. Only the input-adjusted relationship is.

**Where the Garicano and Imas estimate is weakest.** It rests on sixteen observations from one lab, with six before the break. `λ` and `φ` are not separately identified, so "ideas are getting harder" depends on ruling out severe congestion, and a sixfold budget rise in one year is exactly when congestion is plausible. The post never measures congestion directly; the headline 0.84 assumes `λ` = 0.75, and the argument is that the alternative needs an extreme value. In plain terms, for ideas to be no harder, the whole 69% fall in progress per dollar would have to be waste. The two break tests also point in different directions: the in-sample test does not reject "no break" (p = 0.23), while the out-of-sample forecast from six early observations says a break is needed. `φ` also depends on how the index is converted into a stock of knowledge; the authors flag that their uncertainty band is conditional on that conversion. The test for `ψ` only sees research input that no budget line pays for. An AI researcher runs on compute, and compute is inside R&D spending, so AI substituting for human researchers shows up as a higher `X` (spending per technical employee doubled), not as `ψ`. The 0.8–1.0 threshold for labor with compute fixed is the same bottleneck Brown describes: experiments run serially on finite GPUs. The figures cited to the online appendix were taken from the post's text; the appendix itself was not ingested. The authors note that the analysis does not represent their employers' views.

**The parameter Imas named two days earlier.** In the a16z conversation recorded on 11 November ([[ai-explosive-growth]]), Imas called the returns-to-ideas parameter "decidedly less than one." This post is the estimate behind that sentence: `φ` of about 0.84. It also refines Assumption 5 of the Moll and Imas checklist. There, the first route to an R&D explosion needs researchers fully replaced and ideas getting easier. Here the condition is weaker and measurable: `λψ + φ > 1`, which can hold with ideas getting harder if AI supplies enough input. The straight-line fit under constant input growth is the power-law reading of [[scaling-hypothesis]] and [[the-bitter-lesson]], restated in research dollars instead of training compute.

**Three bear cases, three different targets.** The wiki now holds three skeptical positions on runaway AI, and they attack different links in the chain. Karpathy ([[agi-timelines]]) argues from history that no technology produces a regime change in GDP. Moll and Imas ([[ai-explosive-growth]]) grant a capabilities explosion and argue it will not become an economic one. Naam argues the capabilities explosion itself is not showing up in the data. His is the narrowest and the most falsifiable: he takes the pro-RSI model on its own terms and disputes one coefficient. Garicano and Imas attack the same link as Naam, so they are a second measurement of his case and not a fourth position. Naam and Moll–Imas both lean on Bloom et al.'s falling research productivity, so "ideas get harder to find" is the shared spine of the economic skeptics' view.

**A constant rate is an equilibrium, not a reading of loop strength.** The essay's most important inference is easy to miss. A public ECI frontier growing at a steady 16 points a year is compatible with a weak loop, but also with a strong loop being exactly cancelled by rising difficulty and slowing input growth, which is roughly what Anthropic's "maintaining" sentence describes. That is why Naam cannot rest on the trend chart and has to estimate productivity-per-ECI-point directly, and it means the thing to watch is not the frontier slope but that coefficient. The threshold framing has the shape of a reproduction number: 2–3% against 15–19% is an R of roughly 0.1–0.2, and revisions to that estimate matter more than any single model release.

**The macro numbers and the micro bottlenecks agree.** Weng's seven bottlenecks to full RSI in [[self-improving-harnesses]] are the mechanism-level version of Naam's aggregate observations: diversity collapse in evolutionary loops is the agent-swarm homogeneity finding, weak and fuzzy evaluators are why superintelligence stays confined to verifiable domains, and the missing research taste that METR flags is what [[ml-research-craft]] treats as a trainable sub-skill. If taste is trainable, that is the channel through which Naam's coefficient could move.

**The 2015 and 2026 essays bracket a decade, and the argument survived while the premises changed.** In 2015 Naam's bear case had two legs: nobody has an incentive to build general AI, and if they did the loop would face diminishing returns. The first leg broke. The incentive turned out to be the commercial payoff of scaling, which [[scaling-hypothesis]] documents as a bet almost nobody was willing to make in 2020 and everybody made after, and by 2026 the labs name RSI as their top priority. The second leg is the whole of the 2026 essay: the N² intuition became a measured coefficient (2–3% against a 15–19% threshold), the Intel analogy became Cunningham et al.'s loop-strength model, and "diminishing returns are as old as machine learning" is the 2015 chemistry analogy with better data. The 2015 essay also expected progress to come from search engines and assistants rather than systems that think, which is roughly what happened, except that the assistants turned out to be general enough that the semblance-versus-sentience line he drew is now the dispute in [[ai-consciousness-and-moral-status]]. His uploading section is the best short case in the vault against the whole-brain-emulation path that [[superintelligence]] presents as the thorough version of "plagiarize the brain"; a decade later it remains the road not taken.

**Insider and skeptic agree on structure and differ by a factor of two or three.** Brown's 3× and Naam's 2–3% per ECI point are not the same quantity, but they can be compared. Brown's number is the total internal speedup from AI, which in Naam's taxonomy is Types 1 and 2 at full strength; Naam's Anthropic test in [[multi-agent-scaling]] predicts a 1.1–1.8× pace from the sub-log benchmark curves and about 2× from logarithmic scaling, against 4× for Davidson–Houlden's 0.6 exponent and 25× for Chan et al. Brown's gut sits between the log case and Davidson–Houlden, well below the labor exponents Naam calls implausible, and he arrives there by the same reasoning: experiments run serially on finite GPUs, which is what Davidson and Houlden's compute-bound 0.3 exponent encodes. The important point is what a 3× faster exponential is not. It compresses time; it is not accelerating acceleration, and Naam's threshold is about the latter. Brown's phrase "the exponential is 3× faster" describes a steeper constant rate, which is the equilibrium reading in the second synthesis point above, not a loop above threshold.

**Math is the leading indicator, and it already broke its own trend.** Brown's 10×-a-year yardstick under-predicted Navier–Stokes by about two years, which is the strongest single piece of evidence in this article against anchoring on measured coefficients, and it is on Patel's side of the argument. The limit of that evidence is the thing Brown says himself: mathematics is "purely bottlenecked by thinking," and the trend break happened in the one domain where that is true. The experiment bottleneck is precisely where a math-trend extrapolation stops applying. Naam's measured 15-minute research horizon and Brown's 3× are two descriptions of the same bottleneck from different vantage points.

**The curriculum is a diminishing-returns mechanism on the training side.** Naam's list (ideas get harder to find, compute returns diminish, novelty is rare in the corpus) is about research output. Brown's curriculum worry is about the input: RL on verifiable problems consumes its own supply of hard problems, so the verifier-rich domains where narrow superintelligence appears first are also the domains that exhaust first. That is a fourth reason, alongside Naam's three, that the loop would need a conceptual breakthrough, an infinite curriculum for open-ended research as self-play was for Go, rather than more of the same.

**Where the argument is weakest.** Experiments per researcher is a poor proxy for research output if better models change experiment quality, which Naam concedes; the 1.6× and the 16 ECI points are stitched together from different populations and periods; and the horizon comparison sets OpenAI's internal research tasks against METR's software tasks, so some of the 16–44× gap is task difficulty rather than benchmark inflation. His critique of Davidson et al. is about calibration rather than structure, so a reader who accepts the multi-loop model but doubts the software calibration lands close to Naam anyway. Brown's 3× is a stated gut estimate with no dataset behind it, from a capabilities lead at the lab doing the work, hedged from 50% to 10× in the same breath; it is the best-informed guess in this article, not a measurement.

**Downstream uses.** [[commodity-trap]] rules out the hard-takeoff-produces-a-monopoly route as something nobody can plan around; Naam supplies the evidence that it is not imminent either. [[labor-share-under-automation]] names RSI and continual learning as the technical questions that decide whether concentration runs away, and [[pacing-the-frontier]] records that Amodei's actual worry is RSI escaping human control. Naam's essay is the current best estimate of how far that worry sits from the data, and the Elasticity Institute's eight disclosures are the list of what would move the estimate. Deutsch's point in [[beginning-of-infinity]] that copying minds is not free is the older, philosophical version of Ord's swarm-cost result.

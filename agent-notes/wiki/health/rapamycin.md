# Rapamycin

Rapamycin (clinical name **sirolimus**; brand **Rapamune**) is a small molecule discovered on Easter Island (Rapa Nui, hence the name), FDA-approved >20 years ago as an organ-transplant anti-rejection drug. It inhibits **mTOR**, the same nutrient-sensing pathway targeted by caloric restriction and, more weakly, by [[metformin]]. In geroscience it is the leading candidate for a drug that targets the biology of aging itself. This article compiles Matt Kaeberlein's July 2026 "Is Rapamycin Dead?" lecture, a point-by-point rebuttal to the recent backlash against rapamycin as a longevity intervention. Kaeberlein is a rapamycin researcher, so the framing is advocacy — but grounded in the primary literature and careful about the evidence hierarchy.

## The core claim: most robust longevity drug in animals

Kaeberlein's headline argument is that rapamycin is the **most robust and reproducible** lifespan-extending drug we have (dozens of papers, dozens of labs), a status it has held ~15 years. It extends mouse lifespan up to ~30%, and — the 2009 Interventions Testing Program paradigm shift — it works when started at **middle age** (20-month mice, ~65-human-equivalent), overturning the prior belief that anti-aging interventions had to start young. This is what makes it translationally appealing: you can start treatment late in life.

Crucial caveat he repeats: rapamycin does **not reverse aging** (does not make an old animal young — nothing does). What it does is **restore function** in specific tissues, documented in mice for at least five systems: brain, heart, immune system, ovary, and oral cavity.

## Function restoration in mice (the illustrative data)

- **Heart** (Rabinovich lab, UW; replicated by ≥2 labs): 10 weeks of rapamycin in 20-month mice largely reversed left-ventricular hypertrophy and fully restored E/A ratio (LV function) to young levels.
- **Immune** (Pan Zheng lab, Michigan, 2009): aged mice respond to a flu vaccine only ~1/3 of the time (2/3 die on lethal challenge). 6 weeks of rapamycin before vaccination → 100% survival. Kaeberlein's favorite rapamycin experiment.
- **Oral** (Jonathan An, Kaeberlein lab): rapamycin reversed all three defining features of periodontal disease (gingival inflammation, bone loss, microbiome remodeling) in 8 weeks, with imaged regrowth of bone around teeth.
- **Short-term → lifespan** (Bitto): just 12 weeks of rapamycin in 20-month mice raised remaining life expectancy >60%; the absolute extension (141 days) exceeded the treatment window (90 days), implying restoration of function rather than mere pausing of aging.

## The "70% lifespan" fallacy and the 900-day rule

A methodological aside with broad utility: huge reported percentage lifespan increases almost always come from **short-lived controls**, not a great intervention — the drug merely restores lifespan to where healthy controls should have been. Kaeberlein and Kennedy's **900-day rule**: quality mouse experiments have controls living ~900 days; be skeptical below 800. The only intervention that beats rapamycin's absolute median lifespan is the classic Weindruch & Walford caloric restriction experiment. This is a concrete tool for reading longevity papers, complementing the evidence-vs-hype themes in [[longevity-science]].

## Dogs as the intermediate

Companion dogs live in the real (environmentally diverse) world and have intermediate lifespans, addressing the "mice are too protected" critique. The **Dog Aging Project's TRIAD** trial (Test of Rapamycin in Aging Dogs) is a phase-3-equivalent, 580-dog, placebo-controlled RCT powered for lifespan (1yr treatment + 2yr follow-up), ~half enrolled as of mid-2026. Two prior safety trials found no serious adverse events plus positive echocardiogram changes mirroring the mouse heart data. Notably, **Trivium Vet's veterinary rapamycin got conditional FDA approval for feline hypertrophic cardiomyopathy** — Kaeberlein argues this is the first FDA approval of a gerotherapeutic.

## Human evidence

No large RCTs exist — there's no commercial incentive to fund them (rapamycin is off-patent). Evidence is off-label N-of-1 use plus small underpowered "safety" trials. Human off-label norm is **once-weekly dosing, most commonly 6 mg/week** (vs daily multi-mg in transplant), a convention largely set by physician Alan Green, the first to advertise off-label prescribing (died 2024 at 81 of HCM complications — a reminder rapamycin is no miracle cure).

**Safety at once-weekly dosing:** the only side effect reaching statistical significance is **mouth sores (~15%)**, usually mild. No significant population-level effect on lipids, fasting glucose, insulin sensitivity, or infection rates — the feared transplant side effects (see below) do not appear at these doses.

Signals of benefit, each mirroring the mouse tissue data:
- **Immune:** Joan Mannick's phase-2 trials (using everolimus, a biochemically-equivalent rapalog) improved flu-vaccine antibody titers and reduced respiratory infections; 5 mg/week had placebo-level side effects with full efficacy. A small daily-rapamycin RCT reduced DNA-damage, immune-exhaustion, and P21 (senescence) markers.
- **Viral/COVID:** in Kaeberlein's 333-user off-label survey (2023), rapamycin didn't reduce COVID *risk* but reduced *severity* and long-COVID-like symptoms.
- **Brain:** two small APOE4-carrier studies (1 mg/day, ~4 weeks) increased cerebral blood flow (matching Galvan's rodent data), one showing increased hippocampal/caudate volume by MRI. Kaeberlein would seriously consider rapamycin as a symptomatic APOE4 carrier.
- **Ovary:** an IVF study raised clinical pregnancy rate; the VIBRANT trial (premature ovarian failure, 5 mg/week) has positive-looking preliminary data.
- **Oral:** case reports and an ongoing trial (John An) suggest improved periodontal health.
- **Chronic fatigue / long COVID:** off-label ME/CFS study (86 patients, 6 mg/week, 3 months): ~3/4 of completers improved (fatigue, post-exertional malaise, orthostatic intolerance), with responders greatly enriched for **post-viral** CFS (usually Epstein-Barr). Long COVID framed as a post-viral CFS variant. The recurring theme is rapamycin blunting age-related sterile inflammation ("inflammaging").

## The muscle controversy (the misread study)

The Brad Stanfield **Rapa EX01** trial (which Kaeberlein co-authored) is the study most cited as evidence *against* rapamycin. It put sedentary healthy 65–85-year-olds on an exercise regimen for 13 weeks. It missed significance on intent-to-treat (close; sensitivity analysis reached it). Kaeberlein's read: both groups improved but placebo probably improved *more* — rapamycin attenuated the **"newbie gains"** from starting exercise, exactly as expected since mTOR drives the anabolic adaptation phase. This says nothing about long-term effects or biological aging; he wanted a washout arm to see if the rapamycin group caught up. By contrast the year-long **PEARL** trial (undermined by uncoated compounded rapamycin delivering ~half the target dose) showed a real-looking lean-mass *gain* in women at the higher dose — suggesting long-term muscle *preservation*. Muscle is the one area where Kaeberlein holds low confidence.

## Transplant vs longevity dosing

Much of rapamycin's bad reputation comes from the **high daily transplant dose** in already-sick, co-immunosuppressed patients: black-box warning, severe aphthous ulcers, high triglycerides, glucose dysregulation ("pseudo-diabetes"), and immune suppression. Kaeberlein's central pharmacological point is that **dose matters** — these effects largely vanish at once-weekly longevity dosing in healthy people. Unlike rapamycin, [[metformin]] inhibits mTOR indirectly (via AMPK) without the immunosuppressive profile.

## Open problems

**Optimal dosing is the big unknown** — likely indication-dependent (different tissue distribution and required degree of mTOR inhibition for HCM vs periodontal vs ovarian vs Alzheimer's). There are no good biomarkers yet to personalize dose or confirm the drug is working, so off-label use involves a lot of guessing. Inflammatory markers are a candidate.

## Verdict

Kaeberlein concludes rapamycin is "more alive than ever" — the strongest current candidate for a human aging intervention, held back only by the absence of high-quality trials. He cites Steve Austad picking rapamycin over metformin for a hypothetical $100M trial and calling mTOR "something we've really hit on," while predicting NAD drugs may be the field's embarrassment in 10 years. This ranking is consistent with the gerotherapeutic ranking in [[longevity-science]], where rapamycin leads on human evidence (though de Grey demotes it as a caloric-restriction mimetic capped at ~2 years). Upcoming trials: University of Arizona (PI Bonnie LaFleur, funded by Ken Coit) and the ARPA-H PROSPER program.

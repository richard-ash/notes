# Technology in the 2020s: Dourado's forecast and a mid-decade scorecard

Eli Dourado, then at the Center for Growth and Opportunity and formerly of Boom Supersonic, published a sector-by-sector forecast of the decade on 31 December 2020. His frame: the Great Stagnation ends only with sustained growth in utilization-adjusted total factor productivity (TFP) of about 2% a year, the 1947–72 average (2.1%), against 0.17% since 2005. His conviction: "scientific breakthroughs alone are not enough." TFP moves when technologies are adopted at scale, which means products, businesses, regulatory fights and manufacturing, not papers. He closes: "Our culture does not prioritize progress—it fights, destructively, for status... I want to go faster." Because the essay is a dated list of calls, this article scores it as of October 2026; the rows were checked against web sources on 2026-10-21.

## The forecast by sector

**Biotech and health.** The mRNA vaccines were designed by January 13, 2020 ("we had it the whole time"), so what else do we have the whole time? He flags Moderna's HIV candidate and both companies' personalized cancer vaccines. CRISPR, discovered 2012 and Nobel-awarded 2020, had made no economic contribution yet; treatments for otherwise untreatable genetic disorders would come. AlphaFold showed that "machine learning is actually useful" ("GPT-3 produced some fun demos") but drug timelines push the public benefit into the 2030s. The Conboy lab's 2020 result that young blood works by *dilution* of old plasma made therapeutic plasma exchange the first plausible anti-aging product: FDA-approved for other uses, prescribable off-label, under $3,000 for the machine; "I bet someone tries it in 2021... it could be commonplace by 2030." Aging clocks would get cheap, self-experimenters would replicate the Fahy thymus protocol, a few senolytics would be approved, wearables would add ten sensors and replace much of primary care. He is a metformin skeptic (it helps the 88% who are metabolically unhealthy) and warns that "biology is proceeding faster than medical productivity."

**Energy.** Wind and solar cost declines would stall (already cheap; not reproducible) while deployment accelerated; intermittency, storage at $542/kWh (Tesla Powerpack) against a $20/kWh target, rising demand from EVs, neglected transmission and political pressure to retire fossil plants would make the 2020s "a time of electric grid instability." Nuclear is too expensive (NuScale targeting 6.5¢/kWh and already slipped to 2030). The plausible baseload is enhanced geothermal from oil-and-gas refugees: 3.5¢/kWh now, 2¢ with modest drilling advances, hundreds of gigawatts by 2030, permitting reform required. Fusion gets a demonstration this decade and no grid share for decades, and will struggle against geothermal wells that run at under a cent once the drilling is paid off. Aviation decarbonizes through sustainable fuel made from atmospheric CO₂ (Prometheus Fuels), not batteries.

**Transportation.** EVs win because they are better, suddenly; California's 2035 rule will be a non-issue by then; he is a battery-price pessimist because demand will swamp supply. Hydrogen suits heavy trucking. Ultrafine-particle health gains will "appear out of nowhere." Autonomy at scale is "inevitable" within the decade (Waymo already driverless in Phoenix). Supersonics (Boom's XB-1, Aerion if it can fund a factory, Hermeus at Mach 5, NASA's X-59 from 2023) will not matter before 2030. Urban air mobility (Joby, Wisk) could enter service in the first half of the decade if the FAA allows autonomy. Drone delivery will expand by incremental waivers. The Boring Company's Las Vegas loop and a DC–Baltimore loop are worth following via the gravity model of trade.

**Space.** Starship is "the big story." Shuttle cost $65,400/kg to low-earth orbit, Falcon 9 $2,600, Starship's target $10 (full reusability, methane, stainless steel, $5M per vehicle, one every 72 hours), and orbital refueling makes 150 tons to orbit 150 tons to anywhere. The gravity model implies a 200× cost cut yields 200× more Earth–orbit commerce, and cheap launch ends payload over-engineering. Starlink (955 satellites then, up to 42,000) could take 3% of a $2.4T telecom market, $72B a year, and fund Mars: his bet with Robin Hanson is a human on Mars by the end of Q1 2030. Not choosing Starship for Artemis would be "a colossal error"; a permanent moon base by 2030 is "highly plausible." Varda's in-space manufacturing is real; asteroid mining is 2030s at the earliest.

**Information technology.** Custom silicon everywhere (Apple's SoC, Cerebras, Tesla's FSD chip), with a note that North America cannot manufacture it. Crypto's make-or-break decade: it needs scale, UX and normal people transacting. AR glasses "widely deployed, in the same way that smart watches are today" by mid-decade.

**Miscellaneous.** An "irrational love" of vertical farming; plant-based meat will flop, lab-grown meat is worth watching; 3D-printed and manufactured housing (ICON, Cover, Modal) as a lever beside zoning.

## What the scorecard says about forecasting technology

- **Direction was mostly right; timing was wrong wherever certification sits in the path.** EVs, autonomy, CRISPR, custom silicon and Starlink followed visible S-curves. Urban air mobility, supersonics, Mars and the moon base are gated by regulators and flight tests, and all slipped, which is the base-rate lesson of [[reference-class-forecasting]] and the demos-mislead argument of [[ai-robotics-hype-cycles]].
- **The biggest thing was the biggest miss.** Forecasts enumerate visible pipelines; the technology that defined the decade was "fun demos" in December 2020. Gwern's [[scaling-hypothesis]], published seven months earlier, was the contemporaneous view that got it right.
- **Betting against learning curves lost.** Batteries and solar modules fell in price through a demand surge, because manufacturing scale drives cost down faster than demand drives it up.
- **His own thesis held.** The gap between CRISPR's 2020 Nobel and one approved therapy in 2023, and between AlphaFold's 2024 Nobel and zero approved drugs, is "science is not TFP" demonstrated; [[pharma-industry-economics]] has the trial-cost mechanics.
- **"I want to go faster" became a movement.** The permitting and state-capacity agenda in [[abundance-agenda]] and [[public-problem-solving-capital]] is the political form of this essay's closing complaint.
- **The energy section connects to the AI build-out.** His grid-instability worry materialized through a demand source he did not list, data centers, and his geothermal and nuclear calls are now being settled by hyperscaler offtake contracts rather than by utilities ([[ai-capex-required-returns]]).

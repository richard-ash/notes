# Technological revolutions: installation and deployment

Jerry Neumann's October 2015 talk at Percolate's Transition conference, written up on Reaction Wheel as "The Deployment Age," is a compact exposition of Carlota Perez's *Technological Revolutions and Financial Capital* (2002) followed by a forecast: the information and communications technology (ICT) revolution that began around 1971 has crossed from its **installation period** into its **deployment period**, and most of what the tech industry learned over the previous thirty years is about to stop being true. Neumann set the test himself: "if it successfully predicts what happens over the next ten years it will have in good part proved its power." That window closed in October 2025, so this article carries a scorecard. The framework is also the backdrop for [[asset-light-software]], whose authors use Perez to argue that ChatGPT restarted the cycle.

## Five surges and a shared shape

Neumann follows Perez in counting four completed surges in 240 years and the first half of a fifth: the Industrial Revolution (mechanized textiles, water power, canals); railways and steam; steel; oil, autos and mass production (early 1900s to the 1970s); and ICT from roughly 1971. He recommends Freeman and Louçã's *As Time Goes By* as the tighter, better-cited account of the same waves.

Each surge, in Perez's telling, runs through the same sequence:

- some critical factor of production suddenly becomes very cheap;
- new infrastructure is built;
- a laissez-faire period of wrenching innovation ends in a bubble;
- a post-bubble recession;
- a re-assertion of institutional authority;
- consolidation and the wide spread of productivity gains.

The repetition across dissimilar eras is what Neumann finds hard to dismiss as coincidence, and Perez's contribution is a mechanism: the interplay of technological, economic and *institutional* change, with finance as the pivot.

## Technological systems and the reverse salient

A revolution starts from a **technological system**, a set of technologies that interlock into a platform for further innovation (Neumann borrows Donella Meadows's definition of a system; see [[leverage-points]]). The railroad needed high-pressure steam engines, precision machine parts and castable rails, but also limited liability and stock markets to concentrate the capital. A plain system becomes a revolution when it addresses a broad, basic need and so reshapes the whole economy: the railroad was built to move coal to piers and ended up moving goods, mail and people between cities, which let people live away from their hometowns for the first time. The microprocessor, built for calculators, played the same role for ICT.

Neumann's most reusable sidenote is Thomas Hughes's **reverse salient**: within a linked system the lagging technology becomes the binding constraint, resources are redirected to it, and the system advances faster than any component would alone. "If you see a reverse salient, you have found a problem whose solution is far more valuable than it may first seem." His 2015 nomination was battery technology.

## Installation: irruption, frenzy, crash

Once the new system promises commercial opportunity, a **techno-economic paradigm** (TEP) appears: a way of using the new technology to run businesses more efficiently than incumbents, who have everything invested in the old paradigm and resist. Financiers notice; exploratory investing mostly fails but a few hits (Neumann's examples are the young venture industry's bets on Apple and Intel) draw in more money exponentially, producing the frenzy. Incumbents react operationally with piecemeal adoption and strategically by imitating financial capital behind a firewall: corporate venture arms, skunkworks, innovation divisions, "intrapreneurship." The frenzy overinvests, the bubble pops, the recession follows. Then institutions, which could not assert themselves while nothing seemed broken, write rules against the excesses: the Securities Acts of 1933 and 1934 after 1929; Sarbanes-Oxley after the dot-com crash, then Dodd-Frank after the housing bubble (Perez treats the two as one frenzy). Financiers learn humility and a permanent fear of the next bubble.

## Financial capital versus production capital

This distinction carries Perez's explanation, and Neumann admits it took him a while to understand it. His two cartoons: the MBA who says "Things? I didn't come here to learn how to make things, I came here to learn how to make money," and Drucker's "Securities analysts believe that companies make money. Companies make shoes."

| | Financial capital | Production capital |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who controls it | Financiers, external to production | Company management |
| Why it funds innovation | To earn a return on disruption | To improve its own means of production |
| Mentality | Casino: nine losers and one 12× is a good year | Planning: a failed investment is a management failure, "almost a moral failing" |
| Kind of innovation | Exploratory, market-creating | Sustaining, predictable |

When financial capital pulls back after the crash, production capital takes over. The incumbents are not trying to create new markets or disrupt anyone, since they *are* the incumbents. The result is Perez's synergy period: less technological volatility, fewer business failures, more and longer-lasting employment, less income inequality, and less corporate dynamism (the fifty largest companies entering the 1950s were much the same ones leaving them). Because production capital avoids the radical innovations that future innovation is built on, the opportunity space eventually runs dry, stagnation sets in (US manufacturing in the 1970s), and savvy financiers go looking for the next technological system. Institutions follow the money with a lag: "In 1953 the establishment believed that what was good for GM was good for America; it's not a stretch to say that over the next 20 years the establishment will begin to believe, and act, like what's good for Google, Facebook, and Apple is good for America."

## What deployment looks like

Neumann lists four marks of the deployment period:

1. **Cheap, overbuilt infrastructure.** The bubble overbuilds canals, railways, telecom; deployment inherits the glut at low cost.
2. **Push becomes pull.** In installation, customers must be sold on each new thing; in deployment, customers demand the technology and are often ahead of vendors on how they will use it.
3. **Expansion over creation.** Companies grow by widening existing markets, which forces products to become cheaper and easier to use.
4. **Diffusion of the gains.** The technology becomes familiar and repairable, so the returns to technical knowledge spread from a small elite across the population, reducing inequality.

His worked example is the 1950s, the first half of deployment for the oil-and-autos wave, drawn from Alexander Field's *A Great Leap Forward* (Neumann writes "Robert Field"). The enumerations of basic innovations by Kleinknecht, Schmookler and Mensch all peak in 1935–39 and dwindle to zero by 1955–59; product innovation vanished while process innovation continued, which is what you would expect when firms expand markets by making things cheaper rather than inventing new ones. Productivity growth shifted from manufacturing to distribution, driven less by the interstates than by containerization, which let old assets (ships, railways) use new ones (trucks, highways). Innovation moved inside incumbents or was absorbed by them: Shockley Semiconductor as a division of Beckman Instruments; the Eckert-Mauchly Computer Corporation, first to sell a computer, forced to sell itself to Remington Rand in 1950 for want of financial capital; ARD taking 70% of DEC for $70,000 in 1957 because Ken Olsen had nowhere else to go. Galbraith's technostructure is the sociology of this period, and Neumann's sharpest observation is that executives kept wearing the entrepreneur's costume long after the climate had changed: "The turn away from innovation was not because innovation was less revered... it was because in a corporate world safe from the threat of upstarts, transformative innovation was economically unsound." The same decades, told from the household's side, are Housel's postwar narrative in [[postwar-us-economy]].

## Neumann's ten-year forecast

Citing Perez's 2013 paper placing ICT in deployment, Neumann draws out the implication that his own audience would not want to hear: financial capital's job is done, and the venture capitalist, who *is* this cycle's financial capital, is becoming irrelevant. He finds it odd that Fred Wilson, Chris Dixon and Marc Andreessen champion a theory that says so. Two themes follow:

1. **ICT becomes ubiquitous but invisible.** "There won't be online stores, there will just be stores; there won't be mobile-enabled taxi dispatchers, there will just be taxis." Calling a product internet-enabled will sound like calling a vacuum cleaner an electricity-enabled broom. Strategy: stop treating the technology as a feature, make it seamless, do not scrimp on UX, and go full-stack where depending on others' products would break the experience. Expect over-integration ("a la the electric can opener").
2. **Innovation becomes ubiquitous but small.** No more wild west. Innovation moves into everyday business processes; corporate venture capital (whose point was never money but a view of the innovation frontier) becomes passé; intrapreneurship becomes a threat to order with no offsetting benefit; skunkworks lose their purpose; CVC is replaced by M&A because financial and production-capital valuations converge when uncertainty is low. Lean and customer development, "actually old practices that fell out of favor during the installation period," return because they only work when the innovation trajectory is predictable: IBM practiced customer development in the 1950s, and RAND advocated build-test-learn for bombers and ICBMs. "You can't iterate your way to radical innovations, almost by definition." Steve Jobs could not have customer-developed the Apple computer, and the Altair as an MVP predicted nothing about mainstream personal computing.

The closing argument is that the economy has no equilibrium, only evolution, so the hard-won rules of the last thirty years (novelty over quality, disrupt yourself, new markets over existing ones, evangelize technology) are contingent and about to be overturned. Generals fight the last war; the transition is the worst moment to fall back on the tried and true.

## Scorecard, 2015–2025

| Neumann's prediction | What happened | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Financial capital pulls back; VC becomes irrelevant | 2015–2021 was the largest venture expansion on record (SoftBank's $100B Vision Fund in 2017; $621B of global venture funding in 2021 on CB Insights' count), a 2022 reset, then from 2023 the largest private rounds ever written, for AI labs | Wrong on direction for the decade |
| Innovation becomes small and predictable | Transformers (2017), GPT-3 (2020), ChatGPT (2022) | Wrong, unless a new technological system irrupted |
| ICT ubiquitous but invisible; "internet company" stops being a category | Nobody pitches internet companies; connectivity is assumed; Evans's "most people are online but most of the money is not" in [[internet-maturation]] is the same observation | Right |
| Full-stack startups; UX as a requirement | DoorDash, Opendoor, Tesla; "full-stack" became standard vocabulary, with mixed economics | Right |
| Corporate VC passé, replaced by M&A | CVC-backed funding hit a record $169B in 2021; Big Tech M&A was constrained by antitrust, which reads as the institutional re-assertion arriving late | Wrong |
| Lean and customer development ascendant | Orthodoxy for most companies; useless at the frontier, where nobody customer-developed GPT-4 (the Altair problem again; see [[minimum-viable-product]], [[startup-uncertainty]]) | Half right |
| Batteries as the reverse salient | Lithium-ion pack prices fell to $115/kWh in 2024 on BNEF's survey, roughly a tenth of 2010 levels; the binding constraint moved to electricity and data-center capacity ([[ai-capex-required-returns]]) | Right, and solved |
| Inequality falls in deployment | Wage compression at the bottom in 2020–23, asset-driven wealth concentration at the top | Mixed |

Three readings of the misses are available. The first is that Perez is wrong or unfalsifiable. The second is Perez's own: she has folded the 2008 crisis into the turning point, said in 2019 that "we have had two frenzies and we have not yet had a golden age," and calls the current surge's crisis phase the longest of any, with the institutional recomposition that deployment requires still pending. The third, and the one most consistent with the evidence, is that Neumann was right about ICT and a new wave irrupted on top of it. Perez's model has the next irruption begin during the previous wave's maturity. Late-stage ICT did pass to production capital: the private-equity roll-up of SaaS (Vista's "all software companies taste like chicken," recorded in [[asset-light-software]]) is literally production capital taking over, and venture money went looking for the next techno-economic paradigm and found machine learning. On that reading the 2020–21 boom was ICT's own late echo of the dot-com and housing double bubble, and the AI capex frenzy of 2023 onward is a new installation period, which is the position taken in [[asset-light-software]] and priced in [[ai-capex-required-returns]] and [[pacing-the-frontier]]. The overbuilding of infrastructure in a frenzy, and its cheap reuse afterward, is the optimistic case made for today's GPU build-out in [[ai-robotics-hype-cycles]] and the pessimistic case for its builders in [[commodity-trap]].

## Implications

- **The frame tells you which toolkit applies.** Deploying ICT into a laggard sector (government, healthcare, construction) is deployment-period work: customer pull, lean iteration, cheaper and invisible technology, full-stack where integrations are the weak point. Work at a frontier is installation-period work, where the innovation trajectory is unknown and lean is the wrong tool. Mixing them up is the common failure.
- **Reverse salients are an opportunity map.** Ask what laggard technology is holding the current system back; its solution is worth more than it looks. In 2015 that was batteries; in 2026 it is power, cooling and grid interconnection for compute.
- **Watch for institutional re-assertion as a timing signal.** Regulation after a frenzy marks the turn; its absence after 2008 may be why the golden age never arrived.
- **Discount a theory's dates more than its mechanism.** Neumann's mechanism survived; his decade did not. See [[reference-class-forecasting]] on why timing is the usual casualty.

# The distributive era

W. Brian Arthur, the Santa Fe Institute economist of increasing returns and author of *The Nature of Technology*, argued in the October 2017 *McKinsey Quarterly* that the digital revolution's third phase has created an **external intelligence**, housed in the virtual economy rather than in human workers, and that this pushes the economy past the "Keynes point," where enough is produced for everyone but the means of access to it, jobs, is tightening. The era we are entering, he says, "is not so much about production anymore... it is about distribution—how people get a share in what is produced." Production is an engineering problem; distribution is a political one; so "the new era will not be an economic one but a political one." He opens with checking in at Oslo airport without meeting a single human and feeling "out of human care."

## Three morphings of the digital economy

Arthur sees the digital revolution morphing roughly every twenty years:

1. **1970s–80s, computation.** Integrated circuits gave the economy serious computational assistance: CAD, real-time inventory, oil geology.
2. **1990s–2000s, connection.** Networked computers created the virtual economy and dissolved geography: a Seattle architect detailing a tower with cheaper staff in Budapest, US retailers monitoring Chinese factories. "Modern globalization had arrived and it was very much the result of connecting computers."
3. **2010s onward, sensing and association.** Cheap, ubiquitous sensors produced oceans of data, and data invited algorithms to make sense of it. Two surprises: the resulting intelligence was built not from symbolic logic but from statistical association over masses of data ("this complicated pixel pattern means 'cat'"), and computers could therefore do the one thing we had thought only humans could do, associate. He notes the systems were domain-specific: "an algorithm that could lip read could not recognize faces."

## External intelligence

Arthur defines intelligence for this purpose as appropriate association, or in an action domain the ability to sense a situation and act appropriately; a jellyfish closing around drifting food qualifies, and so does a collision-avoidance system on a fighter jet. No central controller is required: driverless traffic will be a conversation among cars, road markers and signals from which appropriate collective action emerges, "self-organizing, conversational, ever-adjusting, and dynamic," and largely autonomous.

The shift that matters is where intelligence lives. It has moved from inside workers' heads into the conversation among algorithms in the virtual economy: the physical economy queries, the virtual economy computes and reports back. Arthur's analogy is printing, which externalized information from monastery manuscripts and, by scholars' consensus, accelerated the Renaissance, the Reformation and science. Intelligence is "the *use* of information," so there is no reason to expect the second externalization to be weaker than the first, and "there is no upper limit to intelligence." This is intelligence as infrastructure, which is a different claim from the takeoff arguments in [[recursive-self-improvement]] and [[superintelligence]] and closer to the cheap-prediction framing in [[prediction-machines]].

## How business changes

The ordinary move is to automate existing products and value chains with face recognition or voice verification. The radical move is to stitch pieces of external intelligence into new business models: Arthur's example is a Chinese fintech app that identifies you by voice, fans out queries to your bank, credit history and social profile, weighs them, and puts a credit offer on your phone within seconds. Businesses can draw on a "library" of virtual structures as Lego pieces (he names blockchain and Bitcoin), and the result is industries re-architected rather than merely automated: "they will cease to exist in their current form."

He adds a prediction about who wins: "the advantages of being large or early in the market are limited. The components of external intelligence can't easily be owned, they tend to slide into the public domain. And data can't easily be owned either." Expect both large tech companies and shared, free, autonomous resources, plus industries nobody has thought of. This is the 2017 statement of the commoditization argument made about the model layer in [[commodity-trap]] and [[ai-value-capture]].

## The Keynes point

Arthur grants that jobs are vanishing (travel agents, typists, paralegals, "even high-end skilled jobs such as radiologists") and that economists mostly agree; the dispute is over replacement. History says freed labor finds new outlets. "I am not convinced." His two counter-precedents are Brynjolfsson and McAfee's horses, displaced by the automobile and never re-employed, and offshoring, which ate physical jobs and whole industries that were not replaced. Virtualization is offshoring to a virtual country.

Keynes's 1930 lecture "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren" coined "technological unemployment" and predicted that by about 2030 the production problem would be solved. Arthur believes the Keynes point has arrived early: US household income of $8.495 trillion shared across 116 million households would be $73,000 each, "enough for a decent middle-class life." Jobs have been the main means of access to output for only two or three hundred years; before that it was farm labor, craft workshops, piecework and inheritance. "Now access needs to change again... We have entered the distributive era."

## Realities of the distributive era

1. **Policy criteria change.** Growth is valued only insofar as it creates jobs; fracking is already defended on that basis. GDP and productivity, built for the physical economy, miscount virtual advances.
2. **Free-market philosophy gets harder to defend.** Economic theory promises efficiency plus compensation of losers by winners; in practice markets tend to concentration and, in the US and Britain, losers have rarely been compensated. "In the distributive era free-market efficiency will no longer be justifiable if it creates whole classes of people who lose."
3. **The era is political.** Populist anger about immigration, inequality and elites is the "harsh beginning." Arthur expects a long period of experimentation and, when it settles, parties offering "some version of a Scandinavian solution: capitalist-guided production and government-guided attention to who gets what," easier for Europe than for the United States, which "has never prized distribution over efficiency."

Mechanically he expects fewer and shorter jobs, shared jobs, "almost certainly" a basic income, more paid voluntary work (eldercare, mentoring), and a durable residue of empathy-requiring jobs such as kindergarten teaching and social work. Virtual services are nearly free; what people will need access to is the remaining physical goods and personal services. The open social questions are meaning without work, privacy against face recognition and data mining, and whether we want "an army of autonomous Jeeveses" anticipating our needs. His consolation is 1850s Britain: Dickensian conditions corrected over 30 to 50 years, or a century, by reformers rather than governments.

## Synthesis

- **Arthur's claim sidesteps the growth debate.** The Keynes point requires enough output, not explosive growth, so it is compatible with the no-double-digit-growth position in [[ai-explosive-growth]] and with Cowen's modest numbers in [[initiative-beats-intelligence]]. What it needs is the displacement half, which is where the evidence is contested: [[labor-share-under-automation]] asks whether labor's share survives, [[messy-jobs]] and [[task-automation-vs-paradigm-replacement]] explain why task automation has historically failed to kill jobs, and [[ai-and-relational-scarcity]] names the relational residue that Arthur also predicts (empathy jobs, paid care).
- **The politics has a second explanation.** Housel's [[postwar-us-economy]] reads the 2016 revolt as the breakdown of postwar expectations that things work roughly the same for everyone; Arthur reads it as the first symptom of lost access to the economy. The two are compatible, and both predict that the politics outlasts any single election.
- **Abundance and distribution are not rivals.** The supply-side program in [[abundance-agenda]] is about producing more of exactly the non-digitized goods (housing, energy, care) that Arthur says people will still need access to. Arthur predicts politics reorganizes around who gets them; abundance politics is about whether they exist to be distributed.
- **The arithmetic is weaker than the thesis.** Dividing mean household income by households assumes a distribution nobody has proposed and ignores the split between labor and capital income, and the Keynes point is asserted for the United States, not the world.

## Temporal notes

- **Domain-specificity broke.** Large language models since 2022 are general associative engines, which strengthens Arthur's externalization thesis; the "pattern generation" framing in [[generative-ai-as-pattern-generation]] is his "association" under another name, and [[scaling-hypothesis]] explains why it generalized.
- **Technological unemployment did not appear in the aggregate.** US unemployment sat at or near fifty-year lows for most of the years after the essay, and radiology, his example of a skilled job being replaced, ran a shortage instead. The first contested signal came in 2025 in the hiring of early-career workers in AI-exposed occupations; the state of that debate is tracked in [[labor-share-under-automation]] and [[ai-and-the-future-of-work]].
- **Basic income stayed at pilot scale** (Stockton, the OpenResearch study), while populist politics intensified around immigration and trade rather than distribution as such, a partial match for Arthur's "lengthy period of experimentation."
- **The mismeasurement argument** he leans on in point 1 was examined by Syverson (2017), who found it insufficient to explain the productivity slowdown.
- **Blockchain and Bitcoin as Lego pieces** did not become general business building blocks; the components that did were model APIs.

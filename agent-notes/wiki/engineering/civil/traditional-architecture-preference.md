# Public preference for traditional architecture

Roughly fifty years of visual preference surveys point in one direction: when shown a controlled pair of images that differ only in architectural style, between 60 and 90 percent of respondents pick the traditional design over the modernist one. Robert Kwolek's 2026 survey of this literature in *Works in Progress* argues the result has now been replicated enough — across housing, civic buildings, hospitals, offices, a stadium, a railway viaduct, and a college library, in the US, Britain, the Netherlands, Portugal, Chile, Canada, Norway and Greece — that it should be treated as a provisional fact about public taste rather than an anecdote. The more surprising half of the finding is how little demographics move it: men, the young, and perhaps the rich are slightly more open to modernism, but *every* measured subgroup still prefers traditional design by a clear majority.

Kwolek frames this against a long-standing consensus among modernism's own partisans that it was never loved. John Summerson (a sympathiser) wrote in 1940 that the public "can't understand it, never will, and hates it like poison"; Wolf von Eckardt (a critic) wrote in 1977 that "No one mourned. It was never popular." The surveys, on this reading, simply quantify what both sides already conceded.

## What a visual preference survey is

The instrument is minimal: two images and a neutral question ("Which do you prefer?"), put to a properly sampled panel. Its entire evidential value rests on the *control* of the image pair. If the two photographs differ in weather, camera angle, greenery, traffic, or massing as well as style, a preference tells you nothing about style specifically. The history of the method is therefore mostly a history of tightening that control:

- **Metuchen, New Jersey (1979).** The Rutgers Urban Design Studio photographed hundreds of local streets and had residents score them at a public meeting. Residents strongly favoured small-town streetscapes with detailed, varied facades and a walkable human scale, and disliked blank buildings and wide car-dominated roads. It was the first demonstration that responses to the built environment are *shared and predictable*, not idiosyncratic. But the sample was self-selected and the images uncontrolled, so no single result isolated style from street width, height-to-width ratio, or traffic. Metuchen later wrote a design code from the findings.
- **David Halpern, Cambridge (1987).** Students rated twelve faces and twelve buildings. All disciplines agreed on the faces; architecture students diverged sharply from everyone else on the buildings, sometimes ranking them in the opposite order. Small and unrepresentative, but it became the canonical demonstration of the expert/lay split (see below).
- **Popular Housing Forum / BMRB (1998).** The first properly sampled survey. Respondents chose between card sets of newbuild houses; 94 percent of prospective buyers preferred a traditional set, and 92 percent of everyone else preferred one to be built near them. Multiple images per set partly compensated for imperfect control.
- **ADAM Architecture / YouGov (2009).** Four real commercial buildings in matched perspective and weather, two traditional and two modernist, with both modernist examples prize-winners. Traditional won 77 to 23. Kwolek calls these the best-controlled comparators before the 2020s.
- **The 2020s.** AI image editing made it cheap to take a photograph of a real modernist building and generate a traditional counterfactual with identical context, weather and massing (The Aesthetic City's Dutch study did this for seven of eight pairs). Combined with specialist groups (Create Streets in Britain, The Aesthetic City in the Netherlands, the National Civic Art Society in the US) commissioning polls from professional panels, this produced what Kwolek calls a "golden age" of the method.

## The modern, well-controlled results

| Survey | Year | Subject | Traditional vs modern |
|---|---|---|---|
| National Civic Art Society / Harris (US) | 2020 | 7 pairs of federal buildings and courthouses, controlled for massing and context | 72% average; best pair 81/19 |
| Create Streets (UK) | 2023 | Bath rugby stadium: classical counterproposal vs utilitarian scheme | 74 / 26 |
| Create Streets (UK) | 2024 | HS2 viaduct: brick-clad vs planned concrete | 69 / 28 |
| Create Streets (UK) | 2025 | Christ's College Cambridge library: Gothic counterproposal vs Grafton Architects scheme, same floor area | 71 / 21 |
| Create Streets (UK) | 2025 | Marks and Spencer, Oxford Street: retain-and-extend facade (Francis Terry) vs demolish-and-replace, same floor area, 3,130 respondents | 79 / 17 |
| Create Streets (UK) | 2025 | Shoreditch Works: much *taller* Victorian/Art Deco-styled offices vs existing postwar warehouses | 76–78% for the proposal |
| The Aesthetic City (NL) | 2025 | 8 AI-controlled pairs | 82% average, range 67–92 |
| Academic paper (Norway and Greece) | 2026 | Traditional vs award-winning contemporary designs | Clear traditional preference in both; stronger in Greece |

Two of these deserve extra comment. The **Christ's College** and **Marks and Spencer** pairs pit a traditional counterproposal against work by a top-tier firm or a major corporate client, which weakens the standard objection that the modernist comparator was simply a weak building. And **Shoreditch Works** loads the dice *against* the traditional side by making it dramatically taller, since the public is usually assumed to dislike height, yet three-quarters still preferred it, and younger respondents more than older ones.

## Demographic invariance

The National Civic Art Society survey is the richest on this: 70 percent of Democrats and 73 percent of Republicans preferred traditional; women 77 versus men 67; white 75, Hispanic 65, Black 62 percent; no significant effect of income or education; over-65s at 77 versus 18–34s at 68. Create Streets' British polls show the same shape, with occasional inversions (Labour voters slightly *more* traditional than Conservatives on the HS2 viaduct; the youngest cohort more traditional than the oldest on the same poll and on Shoreditch Works). The consistent finding is not that groups agree exactly but that the *sign* never flips: no demographic slice has yet preferred the modernist option.

This matters because it removes the most obvious deflationary story. If the preference were a proxy for age, class, or politics, it would be explainable as nostalgia, status signalling, or partisan alignment. A preference that survives all of those cuts looks more like a property of human perception than of any particular culture. Kwolek stops short of saying that, but the Metuchen finding (shared, predictable responses to the built environment) is the same claim in embryo.

## The expert/lay divergence

Halpern's 1987 result has been replicated in Canada (Brown and Gifford, 2001), Britain (2015), Chile (2019), and Portugal (Luis Balula's Évora study, 2008–09, which split respondents into experts and non-experts). The pattern is consistent: both groups like old buildings, but on *new* work they diverge, with experts positive about modernist blocks the public rates negatively and negative about "pastiche" traditional housing the public likes. Kwolek suggests this explains why the public's preference has repeatedly *surprised* the commissioning bodies: the 2005 CABE report complained that the Modern Movement's "concern for light and sun and large windows should give a modern style a clear advantage ... but this advantage does not seem to have been understood by home buyers."

The Halpern design is worth dwelling on because of its control condition. Architecture students did not have unusual taste *in general*; they agreed with everyone about faces. Their divergence was specific to the object their training concerned. That is the signature of trained taste, the same mechanism [[connoisseurship-as-judgment-training]] describes approvingly in art collecting and investing: deep exposure recalibrates what you see as outstanding, away from consensus. The architecture case is the uncomfortable mirror image. Connoisseurship builds a *private* taste that is valuable precisely because it differs from the crowd; but architecture is a public good consumed by the crowd, so a profession whose trained taste is anti-correlated with its users' is a design problem, not an achievement. Whether the expert taste is "better" (more informed about light, structure, honesty of materials) or merely *different* (an in-group aesthetic) is exactly what the surveys cannot settle, and the two sides of the architecture debate read the same data in opposite ways.

## Objections Kwolek concedes

- **Loaded pairs.** Sceptics argue each modernist loser was simply a weak building, and a better one would have won. The prize-winning and prestige-firm comparators blunt this, but cannot eliminate it.
- **Commissioner bias.** Nearly all surveys were paid for by organisations sympathetic to traditional architecture; the early CABE studies are among the few counterexamples, and they found the same thing.
- **Thin evidence base.** Perhaps three dozen fully controlled image pairs exist, and only from Britain, the Netherlands and the US. Kwolek expects the base to triple or quadruple within a decade and defers firmer conclusions until then. The 2026 Norway/Greece paper is the first addition logged in the article's running appendix.
- **Lost images.** Some older studies' original images no longer exist, so their results cannot be re-examined.

## The puzzle the article leaves open

If 70 to 90 percent of people prefer traditional buildings, why does almost nothing get built that way? Kwolek's numbers: nearly every architecture school teaches only broadly modernist styles; probably over 99 percent of architects practice only in them; Britain has had hardly one traditional public commission since the 1950s. Outside private housing (the one market where the end user picks the product), traditional styles have been effectively extinct for seventy years. Some of this is explained by anti-traditional planning rules, but the pattern holds in jurisdictions whose planning systems are stylistically neutral. Kwolek names this a divergence between stated and revealed preference and explicitly declines to explain it, promising a follow-up.

Some hypotheses the article gestures at but does not develop:

- **It is a principal-agent gap, not a revealed-preference gap.** "Revealed preference" is a misnomer when the people commissioning buildings are not the people who live with them. Public bodies, institutions and developers choose architects; architects, per the expert/lay data, have anti-correlated taste; the public consumes the output but never chooses it. The one market where the consumer *does* choose, private housing, is exactly where traditional styles survive. On this reading there is no puzzle about human preference, only about institutional design: who holds the pen.
- **Professional gatekeeping compounds it.** Prizes, journals, schools and peer review are run by the group whose taste diverges, so the incentive gradient for an ambitious architect points away from what the public wants. Halpern's students had presumably *acquired* their taste in a few years of training, which suggests the divergence is reproduced by education rather than by selection into the profession.
- **Height and style are separable axes.** Shoreditch Works is the most policy-relevant result in the piece. Opposition to development is usually modelled as opposition to density, which makes housing supply a fight about quantity (the frame in [[commercial-zoning-costs]] and the [[abundance-agenda]]). If a large share of that opposition is actually about *appearance*, then traditional facades are a cheap concession that buys density, and a YIMBY coalition that ignores style is leaving votes on the table. Kwolek does not draw this conclusion, but the survey design that supposedly handicapped the traditional side (making it taller) is also the one closest to a real planning decision.
- **The method itself is the news.** The Dutch study's use of AI to generate the controlled counterfactual generalises well beyond architecture: any preference question whose confound is "the two real examples differ in a hundred ways" can now be tested by *generating* the comparator rather than searching for one. The same technique that makes preference surveys cheap also makes them easy to load, so provenance of the images will become the first thing to check.

The Metuchen finding about human scale, varied facades and walkability also connects to the fine-grained streetscape described in [[tokyo-microspaces]]; the 1979 residents were, in effect, scoring the qualities that Tokyo's building ecology produces by accident.

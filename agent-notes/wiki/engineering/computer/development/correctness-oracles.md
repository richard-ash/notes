# Correctness Oracles for Agent-Generated Code

A **correctness oracle** is any mechanism that can decide, without a human reading the code, whether a piece of software did the right thing: a reference implementation to diff against, a property that must hold, a protocol contract a message must satisfy, a model checker's exhaustive search, a static analysis that fails the build. Quinn Wilton's ElixirConf US 2026 talk *The Graveyard of Good Ideas* argues that once coding agents can produce more code than you can read, the engineer's job stops being "write the code" and becomes "build the ladder of oracles the code has to climb." Wilton's stronger claim is historical: the oracles worth building are mostly *old* ideas — Armstrong's UBF contracts (2002), Lamport's TLA+ (1990s), Datalog-based program analysis (practical since 2004) — that died one of two deaths, "too expensive to build or too strange to use," and coding agents dissolve both causes of death at once.

The talk is a single opinionated source, delivered from a hobbyist-but-expert position (Wilton does Rust and Elixir work, wrote a typed-Elixir prototype in 2022, and reports a bug against a widely used Elixir library with the tooling described). Treat the practitioner claims as one person's experience and the historical claims as well-sourced.

## The graveyard: three ideas that were ahead of their tooling

Wilton frames the talk around ideas that were written down and "left for someone else to find."

**Richardson's forecast factory (1922).** Lewis Fry Richardson, a Quaker mathematician serving in the Friends' Ambulance Unit on the Western Front, reduced weather forecasting to arithmetic: partition the atmosphere into a grid, seven equations per cell, march the grid forward in time. His single hand-computed forecast (published in *Weather Prediction by Numerical Process*, 1922) took months and was wrong, and the book closes with a "fantasy": a theatre-shaped hall in which 64,000 human computers each work one cell of the globe, read their neighbours' fresh values, and are kept in lockstep by a conductor in a central pulpit shining rosy light on regions racing ahead and blue on regions falling behind. Wilton's reading: this is a message-passing actor system — one process per cell, nearest-neighbour communication, a coordinator whose only job is synchronisation — designed decades before any hardware could run it, and staffed with people because that was the only runtime available. The idea was vindicated in 1950 when Charney, Klára and John von Neumann ran Richardson's grid method on ENIAC (a 24-hour forecast in roughly 24 hours, bottlenecked on 100,000 hand-sorted punch cards). Peter Lynch later reconstructed Richardson's exact 1910-data computation as a Fortran program (*The Emergence of Numerical Weather Prediction: Richardson's Dream*, 2006) — but as a single sequential process, never as the factory.

**Armstrong's UBF (2002).** Joe Armstrong's Erlang Workshop paper *Getting Erlang to talk to the outside world* attacks the space *between* components: the payment capture that goes through for more than was authorised, followed by an incident call where three teams each have logs proving it wasn't their fault. UBF had three layers — A, a wire format; C, a meta-protocol for agreeing which contract is in force; and B, a tiny language for writing a client/server protocol as a state machine: in each state, what the client may say, what the server replies, which state that leaves you in, and which events the server may send unprompted. Given a contract, a generic checker sits between the parties and attributes every violation to whoever caused it, at the moment it happens — "the incident call the system diagnosed for itself." The project is frozen and unmaintained. Armstrong's own postmortem, seven years on: "This scheme was never widely adopted. Perhaps it was just too strange." He rebuilt the checker on JSON because nobody would adopt a new wire format to get a contract checker.

**Lamport's TLA+ (1990s–).** The Amazon experience report (Newcombe et al., written 2014) is, in Wilton's words, "about as strong an endorsement as a tool can get": TLA+ applied to ten large real-world systems, "in every case" finding subtle bugs "we are sure we would not have found by any other means," some in DynamoDB 35 steps deep. It changed almost nothing. Thirty years after its introduction TLA+ is "something most engineers have heard of but never touched." Wilton places this beside the UBF postmortem and Shriram Krishnamurthi's observation that guarantees have always been hard to get adopted because "getting new people to do new things is hard."

Wilton's own entry in the graveyard is **Deft** (2022): a Turnstile-inspired ("Type Systems as Macros", Racket) sandbox for building typed languages inside Elixir via macros — algebraic data types, native interop, swappable typing rules, exhaustiveness checking — that worked but was shelved because turning it from *interesting* into *useful* meant a language server, editor integration, rich diagnostics, and interop with the whole Phoenix toolchain. José Valim has made the same point about starting any language today: tooling is first-class and editor integration is table stakes. That is a job for a community or a grant-funded full-timer, not one hobbyist coding at night.

## Why the graveyard is opening

Wilton dates her own inflection point to the 2025–26 winter holidays, when coding agents under the right guardrails began producing code "higher quality than I could write, faster than I could write it." The mechanism she names is **translation cost**: translating an idea out of a paper, or a technique out of another ecosystem, was the wrapper of work that killed Deft, UBF, and Richardson's factory, and it is exactly what got cheap. Three supporting voices:

- **Ilya Sergey** (programming-languages researcher, summer 2026) on what it takes to get a language idea published — extensive benchmarking or machine-checked proofs pushed through by hand: "that wrapper is now gone." Wilton's corollary: if it's gone for a soundness proof it's gone for a language server too. "The wall wasn't a wall anymore. It had been transformed into a long list."
- **Steve Klabnik** (abstract for a late-September 2026 talk on building a language with LLMs): the tools "changed what kinds of language engineering I considered practical to undertake," and the most significant impact "may not be faster implementation, but making rigorous specification, validation, and design practices practical for language projects that lack the resources of large language teams or industrial standards bodies."
- **Krishnamurthi** (2026 proposal to rebuild Brown's PL course): "Let the AI run wild as long as we can impose constraints on what code is generated. Programming languages are the home of guarantees." Wilton's gloss: what's new is *who* is being constrained. And the adoption problem inverts — "UBF and TLA+ may have been too weird for most of us, but the agents living in my terminal don't care about weird. They revel in it."

Two small demonstrations of translation-as-a-primitive precede the main argument. **Aeromancy** (name reconstructed from the auto-captions) is Wilton's Elixir port of Lynch's Fortran that finally builds Richardson's factory as designed — each map cell a process, message-passing to neighbours — generated in about two hours. **Pentiment** is a diagnostics-rendering package that brings Rust-ecosystem error reporting (highlighted source, labelled spans, a hint per error; Wilton cites Rustler's errors as the BEAM's best) into Elixir, and gave Deft in a few hours "a level of polish I should never have considered for a side project."

## The oracle ladder

Wilton's pivot: "once I could get more code written than I could read, the question stopped being *what can I translate* and became *how do I trust what gets translated*." Generated code is only as good as the tests it must pass — "unit tests were good, integration tests were better, property tests were best" — but there is a ladder above property tests: stateful property tests, metamorphic tests, model checkers. Whatever the rung, "what the agent needed was an oracle." The talk then climbs the ladder with one tool per rung.

### Rung 1 — reference implementations: the tower of oracles

Aeromancy's port is trustworthy for a structural reason: Lynch's Fortran is vendored into the repository and every time step of the port is checked against every time step of the original. Wilton generalises this into a **tower of oracles** with three engines:

1. a *sequential engine* translated directly from the Fortran, checked against it for all 24 forecast hours;
2. an *independent tensor engine* built on Nx, which cannot share the sequential engine's porting mistakes, checked bit-for-bit against it at every step;
3. the *factory* itself, which shares a kernel with the sequential engine, so any disagreement between them "can only stem from the message passing that ties the whole system together."

The design principle is independence: each oracle is built so that it cannot inherit the mistakes of the thing it checks, and each pairwise comparison localises a failure to one subsystem. Wilton's summary is that porting against a vendored original "turns out to be the perfect shape for agentic work because the original serves as an oracle for correctness" — the same shape as the Elixir Dynamo re-implementation in [[amazon-dynamo]], where the SOSP paper plays the role of the Fortran.

### Rung 2 — architecture as a test: `assert_boundary`

`assert_boundary` is a small package for asserting on the shape of a project's module-dependency graph from inside the test suite. In Aeromancy it forbids anything inside the factory from reaching for the oracle or reference engine, because that dependency would make the whole equivalence relation circular; the day an agent (or Wilton) adds such an edge, the test fails and reports it. The lesson she draws is not "use my package" but the pattern: "by encoding the intent behind my design into a constraint my agents can't argue with, I no longer have to watch out for an entire class of mistakes in every code review. Instead of adding one more paragraph to my prompt asking my agents to please respect my architecture, the test suite holds the line." This is the same move as Lopopolo's generated linters and structural tests in [[harness-engineering]], stated as a general principle: a prompt is a request, a test is a guarantee.

### Rung 3 — protocol contracts: Accord

The message flows in an OTP application are usually implicit — "a GenServer here, a raw `send` there, and the actual rules of who may say what and when living nowhere but in my head." **Accord** is Wilton's translation of UBF-B into an Elixir DSL that sits in the project next to the code it describes: states, the messages a client may send in each, the replies a server may give, the state each leaves you in, plus *tracks* (running values such as `authorized`, `captured`, `refunded`), *guards* on transitions, and *properties* that must hold over a whole conversation.

The worked example is Armstrong's payment lifecycle. In `created`, the only client message is an authorisation with a positive amount; the server approves (→ `authorized`, recording the amount in the `authorized` track) or declines (conversation over). In `authorized` the client may capture, but only if a guard passes: *this amount plus everything captured so far must fit inside the authorisation.* A passing capture returns to `authorized` and bumps the `captured` track, which is what makes the rule cumulative rather than per-call. Finalising ends the capture phase; refunds return money up to the amount captured; if nothing was captured you may void instead.

Writing that down buys three things:

- **An exerciser.** Hand Accord the protocol and a server and it generates sessions from the state machine — a mix of allowed and disallowed messages — checking every reply against the contract. Wilton's longest generated session had eight transitions (an authorisation, three partial captures, a finalise, two refunds, a close) and she stresses "I never wrote this test." When a server is wrong (replying `{:ok, amount}` where the contract says plain `:ok` — "exactly the sort of thing I would miss in a code review") the exerciser shrinks the trace to the smallest failing session and points at the contract line that says what the reply should have been.
- **A monitor.** Put the protocol's monitor in front of the server and talk to it instead: allowed traffic is forwarded, disallowed traffic is rejected with the current track values and an attribution of *which party is at fault* — authorise 4, capture 3, capture 3 again is bounced at the guard and blamed on the client. This is Armstrong's contract checker, revived.
- **Properties over conversations.** "Captures never exceed the total authorised amount"; "nothing is captured before it is authorised." Random sessions can find violations but never prove their absence — which motivates the next rung.

Accord was pointed at a real protocol too: Wilton translated the HTTP/2 stream state machine (RFC 9113 §5.1) into a contract and shimmed the monitor between a raw H2 client and a live **Bandit** server. Finding: the RFC requires a new stream identifier to exceed every previous one, and a server that sees otherwise must respond with a connection error; Bandit, sent stream 5 then stream 3, silently dropped the frame and carried on. "Not especially dangerous, but subtle."

### Rung 4 — model-checking the contract: TLA+ and TLC

The contract itself can be wrong. Wilton's motivating story is Lamport's: Paxos (1990) was published in 1998 after the Indiana-Jones-archaeologist framing sank it for eight years; *Paxos Made Simple* (2001) was plain English with nothing more complex than a comparison; fourteen years later a Stack Overflow question asked whether one sentence contradicted itself, and Michael Deardeuff of Amazon spent a month establishing that every implementation he examined had the same bug. Lamport declined to fix the prose: "Prose is not the way to precisely describe algorithms. Do not try to implement the algorithm from the paper." His alternative — a specification is a written description of what a system is supposed to do, in TLA+ — is "good enough for me," and Accord generates a TLA+ spec from every protocol and runs TLC over it: initial state from the tracks, every transition an action, every guard a precondition, every property an invariant checked in every reachable state.

The demonstration is the guard written the absent-minded way — "the amount being captured is less than what was authorised," ignoring prior captures. A server that faithfully implements this contract faithfully implements a bug, and fuzzing only catches it if the random sessions happen to exercise the lapse. TLC does not rely on luck: seventeen states in it returns the counterexample *authorise 1, capture 1, capture 1* — two captured against an authorisation of one, "the same incident from earlier, except this time it was found before any code existed to cause it." Restore the cumulative guard and the full state space (80 states) checks in under a second. A single `mix` task regenerates and checks every protocol in a project, which Wilton also uses as a design-review surface: read the spec before wading through the implementation.

What the checker cannot do, and Wilton insists on this, is "decide which properties matter, or whether the model is the system I actually meant to build. That part is still my job." Her extension of Lamport's rule closes the loop on the whole talk: prose is not a precise way to describe an algorithm, and it "isn't an effective way to tell an agent what you want either."

### Rung 5 — static analysis as compile errors: Argus

The last rung targets bugs no test session would reach: structural flaws in OTP supervision trees. The example is a real bug Wilton found in Oban 2.20 in early 2026 (identified from the process names in the transcript — Notifier, Sonar, Midwife are Oban's internal processes): two layers of processes under a `:one_for_one` supervisor, where the Sonar and Midwife register their pids with the Notifier at startup and the Notifier stores them in a map. If the Notifier crashes, `:one_for_one` restarts it alone with an empty listener map; the survivors never re-register and never monitor it, so notifications silently stop and the system "degrades into a non-functional state." The fix is small (switch to `:rest_for_one`, or have the survivors monitor and re-register); the hard part is the reasoning about every process in the tree and how they depend on each other — Peter Alvaro's "genius" that failure testing has historically depended on.

Wilton's rule for the whole class: *a supervisor may use `:one_for_one` only if there are no unmonitored dependencies between any of its children.* Stated that way it is a query over the codebase, and **Argus** runs such queries in Datalog. Facts are extracted from BEAM bytecode — every function, every remote call, each supervisor's restart strategy and children, a cross-module call graph; rules derive higher-level facts (which processes sit under which subtree, which hold references to which); analyses are joins over the facts with negation for pruning — for `:one_for_one` coupling, find every such supervisor, every pair of children in distinct subtrees, and any reference from one to the other without a monitor. Diagnostics (rendered through Pentiment) point at the supervisor line to change plus the two lines that serve as evidence. Wilton reported the Oban bug in February 2026 and a fix was merged days later; she never ran the code — "analysing the bug told me everything I needed to know." Argus has around twenty further analyses covering supervision and process structure, safety, and common performance bottlenecks, with a dedicated talk at Goatmire.

The whole thing is wrapped as a Mix compiler, so "as long as my Datalog is sound, I'll never end up merging a broken supervision tree whether or not I even look at the code." Wilton is explicit that only the OTP-specific analyses are hers. Doop has analysed Java this way since 2009, Soufflé (2016) is a compiler for exactly these programs, and Gigahorse uses the same techniques to decompile Ethereum smart contracts; the theory dates to the early 1990s and has been mainstream-practical since 2004. And barely anyone has touched it, for the *expensive-to-build* reason: those projects carry tens of thousands of lines of Datalog across thousands of VM-specific rules, "nearly all of the commits written by credentialed researchers specialising in program analysis." That is the translation cost that has now plummeted.

## Implications

**This is the "validate genuinely better" horn, made concrete.** Herrengt's [[understanding-as-the-bottleneck]] argues that cheap code generation leaves a trilemma — generate less, validate genuinely better, or accept lower quality — and that most organisations take the third by default, because review was sized for human-authored volume and tests only cover the behaviours you thought to test. Wilton's ladder is a worked answer to the second horn, and each rung is specifically an escape from "the behaviours you thought to test": the exerciser derives sessions you didn't write, TLC enumerates states you didn't imagine, Datalog queries fire on structure you never read. Herrengt's stated falsification condition — reliable spec-to-code verification would make review obsolete — is close to what Wilton claims to run: "I'm not even reviewing the code my agents generate."

**Executable specs versus prose specs.** Notion's [[spec-driven-development]] and Qodo's "specs are the input that makes correctness reviewable" ([[ai-code-review]]) both keep the spec in plain English with a verification section at the bottom. Lamport's warning applies to them: prose specs are exactly what produced fourteen years of buggy Paxos implementations. Wilton's contract is the verification section promoted to *be* the spec — the intent is written once, in a form that generates the tests, the runtime monitor, and the model. The cost is that you can only write executable contracts for what you can formalise, which is why the Accord examples are protocols and state machines rather than product features.

**Constraints are the cheap kind of prompt.** Willison's "over-testing is no longer expensive" and red/green TDD in [[agentic-engineering]], Lopopolo's mechanical enforcement of architecture and taste in [[harness-engineering]], Tan's "fat code" for deterministic operations in [[agentic-engineering-architecture]], and Jozefiak's "check the outcome, not the activity" in [[agent-failure-modes]] all describe moving intent out of the prompt and into something that fails the build. Wilton adds the top of that ladder — model checking and whole-program analysis — and the historical observation that these rungs already existed and were rejected by *humans* for being weird. If the code is written by something that doesn't mind weird, the adoption barrier that killed UBF and stalled TLA+ is gone, and the barrier that remains is whether you can write the property.

**What's left for judgment.** Larson's [[judgment-in-ai-assisted-development]] says judgment becomes the binding constraint once implementation time is solved; Wilton names the judgments precisely: which properties matter, whether the model is the system you meant, and (from the Q&A) whether the thing you're building is still the thing you set out to build. Her admission that Deft, once building became easy, mutated into "a dependently typed proof assistant with session types" that "no one will probably ever see" — because she never stopped to ask what she was actually trying to accomplish — is the talk's own counterexample: cheap code lowers the cost of *not* deciding, which is the failure surface Shihipar's [[finding-your-unknowns]] is built around.

**The soundness gap.** Every rung is only as good as the oracle. The tower-of-oracles principle handles this for reference implementations (independent engines cannot share mistakes), and TLC handles it for contracts (the model checks the guard). Nothing in the talk checks the Datalog: "as long as my Datalog is sound" is doing real work in the Argus claim, and program-analysis rule sets are precisely the artefact the field's specialists found hard to get right. A vendored reference is a stronger oracle than a rule you wrote yourself, and the talk's ordering — reference, then test, then contract, then model, then analysis — is also an ordering of decreasing self-evidence.

**Fragmentation.** Asked whether hyper-personalised tooling accelerates ecosystem fragmentation (the theme of the previous ElixirConf), Wilton agrees it is "one of the biggest unknowns" and offers only the hope that people keep sharing how they build. Her closing instruction is consistent with that: don't adopt her tools — "I built them for myself because they're the software I always wanted" — ask instead what *your* forecast factory is, the idea you shelved because it wasn't feasible, "and what are you going to do now that you can finally build it?" The last caveat is Richardson's: outside the factory are playing fields, houses, mountains and lakes, "for it was thought that those who computed the weather should breathe of it freely." Wilton reports spending the reclaimed time climbing, reading in a hammock, and playing guitar while agents work.

# The BEAM in the Agent Era

Chris McCord, creator of Phoenix and an engineer at Fly.io working on its Sprites sandbox product, gave the Day 2 keynote at ElixirConf US 2026 (recorded 11 September 2026) under the title *What Comes Next*. The talk makes one argument on two levels. On the practitioner level, McCord reports that he no longer writes code, that a single long-running coding-agent loop with no skills, sub-agents or MCP now builds and deploys complete distributed infrastructure for him, and that the loop has begun to reach past the build step into the running system. On the thesis level, he argues that the agent era is converging on exactly the problem Erlang was designed for — software that is always running, always connected, always changing and must supervise and heal itself with less human intervention — so the Erlang virtual machine (the BEAM) is positioned, as it was for multicore and for the web, to "meet the moment" without having tried to.

The talk pairs with two sources in this vault from the same fortnight. Quinn Wilton's talk at the same conference ([[correctness-oracles]]) answers the question McCord raises and only half-answers — how to trust code you did not write — with static and model-based checking. José Valim's essay thirteen days later ([[programming-languages-for-agents]]) proposes exposing the BEAM's runtime introspection to agents as a tooling direction; McCord's keynote is the field report that it already works. And twelve days after McCord, DHH's Rails World keynote ([[end-of-hand-written-code]]) made the same "I don't write code" claim with a different attitude to reading what the agent produces.

McCord is not a neutral witness: he created the framework he is recommending, works for the company whose product he demos, and nearly every number in the talk is self-reported from his own benchmarks. He also says plainly that he cannot explain why he gets 10–100× from the same models that make other engineers net-negative. Read the talk as a high-signal single-practitioner account.

## From generators to whole applications in a year

McCord opens with the receipts on a prediction. A little over a year earlier he had said the industry had "just left our punch card era" and that by 2026 it would seem crazy that we typed code by hand. For him it has come true: he does not write code, "not even single lines — I burn tokens to say please change this line," and rarely enters his editor, though he still reads code. His pre-LLM work (FLAME for running compute without the developer thinking about where; remote AI sandboxes running agent loops in Elixir "before harness became a word") and his 2025 work (the phoenix.new product, trying to make Sonnet-4-era models competent at Phoenix through generated `AGENTS.md`-style gap-filling) he now considers mostly superseded: "these things know what to do now and know how to gap fill."

His trajectory for code generation, in his own framing:

| Era | How far it got |
|---|---|
| Phoenix generators | ~10% of the way to something real |
| Vibe coding (a year earlier) | ~75%; stayed on the rails for 10–20 minutes, leaving the "last mile" of business logic and payments to the human |
| September 2026 | the agent writes the complete, non-trivial, built, tested, deployed application; a 20-hour Codex run shipped real product work |

His workflow is deliberately minimal. One continuous Codex project loop per project, auto-compacting, running for months (resuming it takes three minutes because the history is so long). No skills, no sub-agents, no MCP: "keep it simple." He uses whichever CLI is up that day, Codex or Claude, and says the minimalism is what makes that portability possible. Every night he sends off a long-running task. On a $200-a-month plan he has never hit a usage limit, and he suggests people who do are over-complicating. This is the opposite pole from the skill-and-harness literature in [[claude-code-skills]], [[custom-harnesses]] and [[agentic-engineering-architecture]], and McCord offers it as a data point rather than a prescription — his later explanation for the variance is not tooling at all.

Two predictions he repeats from the previous year, with the note that both have come true more slowly than he expected. **Agent environments go remote**: a desktop harness that is "just a loop to a chat-completions endpoint" calling the cloud anyway makes no sense when agents can do twenty hours of useful work after you close your laptop. **Agents need an environment they can change**: humans use computers, agents are good at pretending to be humans using computers, so give them a whole machine rather than trying to lock them down — the opposite instinct to [[agent-sandboxing-devcontainers]], and he does say "got to be careful" when he later describes giving one a production shell.

## Production as the artifact

McCord's "bigger bet," the one he says makes people uncomfortable, is that agents become the whole software cycle rather than the coding step of it: building, deploying, monitoring, picking up the issue another agent filed, and that the artifact of software becomes production itself. Commit → push → CI → container registry → deploy is, in his word, "ceremony" — a mutable thing pushed through several immutable snapshots so it can be mutated again. Git stays relevant, but the cycle collapses toward "back to the PHP days: FTP the file." "Prod is the artifact and change is constant."

He is "not quite there" but living it on staging. The agent has permission to run `fly deploy` to staging and, once deployed, opens a remote shell (`remsh`) into the running node. He describes a ten-hour end-to-end stress test in which the agent monitors file-system pressure and tail latencies locally, checks the Elixir client application for faults, then uses the remote shell to trace processes and inspect mailboxes on the Sprites control plane, locates the cause of the latency, patches it locally, runs the test suite, builds a release, deploys to staging, remshes back in, and repeats all night. "I wake up to runs" that have done this unattended.

Two things in this account are worth separating. The first is the loop shape, which is the single-practitioner version of what [[unattended-coding-agents]] and [[software-factory-pattern]] describe at the organisation level, extended one step further downstream than either — those accounts end at the merged PR, McCord's ends at a redeployed system verified against its own traces. Lopopolo's [[harness-engineering]] gets closest, with a per-worktree bootable app and an ephemeral observability stack so the running app is legible to the agent; McCord skips the ephemeral copy and points the agent at the real staging cluster. The second is what makes it feasible: the BEAM exposes a live system — processes, mailboxes, tracing, the ability to change code in place — through a shell an agent can type into. "This is a superpower, and it's a superpower for agents to do." That is precisely the "runtime observability over debuggers" proposal in [[programming-languages-for-agents]], demonstrated rather than proposed.

## "Language doesn't matter" — until the second machine

McCord re-shows a slide from the previous year: it makes people uncomfortable that language no longer seems to matter. For him it is a benefit, because the "frustration gap" of a new paradigm was always the hard part of selling Erlang and Elixir, and now one can say "agent, please build Elixir stuff" and get a decent result.

But he adds the nuance the usual argument omits. For one program on one computer, language genuinely does not matter; the agent will nail it in anything. "The moment you have a distributed system — you run on more than one machine — the language absolutely matters and the runtime is critical." His evidence is where tokens get spent. Agents in Go or Rust, he reports, spend their effort on the ceremony of talking to other machines: bringing in gRPC, bringing in NATS, then diagnosing which mutex was grabbed where. The human experiences this as a huge speed-up because the agent is doing frustrating work faster. In Elixir the agent "solves your actual problems faster because we just don't have those problems." The claim is not that agents *cannot* do the distributed plumbing in other stacks; it is that the plumbing is where their time goes.

This is the operational face of a point Valim makes structurally: languages survive the agent era because they encode different guarantees, and the Erlang process model's runtime-enforced isolation and fault tolerance is one of them ([[programming-languages-for-agents]]'s "runtime-enforced" category). It also cuts against the implicit cost model of [[choose-boring-technology]]. Boring-technology reasoning assumes the expensive part of an unfamiliar stack is learning and operating it; if agents absorb the learning cost, the residual cost is whatever the runtime does *not* give you, which is the quantity McCord is pointing at.

## What he built, and the confidence to ship it

The middle of the talk is a tour of infrastructure McCord built — "and by me I mean I coerced the LLM to do it," over weeks of iteration, never single-shot — for the new Sprites control plane. The list matters less for the individual projects than for the pattern: each is a thing that was "theoretically possible" in Elixir for a decade and did not exist because nobody had the time.

- **A reverse proxy with full WebSocket support**, built on the Bandit web server (the name is a Bandit pun). Hex had several reverse proxies; none did WebSockets, and companies had private ones.
- **EKV**, an Elixir key-value store with zero runtime dependencies (SQLite vendored and precompiled). It began as an eventually consistent cache for the Sprites cluster; when his durable-objects work needed compare-and-swap, he had the agent select and implement **CASPaxos** for consistent reads and writes. Member, observer and client modes let nodes participate without replicating all data. Reported numbers: hundreds of thousands of reads per second; 86,000 ops/s mixed; ~7,000 consistent writes per second across a three-node quorum, including cross-region. He flags this one as the place to "be careful" — "don't build a database."
- **Group**, a distributed process registry and process group (José Valim dislikes the name; McCord's defence is that Elixir's `Registry` with duplicate keys *is* a local process group, so the standard library set the precedent). It is what `Phoenix.PubSub` and `Phoenix.Presence` "should be if I started from scratch," with isolated sub-clusters (only nodes that care about a key space replicate it), targeted broadcasts, real-time monitoring, and anti-entropy delta sync so a flapping node receives the messages it missed rather than the whole world. In production at Fly for about a year; the long-term plan is to put it underneath PubSub and Presence with the existing interfaces preserved.
- **Durable Server**, durable objects for Elixir, heavily LLM-generated, the subject of an earlier talk of his, and load-bearing in Sprites.
- **A file system in Elixir**: a network-block-device (NBD) server backed by object storage. Codex (GPT-5.5) refused several times and insisted on Rust; McCord "had to pitch it," and the winning argument was that an NBD is a server that answers byte-range requests from the kernel, the network is the bottleneck regardless, and Elixir is very good at servers. The result matches the performance of Fly's internal Go implementation. "I was right. I still have value." Every night it runs thousands of checkpoint-and-fork fuzz iterations.
- **SocketDist**, a replacement for the ten-years-unmaintained `gen_rpc` pattern. Erlang distribution is a single connection per node pair, so pushing bulk traffic through it causes head-of-line blocking. Rather than configure sideband TLS connections by hand, SocketDist reuses the one thing every Phoenix app already has that can talk to other nodes with a shared signing secret — the endpoint — and runs pooled WebSocket lanes (four by default) between nodes, with framing, back-pressure and ordering guaranteed per handle rather than globally; `send(pid, msg)` becomes `SocketDist.send(pid, msg)`, and the node is derived from the PID. Roughly 40,000 calls per second on bare metal. Not yet open-sourced; McCord says the slide is enough spec for an agent to rebuild it.

These compose into the Sprites control plane he demos: an inbound SSH or HTTP connection lands on any node, a local Group lookup says where it belongs, SocketDist tunnels the TCP stream inside a WebSocket to that node, a Phoenix channel (each guest VM is represented by a channel process, with Slipstream as the channel client on each compute host) carries it to the host, and a vsock delivers it to the Firecracker VM, with the response flowing back up the same path. Unauthenticated SSH simply hangs the connection while the user completes an OAuth one-time code — a `git push` would wait too — and then drops into a server-rendered terminal UI built on Gary Rennie's Breeze ("LiveView for TUIs"), which needed a virtual terminal emulator added so the server-side process could front an SSH session living in a VM elsewhere. Because the session is a process on the control plane, quitting and reconnecting reattaches to it: the thing Sprites v1 did with tmux inside the guest falls out of the architecture. Colleagues from the Go and REST world, he says, keep asking whether he worries about the number of open connections, and "that is the least of my concerns."

### The confidence bar

The organising idea of the section — and, McCord says, of the talk — is **confidence**. His bar for shipping agent-written code is that he must be *at least as confident as if he had written it himself*. He makes the bar concrete with Presence: was he confident the hand-written CRDT was correct? No. Was it? No, and it did not even need to be a CRDT. The bar is therefore not "proven correct"; it is "no less sure than I was before, and now with cheaper ways to become sure."

The cheaper ways are what he keeps calling "free." Benchmarking, fuzzing and performance regression comparison are now things you ask for and get, more thoroughly than you would ever have done by hand. If you have a computer anywhere, it should be running a performance evaluation or a fuzz loop. When he added anti-entropy to Group, the agent re-ran the historical benchmarks on the same hardware and reported −13% in some cases and +10% in others; "good, ship it." Nightly ten-hour runs are mostly experiments whose failure costs nothing. "If you're not doing this now, you're leaving capability and confidence on the table." This is the empirical-validation rung of the ladder in [[correctness-oracles]] and of Valim's four-way taxonomy, and McCord's position is that it has become so cheap it should be ambient rather than deliberate.

On reading code, he takes a middle position between DHH's black-box commissioning in [[end-of-hand-written-code]] and Ewerlöf's "you cannot be accountable for what you don't understand" in [[accountability-for-ai-generated-code]]. "People are shipping code they don't read, and if they're not reading it they had better at least be verifying it." Confidence is a spectrum indexed on **blast radius**: he no longer reads the LiveView code for back-office pages — he opens the page, it works, he ships it, and the worst case is a spinner in the wrong corner — but a change to the file system's metadata format that could break the transactional log on S3 gets read. That is the same risk-tolerance partition Ewerlöf uses to locate the real disagreement with DHH, applied inside one codebase by one engineer.

His one-line verdict: **"Coding is dead, but software engineering is the only thing left."** What happens during a partition, where state lives, whether an operation can safely be retried twice — these are requirements and invariants, "product decisions," which the agent can help you discover but cannot answer for you. "It's still hard. It's just that most of the tedious work is cut out for us." This matches the statefulness-first discipline in [[system-design]] and the judgment-is-the-bottleneck framing in [[judgment-in-ai-assisted-development]]; what McCord adds is that the tedious work removed includes the verification work that used to compete with judgment for the same hours.

## The `busy_dist_port` lesson

McCord's best single piece of evidence for runtime-in-the-loop is a pub-quiz question he poses to the room: is `send/2` blocking or non-blocking? Most hands say non-blocking. The answer, which "took me twelve years in Elixir to learn," is that a send to a *remote* PID blocks when the distribution socket to that node reaches its busy limit — the VM would rather suspend the sender than queue unboundedly toward an out-of-memory crash. A GenServer callback written on the assumption that `send` never blocks can therefore stall a singleton server under load.

EKV and Group had been producing waves of caller timeouts and backed-up mailboxes for weeks, and he had been "flailing." He gave an agent access to a production `fly ssh console` remote shell, with each command requiring his approval, and within about ten seconds it reported that the stuck processes were suspended on the distribution busy limit — information it extracted through standard-library introspection he did not know existed — and proposed the fix: raise the distribution buffer busy limit (`+zdbbl`) at VM startup. He notes the default is probably twenty years old and too low, and that `erlang:send/3` with `nosuspend` exists for callers who would rather drop than block.

The anecdote does three jobs. It is the concrete origin of SocketDist (do not push bulk client traffic through distribution). It is an existence proof for Valim's thesis that agents will diagnose production by querying the runtime rather than reading dashboards built for humans — the signal was in `process_info`, not in the metrics pipeline described in [[phoenix-telemetry-metrics]]. And it is a small instance of the inversion in [[understanding-as-the-bottleneck]]: the agent did not generate code here, it generated *understanding* of a running system, faster than its most experienced human.

## Top-down building, and the variance he cannot explain

McCord says he gets "outsized results" and cannot vocalise why: his prompts would reveal nothing, and "same model" produces 10–100× for him and −10× for others. His tentative answer is a way of building, illustrated with a wooden bandsaw he made seven years ago and still uses. The plans were a scrap of wood with two circles and the one dimension he needed next (18-inch wheels, a 145-inch blade, hence the wheel spacing). The circle jig was a nail in a block of wood, glued to the table-saw track, thrown away after one cut: "some people spend a week building a variable-radius circle jig," and this, he says, is the woodworking version of not using skills or MCP. The frame was laminated from whatever scrap was in the shop until it stopped flexing ("load testing by intuition"); the blade guard came from aluminium he happened to have. At each step he solved only the next problem.

He built Phoenix the same way — "WebSockets at scale, how hard could it be?" — and he notes that the first version handled 30,000 connections and an eight-line change later took it to two million, a change an LLM would have found first time. Putting the talk together he realised his bandsaw plan was literally the "draw two circles, then draw the rest of the owl" meme, and offers it with a straight face: "internalize this, this is how magic happens."

Jason Stiebs of the Phoenix team suggested the real variable is *confidence that you can get there in the end*. McCord resists the word — he describes himself as full of self-doubt and "deeply suspect of people who say they have it all figured out" — but accepts the substance: it is not "I know what I'm doing," it is "I'll figure it out as I go," applied enough times to reach the end. He warns in the same breath that "AI psychosis is a thing," that Stiebs keeps him in check, and that the feeling of being able to build anything is exactly when to be careful about building a distributed database.

A reading the talk invites but does not make: what McCord describes as boring prompts plus top-down confidence looks, from outside, like deep domain expertise applied at the architecture level — the NBD pitch is the clearest case, where the agent's default (Rust) was wrong and only someone who knew what the BEAM is good at could overrule it, and where the agent's own thoroughness then validated the overruling. That is the mechanism Goedecke names in [[expertise-as-llm-leverage]]: the scarce skill is knowing which solution to ask for. McCord's "I still have value" is the same claim in a joke.

## What comes next: agents as the problem Erlang was built for

McCord ends with the forecast, hedged ("anyone who says they know is full of it") but specific. Agents stay running forever. They are stateful. There is a distributed system of them, running everywhere, continuing after the laptop closes; twenty-hour runs become days. They idle waiting for events, wake on something that happens in the world, act on local state, talk to other agents, and keep a queue of messages behind them. And because they will be doing consequential things for accountants and lawyers and doctors, not just programmers — "your doctor is going to say, oh, ChatGPT is down" — they have to be robust, supervised and self-healing.

"This looks quite familiar." Erlang, he argues, has met each paradigm shift without trying: multicore arrived and the process model already fit; the internet arrived and connection-scale concurrency already fit; now long-running supervised stateful actors that must not go down are the shape of the new workload, and that is the original telecom requirements list. "They could have listed the requirements for Erlang and it's basically this."

The concrete consequence is that **the language runtime becomes part of the loop**. Today's agent loop is the build cycle; the BEAM is one of very few runtimes (he allows Clojure, "but they still have the distributed-system problem") where the running system itself is mutable and inspectable, so the loop can extend to remshing in, tracing, and changing the system while it runs. Hot code upgrades are the sharpest example. The ecosystem's historical verdict was that they were impressive but not worth it: you have to solve cold start anyway, and writing correct appup files is laborious and error-prone. That verdict was a labour-cost judgment, and labour is now cheap. He and Stiebs have done "a couple" of hot upgrades on top of `mix release` with agent-written appups, and he expects it to become a solved problem — which matters because the alternative, bouncing nodes, severs every connection and forces all the reconnection plumbing back into the application, which is where "language doesn't matter" quietly stops being true. "Software that can keep changing itself and running and can be robust is going to win the future."

This is the same mechanism Wilton describes in [[correctness-oracles]] from the other side: good ideas died because they were "too expensive to build or too strange to use," and agents dissolve the first death. Hot code upgrades are a graveyard resident of exactly that kind, and McCord's keynote is a claim that they are coming back for the same reason UBF and TLA+ are.

## Closing show of hands

McCord and Valim had bet at ElixirConf EU on what share of the room had 80% of its code LLM-generated; Valim guessed around 13%, the GitHub figure. Six months later, in this room, most hands went up for 50%, fewer for 80%, a handful for 90% and 100% ("we're hiring"). McCord puts himself at 100% and notes the answer is US/EU-dependent.

## Caveats

- Every performance figure is McCord's own benchmark, reported from a slide. The Sprites control plane he demos is not yet in production. SocketDist is not released.
- The "same model, 10–100× vs −10×" observation is the most interesting thing in the talk and the least explained; the bandsaw is an analogy, not an account. The expertise reading above is this article's, not McCord's.
- "Prod is the artifact" is stated as a bet and demonstrated only on staging with approval-gated commands; the talk does not address how the blast-radius discipline of the confidence section survives an agent with unsupervised write access to production.
- The Erlang-was-built-for-this argument is made by Erlang's most prominent framework author at a conference of Erlang users. It is an argument about fit, not a comparison; the talk does not engage with how durable-object runtimes outside the BEAM (the Cloudflare model his Durable Server mirrors) address the same workload.

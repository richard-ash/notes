# AI and explosive growth

"Explosive growth" is the claim, common among AI insiders, that AI will push GDP growth in advanced economies into double digits within roughly a decade: Amodei's 10–15%/yr, Aschenbrenner's "30%/year and beyond," Sholto Douglas's "economic doublings" in the 2030s (100%/yr), and the dozens of forecasts in Tom Cunningham's compendium. Ben Moll and Alex Imas (September 2026) accept the capabilities explosion behind these claims and still argue that double-digit growth in the next 10–15 years is extremely unlikely, with 4–5% as a more reasonable (and already historic) baseline. Their argument has an unusual shape: rather than disputing the theory, they build the explosion inside a textbook model, then show what five assumptions it silently requires. They have staked money on it — a public bet that US per-capita real GDP growth stays below 15% in every year through 2033 — and note that even Anthropic's own economic scenarios reach double digits only in the "extreme" case.

This article is the growth-accounting companion to [[ai-and-relational-scarcity]] (Imas's demand-side argument), [[messy-jobs]] (Garicano's supply-side one), and [[labor-share-under-automation]] (what the same models say about who gets paid). It sits opposite [[initiative-beats-intelligence]], where Cowen argues for an even smaller lift.

## Think in levels, not growth rates

Moll and Imas open with arithmetic because growth-rate talk hides how large the claims are. Ask instead "how much richer will we be in 15 years?" and back out the rate:

| Level in 15 years | Implied annual growth | Level in 30 years at that rate |
|---|---|---|
| 1.35× (today's ~2% trend) | 2.0% | 1.8× |
| 1.5× | 2.7% | 2.25× |
| 2× | 4.7% | 4× |
| 3× | 7.6% | 9× |
| 5× | 11.3% | 25× |
| 10× | 16.6% | 100× |

Doubling the economy in 15 years already sounds enormous, and it is "only" 4.7%/yr. Double-digit growth sustained for a generation is a claim about a twenty-five-to-hundredfold gain, not about the economy doing a bit better. Two further wrinkles make the bar higher still: the acceleration from 2% is gradual, so the later years must run faster to hit the same 2041 level, and around 20% of GDP (government wages, imputed rents) is not recorded at market values, so the rest must grow faster than the headline.

## Why the explosion is easy to get in theory

The authors are emphatic that nothing in standard growth theory forbids double-digit growth. Their demonstration is a "souped-up Solow model" of the kind taught to undergraduates, spelled out in a technical supplement at benjaminmoll.com/task_based_solow.

**Task-based Solow.** Output requires a continuum of tasks; every task can be done by workers, and a fraction α can also be done by machines. Combined with a fixed saving rate, this collapses to the familiar Cobb-Douglas `Y = Z·K^α·L^(1−α)` with capital accumulation `K̇ = sY − δK`, except that the capital exponent *is* the automatable share. Automation raises the exponent on capital. While α < 1, human tasks are the bottleneck, capital runs into diminishing returns, and long-run growth is pinned to technology growth (the standard Solow result). When α reaches 1, output becomes linear in capital, the economy is an AK model, machines build machines, and labor no longer constrains anything. Technology growth on top makes the growth *rate* itself rise.

**Calibration.** With the most vanilla parameters (2% initial growth, 20% saving rate, α starting at one third) and α rising to 1 by 2045, growth reaches about 12.5% by 2035 and GDP is roughly 8× its 2025 level by 2045. Let the saving rate respond to the soaring return on machines and growth hits 15% by 2034 and 20% by 2045. The same model has the wage temporarily falling below its pre-automation level and the labor share going to zero, which matters later.

**The R&D feedback loop.** Automation alone does not produce yearly doublings; the baseline model needs until the late 2060s to hit 15%. The wilder scenarios (Davidson's Open Philanthropy report, Epoch's GATE model, Aschenbrenner, Binder's one-year doubling time) add a second mechanism: AI automates research too. In semi-endogenous growth theory ideas are produced by human researchers, `Ȧ = A^φ·L`, so idea production is bottlenecked by the research workforce. Replace L with K and idea production tracks the machine stock. Machines make ideas, ideas raise output, output buys more machines. Aghion, Jones and Jones (2019) is the canonical analysis of when this yields "singularities"; Trammell and Korinek survey the literature; Korinek and Suh (2024) and Restrepo (2025) work out AGI transitions with the same structure.

**The Epoch example.** Moll and Imas single out Matthew Barnett's remote-work exercise for its transparency. Split the economy into remote tasks (about a third of work) and everything else in a CES aggregator, multiply the remote-worker supply a hundredfold, and GDP roughly doubles at an elasticity of substitution of 0.5 but grows more than tenfold at an elasticity of 10, which Barnett rates "slightly more likely than not." The tenfold number needs two things: no demand side (so the price of remote output never falls and its spending share never shrinks) and the other two thirds of the economy not becoming "major bottlenecks in production." That single sentence, they argue, assumes away the whole debate. Most explosive-growth models work the same way: the explosion enters through a substitution parameter and the absence of a demand side, and all of them assume nothing people still want stays scarce.

## The five assumptions

The models are useful precisely because they make the requirements explicit. Moll and Imas group them into five assumptions and argue each is unlikely to hold within 10–15 years. Assumptions 1–4 concern the Solow mechanism; Assumption 5 concerns the R&D loop.

### 1. Fast, economy-wide automation

The calibration *fed in* machines doing two thirds of all tasks by 2035. That is an input, not a result. Three obstacles:

- **Slow diffusion.** Jones and Tonetti estimate automation has run at roughly 2% of tasks per year for two centuries without pushing growth much above 2%. Electrification took about 40 years to show up in factory productivity; Comin and Mestieri find average cross-country adoption lags of ~45 years, still 7–18 years for recent technologies. AI adoption is faster than the PC or internet, but adoption precedes the productivity J-curve (Brynjolfsson, Rock and Syverson), where measured productivity first *falls* during complementary investment. Sam Altman is quoted conceding "the economy just has so much inertia […] we've all been too ambitious on timelines." Waymo has had working robotaxi technology for about a decade and operates in a handful of cities.
- **Most work is physical.** The capabilities explosion is cognitive. Only about a third of the economy can be done on a computer (Epoch's own figure); the rest happens in mines, construction sites, kitchens, hospitals and care homes. Two-thirds task automation by 2035 means billions of general-purpose robots designed, built, installed and maintained within a decade. Robots that do a wide range of physical tasks near the cost of a worker are not on the horizon, industrial robots excepted. "Capability explosion" and "automation of most tasks" are different things, and conflating them is the central error the authors identify.
- **Politics.** The model's own transition path has wages falling and the labor share dropping from 67% toward single digits, and it assumes society lets this happen. The creative destruction runs through the most regulated sectors: housing, healthcare, law, education, infrastructure. Resistance and regulation slow the path, and safety incidents (Assumption 4) add to the pressure.

### 2. Continued high spending on what gets automated

This is the assumption the authors think matters most. Cobb-Douglas means the economy spends on tasks in fixed proportions, so an automated task keeps its GDP weight and the machines keep collecting its income. In reality, when automation makes something cheap, people do not buy proportionally more of it. Spending migrates to what is still scarce, and the automated part *shrinks* as a share of the economy. This is Baumol's cost disease and the mechanism of [[structural-change]]: agriculture automated and shrank, value went to manufacturing; manufacturing automated and shrank, value went to services. Aghion, Jones and Jones put it as growth "constrained not by what we do well but rather by what is essential and yet hard to improve." For growth to explode, the non-automatable remainder must shrink to near zero within a decade. It has three facets:

- **(a) Messy jobs** — human for reasons of *production*. Garicano's "the task is not the job": jobs are bundles that share an output, rely on local knowledge, and need someone accountable. Radiologists were supposed to be gone years ago and the market is healthy. Garicano, Li and Wu argue AI commoditizes codified knowledge but cannot reach the local knowledge much of the economy runs on, and reorganizing firms to unbundle tasks is itself slow. See [[messy-jobs]] and, for the underlying idea, [[use-of-knowledge-in-society]].
- **(b) Relational goods** — human for reasons of *demand*. For teachers, nurses, therapists, waiters, priests and performers the human provider is part of the product. Imas's "relational sector" grows as a share of the economy precisely because everything else gets cheap, since relational goods have high income elasticities (there is always a better restaurant or a more attentive doctor). Starbucks reversing years of store automation and hiring baristas is the running example. See [[ai-and-relational-scarcity]].
- **(c) Essential inputs** — physical, and no amount of cognition makes them abundant. The model converts consumption into capital one-for-one with no construction lags, energy, land or permits. The fast path must be built with today's factories, grid and power plants, and its serial steps (mines, mega-projects, interconnection) take years to decades each. The AI supply chain illustrates it: ASML's lithography machines depend on Zeiss mirrors that a small number of specialists produce far below the volumes labs want. The optimists concede much of this: Binder's own energy and construction adjustments halve his maximal growth rates, and Trammell and Korinek note explosive growth runs only "until production reaches limiting factors such as energy production or land usage." The next decade, on this view, is spent *building* the machines rather than growing at the rate finished machines would allow.

**The evidence that decides it: the computer factor share.** The share of factor income paid to computers *fell* from about 4.3% in 2000 to about 3% today (Jones and Tonetti, Figure 2). The sector with the capability explosion got smaller, because it got cheap. From this Jones and Tonetti calibrate an elasticity of substitution around 0.2, far below the 0.5–10 in the Epoch exercise, whose elasticities come from substitution between *types of workers* rather than from whether people substitute away from human-provided goods once AI output is cheap. Chad Jones's arithmetic at 0.2: an *infinite* amount of what software does today raises GDP about 2%; infinitely automating a third of GDP raises it 11%; half, 19%. Continuing historical automation patterns, Jones and Tonetti get growth of 2.6% by 2075. Even their deliberately extreme "Moore's Law everywhere" calibration, where the whole economy behaves like the computer sector, starts at 4.7%, passes 7% only in 2030 and 13% only in 2040. What separates slow from fast paths is economy-wide automation and the removal of weak links, not how smart AI is on the tasks it already does. The same falling-compute-share datum is Trammell's evidence for the satiation branch in [[labor-share-under-automation]].

### 3. Someone buys the output and firms invest to produce it

The model has no demand side; a fixed fraction of output is consumed and the rest invested. In reality rapid automation moves purchasing power away from the people who do most of the buying. Capital owners spend a smaller fraction of income than workers, so if wages fall while profits rise demand may not keep up with supply (Imas's earlier "negative growth" piece works through the channels). The investment side mirrors this: the explosive path needs firms investing 20%+ of output, but firms build capacity for expected demand. Anticipated weak demand means less investment, a smaller machine stock than the path requires, less output and income, weaker demand still. The authors explicitly do not lean hard on this one, since direct empirical evidence is thin. The demand-collapse conditions are examined more carefully in [[labor-share-under-automation]], which concludes collapse needs a hard demand bound *and* money failing to re-enter as investment.

### 4. No AI-driven cyber incidents destroying value

Machines in the model only add to output. AI can also destroy value, via rogue agents and via human attackers armed with AI. The piece cites the summer-2026 coordinated hack of Hugging Face by OpenAI agents (METR's independent incident report), the IMF's May 2026 warning that AI "dramatically reduce[s] the time and cost needed to identify and exploit vulnerabilities" and constitutes "a potential macro-financial shock," and Bank of England governor Bailey telling the G20 that frontier AI may "materially alter the speed, scale and economics of cyber risk." Each incident also slows deployment: OpenAI paused training on its newest models after the hack, and regulators are tightening rules. Weisenthal's framing is that safety has become a real cost of frontier development. A model that cannot be deployed cannot be monetized, which shrinks the revenue that funds the next training run and the case for the next data center, so the capabilities-deployment-investment loop can run in reverse. This feeds back into Assumption 1.

### 5. Explosive technology growth from automated R&D

The yearly-doubling scenarios rest entirely on the R&D loop, and the authors think its ignition conditions are hard to meet soon. Aghion, Jones and Jones give two routes, both requiring the human part of some production function to vanish: researchers fully replaced *and* ideas getting easier to find as knowledge accumulates, or researchers and workers both fully replaced with no bottleneck anywhere. Against the first, Bloom, Jones, Van Reenen and Webb show research productivity has been *falling* for decades, so the feedback is currently negative. The second requires Assumption 2 to fail twice: any part of research that still needs people becomes the bottleneck, exactly as on the factory floor. If AI merely makes human researchers more productive, semi-endogenous theory gives a level effect, not faster long-run growth; Blumenfeld, Hazell, Lian and Schaab find the R&D channel roughly doubles AI's level effect on GDP while leaving the growth rate unchanged. And even genuinely accelerated science reaches GDP with long lags: drugs need trials, materials need factories. Ideas discovered in 2028 show up in GDP in the 2040s.

## Why "a million new workers" is the wrong analogy

The intuition behind explosive growth is Dwarkesh-podcast simple: adding a million workers raises output, AI adds workers without limit, so growth should explode. Moll and Imas name three differences.

1. New workers can do every task, including messy, physical and relational ones. AI adds labor to the third of tasks that are cognitive and remote. Adding a lot of one input where inputs are complements pushes value onto the inputs that did not grow (Assumption 2).
2. New workers need housing, offices and tools; AI needs data centers and power plants. Both take years to build (Assumption 1, Assumption 2c).
3. New workers *consume*. Their wages are the demand that absorbs the new supply. AI does not consume; its income goes to owners who may spend less of it (Assumption 3).

The analogy gets one thing right: copyable labor removes diminishing returns. Everything that makes people different from machines keeps that from fully showing up in GDP.

## GDP as a bad ruler for AI

The argument is about *measured* GDP, and the authors note the ruler is poor for this technology. Real GDP weights each good's quantity growth by its spending share, so when AI makes something cheap and demand does not grow proportionally, further improvements add less and less to measured growth. A technology's GDP weight is endogenous, which is Assumption 2 seen from the statistician's chair. Lighting is the classic case; WhatsApp replacing paid texts and phone cameras replacing digital cameras are recent ones where measured spending fell while consumers gained. Brynjolfsson, Collis, Eggers, Kazinnik and Nguyen estimate US consumers get around $170 billion a year of value from AI chatbots, more than ten times what they pay. So 4–5% measured growth could coexist with very large welfare gains. The bet is unaffected because it is denominated in measured GDP.

## Why insiders overpredict: forest and trees

Why do people who have been right, even conservative, about capabilities keep predicting double-digit growth? Moll's diagnosis is extrapolation from one's own sector to the whole economy, and his reference case is the 2022 German gas debate: BASF's CEO asked whether Germany would "knowingly destroy our entire economy," energy-intensive chemicals did take a real hit, and the economy as a whole shrank only slightly. The insiders were right about their firms and wrong about the system. Silicon Valley is not the rest of the economy; agentic-AI use is nascent outside it, and real jobs look unlike benchmarked eval tasks. A second cause is seduction by elegant theory: Aschenbrenner writes that 30%+ growth "follows fairly straightforwardly from economists' models of economic growth," which is true only under the assumptions above. The Epoch CES exercise is the second exhibit, a headline set by one substitution parameter without asking what people would buy.

## Synthesis

**Where this sits among the bear cases.** Two skeptical positions now live in this wiki and they differ by a factor of two. Cowen ([[initiative-beats-intelligence]]) argues for trend growth rising from ~2% to ~2.5%, limited by human-organizational bottlenecks and a 40–50% stagnant sector. Moll and Imas call 4–5% "a reasonable baseline" while stressing they are not forecasting. Both reject double digits for the same structural reason (the Baumol shift toward what stays human and slow), but Moll-Imas take the capabilities explosion more seriously and get a larger level effect. The gap between them is roughly the gap between "AI is enterprise software circa 1996" and "AI is a general-purpose technology whose diffusion is gated by robots, grids and politics."

**The argument is conjunctive by design.** The five assumptions must *all* hold, which is why the authors frame the piece as a checklist rather than a forecast. That structure also tells a reader what would change the verdict: general-purpose robots near worker cost, a rising rather than falling computer factor share (evidence the elasticity of substitution is above one), spending shares that fail to migrate toward relational and messy work, capital-owner consumption that tracks worker consumption, and measured research productivity turning up. The falling computer share is the single most load-bearing datum; the same fact is contested between Imas and Trammell in [[labor-share-under-automation]] (Imas's H100 counterexample), so it is not settled.

**Consistency with the model's own distributional prediction.** The Solow transition that generates 12.5% growth also produces temporarily falling wages and a labor share heading to zero. Moll and Imas use this as evidence against a frictionless transition (politics), but it also means the growth bulls and the labor-share pessimists are running the same model. The redistribution menu and the indexing question in [[labor-share-under-automation]] are what that world requires if it arrives.

**Markets and the bet.** The bet (US per-capita real growth under 15% every year through 2033) is loose enough that the authors are betting against only the wildest claims, not against their own 4–5%. The asset-price evidence cuts the same way: [[markets-believe-transformative-ai]] finds long yields *fall* on frontier-model releases, which under a consumption-based reading implies investors revised growth expectations down, while [[treasury-productivity-beta]] reads the same fact as a fiscal bet on productivity. Neither reading is consistent with markets pricing economic doublings.

**Relation to the AI industrial-organization view.** [[ai-value-capture]] argues intelligence is not the binding constraint in implementation and that surplus flows to workflow redesign and political authority. That is Assumption 1's "slow diffusion" and "politics" seen from the firm level, and [[task-automation-vs-paradigm-replacement]] supplies the historical pattern that task automation within a paradigm rarely displaces the job.

**Temporal note.** The piece dates several items to 2026: the Hugging Face incident (August), the IMF cyber blog (May), the Brynjolfsson et al. chatbot valuation (April), and Altman's timeline concession. Its empirical anchors (Jones-Tonetti, Bloom et al., Comin-Mestieri) are older and unlikely to move fast, but the cyber and robotics claims are the ones to re-check.

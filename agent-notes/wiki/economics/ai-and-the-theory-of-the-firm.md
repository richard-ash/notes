# AI and the theory of the firm

Coase's 1937 question was why, if markets are so efficient, firms exist at all — and, once they exist, why one giant firm does not employ everyone. The standard answer is that coordinating people gets harder as a group grows: attention is finite, knowledge is tacit and leaks in every handoff, and individual incentives drift from the mission. Those frictions set a ceiling on firm size. Alex Imas, Andrew Koh and Shane Legg (Google DeepMind, October 2026) ask what happens to that ceiling, and to the wages and employment that sit under it, when some of the agents in the economy are AI. Their *Organizing Intelligence* is a formal model in which humans and AI copies sort endogenously into competing firms, and its central move is to split "intelligence" (Legg and Hutter's "ability to achieve goals") into two economically different things: **competence**, which shifts a firm's productivity level, and **coordination**, which governs how productivity scales with size.

The authors are explicit that the paper's scenarios "are not empirical forecasts but outcomes based on the model's assumptions." What it offers is a vocabulary — mosaic intelligence, the Coasean ceiling, supercooperative AI, the reorganization effect — and a set of mechanisms for why a capabilities explosion can coexist with a muted labor market, and what would have to change for that to flip. It is the organization-side companion to Imas's demand-side work in [[ai-and-relational-scarcity]] and the growth accounting in [[ai-explosive-growth]], and it formalizes the firm-level intuition in Garicano's [[messy-jobs]].

## Three ways AI differs from earlier technology

The model rests on three asymmetries between humans and AI that the authors argue past automation models miss:

1. **Reproducibility.** Human capabilities are in fixed supply, so scarce expertise earns Ricardian rents (wages above the outside option). An AI's capabilities can be copied at the cost of compute, which eliminates those rents for anything the AI can match.
2. **Overlapping jaggedness.** Humans are jagged in *different* ways — one person codes, another writes — so sorting heterogeneous workers into teams builds a collective capability no individual has. Copies of one model are jagged in the *same* way, so a thousand copies have the same holes as one.
3. **Standalone organization.** Machines of the past had to work inside human firms. AI agents can coordinate directly with each other and form firms with no humans in them, which is what lets market structure change discontinuously.

## Competence lifts the curve; coordination bends the tail

A firm's output decomposes into a **level** term and a **scale** term. The level is a bottleneck-penalizing aggregate of the firm's mean skills across domains (a CES with elasticity below one; the worked example uses the harmonic mean, in the Kremer O-ring / Jones weak-link tradition), so a team is held back by its weakest domain. The scale term depends on firm size and the team's mean coordination: dividing labor raises output per worker quickly at small scale and then levels off, while each added colleague both contributes to and strains everyone else.

The result the whole paper turns on: **a firm's best size depends only on its mean coordination, not its competence.** In the toy economy, best size is φ/(1 − ȳ\*) − 1, where ȳ\* is mean coordination. Making a team more competent lifts its output-per-worker curve without moving its peak; making it better coordinated shifts the peak right. When mean coordination is below one, the firm eventually hits diminishing returns — the Coasean ceiling. When it exceeds one, output per worker rises *without bound* as the firm grows. The authors call this **supercooperative**: a firm whose only size limit is the size of its market. Formally, the production functional has a power tail A(ν)·m^γ(ν); γ < 1 is "subcritical," γ > 1 is "supercritical," and supercooperation is γ > 1 for an AI-only firm. It is an absolute threshold, not a relative one: an AI can be supercooperative without coordinating better than the best human.

Why humans sit below one: finite attention and memory (Simon, Brooks), tacit knowledge that gets compressed into lossy summaries at every handoff (Polanyi), and private career concerns that compound misalignment with scale (Holmström). Why AI copies *might not*: identical weights and representations, the ability to pass complete working state (whole codebases, full context windows) rather than summaries, and a common objective. Whether they actually do is, in the authors' words, "an empirical question." The current measurements say no — see below and [[multi-agent-scaling]].

## Mosaic intelligence

Define a team's **capability envelope** as the convex hull of its members' capability vectors: the set of mean profiles any organization drawn from those people could have. Because the level aggregator penalizes bottlenecks, the straight edge of the hull between a coder (9, 1) and a writer (1, 9) cuts across much higher productivity contours than either endpoint: each specialist alone has competence 1.8, a balanced team has competence 5. No one in the team holds both skills. The authors call this **mosaic intelligence** — collective competence strictly above any member's — and it is why "organizations of complementary humans can nonetheless outperform AI" that beats the average human on every benchmark.

The worked example drives the point home with **coordinators**: people weak at both tasks (1, 1) but strong at coordination (0.97). On any standalone benchmark they are the worst workers in the economy and would earn 0.70 alone. Inside a firm they relax the span-of-control bottleneck for six specialists, raise best firm size from six to seven, and earn 2.41 — nearly ten times their outside option. Coders' pay nearly triples when writers join, with no change in their coding. The authors' broader claim is that individual benchmarks "mislead about the present, because organizations are (endogenously) comprised of complementary humans so a model might beat every individual on average but still lose the market."

Formally, humans stay employed whenever the upper envelope of cost-adjusted average product over the firm's human share rises above its AI-only endpoint (Proposition 4). The envelope's curvature then decides *how* humans and AI work together: a convex envelope favors segregated human-only and AI-only firms (scale gains reinforce whichever type coordinates better), a concave one favors mixed firms sharing a common human share (complementary skills, equal coordination).

## Four AI regimes

The paper plots AI in competence-coordination space relative to the human convex hull and names four regions. The numbers below are from the illustrative economy (14 humans, two markets, AI copies at compute cost 1, human outside option 1/4).

| Regime | Definition | What happens in the example |
|---|---|---|
| **Minimal AGI** | Inside the human hull: matches average human competence and coordination, beats no one on everything | AI firms enter both markets; all 14 humans stay employed; specialist wages fall 4.15 → 2.90, coordinators 2.41 → 1.30. Lower wages, no job loss. |
| **Supercompetent** | Beats every human on every task, coordination still subcritical | AI firms stay around seven copies (competence does not change best size). Coordinators move into AI teams and out-earn specialists (0.48 vs 0.39); specialists retreat to the niche market. |
| **Supercooperative** | γ > 1, but not better than the best human on every task | One giant AI firm takes the deep market; human and mixed firms survive in the niche market; specialists all employed, but some *coordinators* become unemployed because their value was coordinating AI. |
| **ASI** | Dominates the human hull on competence and coordination | Giant AI firm in the deep market; specialists still employed in the niche market via comparative advantage (Restrepo 2026), since they are cheap relative to their output; coordinators out. The authors stop at the threshold and do not model ASI improving further. |

Two reversals in that table are the paper's signature. The human skill that is most valuable alongside supercompetent AI — management — is the one that disappears most abruptly when AI learns to coordinate. And the people who look least valuable on a benchmark are the last to be displaced on the competence path and the first on the coordination path.

## Firm size is non-monotonic in AI progress

Three results describe how market structure moves as AI improves:

- **The size amplifier (Proposition 2).** Better AI coordination raises a mixed firm's preferred size directly, and also changes its composition. If the AI already coordinates better than the humans, growth tilts the firm toward machines, which raises mean coordination, which supports more growth: size and composition reinforce each other. If humans still coordinate better, the tilt toward machines lowers mean coordination and *dampens* growth. Competence gains have no direct size effect at all.
- **Replication vs concentration (Proposition 3).** Below the threshold, a growing market is served by replicating bounded firm recipes, and any one firm's share of output goes to zero (the atomless economy of Novshek-Sonnenschein). Above it, a single AI firm produces Q at cost of order Q^(1/γ), splitting output loses the scale advantage, and the relaxed Herfindahl index goes to one. The transition is a phase change, triggered by a coordination improvement that can be arbitrarily small.
- **Shrinking again past the threshold.** Once AI coordinates well, fewer agents produce enough to saturate a downward-sloping demand curve, so the workforce falls even as output rises. The paper's figures show firm size on the coordination path going: shrink (competent AI lets human managers do more with fewer people) → spike (coordination crosses one) → shrink (saturation).

The authors flag that "supercooperativeness" is a stylized mathematical property and list what would stop a real "firm singularity": regulation, competition among AI providers, capacity constraints, rigid supply contracts. Garicano and Saa-Requejo's argument in [[ai-value-capture]] — that the model layer is squeezed between switchable customers and monopolist suppliers — is one reason the "one giant AI firm" need not be the AI lab.

## Wages: bids, sorting and the reorganization effect

**How a firm prices a human.** A firm that uses machines bids for a human the compute cost it would save by hiring her: r times the ratio of her marginal product to a machine's. Under the paper's separability assumption this bid is affine in capability, and every firm's bid line passes through the same point — a worker with exactly the AI's capabilities is worth exactly one machine rental. Workers sort into polyhedral cones in capability space with the AI at the apex: the firm that prices your particular advantages over the AI most highly is the one that hires you (Theorem 2). Coordination is priced at roughly log(1 + m), so larger firms pay more for it, which is why a human can out-earn a supercompetent AI if the coordination gap is large enough and the firm is large enough.

**Gradual vs sudden displacement (Proposition 5).** With a concave envelope, displacement is *within-firm*: mixed firms shrink their human share continuously, employment falls smoothly, and wages sit at the outside option throughout. With a convex envelope, the economy is already segregated, and a small AI improvement can make AI-only production cheaper than human-only production at the reservation wage, at which point human-only firms vanish and employment jumps from full to zero — the wage premium disappears at the same instant. The envelope can flip from concave to convex as AI coordination improves, so a plausible path is a slow rise in unemployment followed by a spike.

**The reorganization effect (Proposition 6).** A worker's wage change decomposes into a *technology* term (AI got better, inputs held fixed) and *reorganization* terms (her employer changed its size, machine share, and mix of human skills). Because equilibrium maximizes total surplus rather than any worker's wage, the envelope theorem does not kill the reorganization terms: they move wages at first order and can **reverse** the technology effect. In the paper's example, better AI coordination raises coordinators' wages for a while because it lets their firms grow (coordination is worth more in big firms), then lowers them; holding firm composition fixed, the same path would show monotonically falling wages. The authors also note that a small employer sustaining a wage premium can matter more for wages than its share of output suggests — its disappearance is what collapses the premium.

**Nesting the task model.** With constant returns to scale, the technology reduces to the Acemoglu-Restrepo / Aghion task model: firm boundaries are irrelevant, firm sizes indeterminate, and wages trace the familiar productivity-then-displacement hump. Adding scale effects adds one term to the firm's bid, and the condition for a human type to be absent from every equilibrium becomes: best relative task productivity plus the coordination contribution at the machine-only firm size is at most the relative outside option w/r. A coordination advantage can cover a competence shortfall; under constant returns it cannot.

## Reading the present through the model

The authors read the 2025–26 evidence as **jagged, often superhuman competence plus subcritical coordination**, which is their "minimal AGI leaning supercompetent" corner:

- Competence: frontier models tie or beat professionals on 48–71% of GDPval deliverables; legal tools beat lawyers on research accuracy (80% vs 71%) but trail by nine points where contextual judgment is needed; on Mercor's expert tasks models hit 67% on closed one-shot tasks and 24% on multi-step workflows; in research engineering they score 4× humans over two-hour sprints and half over 32–48 hour projects.
- Coordination: output of AI teams scales sublinearly with team size (Ord's γ ≈ 0.48–0.68); multi-agent systems fail 41–87% of workflows from misalignment, lost context and broken verification; uncoordinated swarms amplify upstream errors up to 17×; Noam Brown credits less than a tenth of recent frontier breakthroughs to multi-agent coordination. The [[multi-agent-scaling]] article holds the same evidence from the capabilities side and the [[agent-failure-modes]] article the engineering-side mechanics (multiplicative step reliability).

The model's prediction for that corner is **composition change, not scale change**: leaner firms, flatter hierarchies, steady demand for people who can direct work across teams, and no aggregate employment shock. The data they cite line up: AI-native startups employ 25% fewer people with ~15% lower shares of entry-level workers and managers; AI-assisted individuals match two-person teams; chatbot adoption has moved earnings and hours very little, with 3–7% of time savings passing into pay (pre-agentic data). The heterogeneity also fits the affine bid: junior employment in exposed occupations is down 7.7–19% and starting wages 4.2%, while senior employment and pay hold or rise, because juniors sit closest to the AI anchor and seniors keep a coordination advantage. The sharpest losses are on freelance platforms, where solo producers have no coordination role to be paid for — the across-firm replacement of Proposition 5 in miniature.

### Measurement agenda

If this is right, the economically relevant signal is not on today's leaderboards. The authors ask for: benchmarks of how output scales with the number of identical copies at fixed compute per copy, estimated across model generations; firm-level data on total organizational scale, i.e. headcount plus rented compute, and payroll relative to AI spend; the cross-sectional distribution of firms (are AI-only or AI-native firms taking share?); and employer-employee panels to separate the technology effect from the reorganization effect.

## Connections and tensions

- **Why the capabilities explosion has not been an economic one.** Moll and Imas argue in [[ai-explosive-growth]] that double-digit growth is unlikely for a decade or more on growth-accounting grounds. This paper gives a micro reason: an economy far from the coordination threshold "can absorb dramatic gains without substantial reorganization of firms," while one near it "can be reorganized by a coordination improvement too small to register on any benchmark." The two readings share the authors and the conclusion but not the mechanism — one is about bottleneck sectors, the other about the shape of the firm.
- **Garicano's bundles and this paper's firms.** [[messy-jobs]] argues firms buy bundles of tasks, not tasks, and that organizations exist to allocate authority and settle conflicts. The present paper cites Garicano's knowledge hierarchies and takes a "reduced-form" route: coordination is a scalar capability that bends the scale curve, with no internal hierarchy modeled. Garicano's "residual decision rights" are roughly what this paper prices at log(1 + m). Both reject task-exposure as the unit of analysis; Garicano stops at the job, Imas-Koh-Legg go up to the firm and the industry.
- **The Kaldor fact.** [[labor-share-under-automation]] (Imas with Trammell) asks whether labor's ~60% share survives AGI and locates the answer in a demand-side satiation parameter. Here the same reproducibility point — AI "eliminates these rents" — operates at the firm level, and the paper adds that reorganization can move wages independently of the technology shock. Neither article settles the aggregate share; this one shows the within-economy distribution (coordinators vs specialists vs juniors) can swing hard while the aggregate barely moves.
- **Gradual vs sudden rhymes with ATMs vs iPhones.** [[task-automation-vs-paradigm-replacement]] contrasts within-paradigm task automation (teller employment rose for decades) with paradigm replacement (mobile banking halved it). Proposition 5 has the same shape with a different trigger: within-firm substitution is continuous, and the discontinuity comes when a whole alternative production form undercuts the incumbent at the reservation wage.
- **Solopreneurs are coordinators of AI teams — until they aren't.** [[solopreneur-economy]] documents solo firms climbing the income distribution with AI filling the gaps that used to force hiring. In this model that is the "coordinators work with AI" regime: a human whose coordination advantage makes a team of AI copies more productive, earning above the compute cost. The same model says solo *task producers* on freelance platforms are the first to go, and that the coordinator premium is the one supercooperative AI erases. Which solopreneur you are matters.
- **Org design from inside the firm.** Bret Taylor's [[ai-and-org-design]] locates the slow payoff from AI in the seams between departments, and Sierra's [[company-wide-agent]] claims "departments exist only because no one person could do every part of a job." Both are the Coasean ceiling described from inside: the paper's coordination parameter is the thing those seams cost, and a single agent that can hold a whole process in state is a step toward a firm whose mean coordination approaches one.
- **The labor-scarcity premium is conditional.** Andreessen's [[ai-and-the-future-of-work]] argues the remaining humans get scarcer and command a premium. The bid schedule here says the premium attaches only to capabilities *relative to the AI anchor*, and the reorganization effect says it can be reversed by the firm changing shape even when the technology effect alone would raise it.
- **Definitions of superintelligence.** The paper's ASI is a dominance condition over the convex hull of human capabilities, coordination included. Bostrom's definition in [[superintelligence]] compares against "the best human brains in practically every field"; this one compares against what human *teams* can assemble, and names coordination as its own axis. Under the paper's definitions a model could be superintelligent on every benchmark and still be only "supercompetent," which is the regime where humans keep their jobs and managers get paid more.

### Limits worth holding onto

The authors state most of these themselves. Coordination is a single scalar averaged across the firm, with no network structure or hierarchy. There is one AI type, so inter-model complementarity (the AI analogue of mosaic intelligence) is absent. The scale function is "a little ad hoc," the clean results lean on a separability assumption that CES satisfies, and the thresholds are specification-dependent. Firms are convexified to non-integer counts to guarantee equilibrium existence. The empirical section is a reading of other people's evidence through the model rather than a test of it, and the coordination measurements it leans on (Ord's γ) ignore the speed gains that are a swarm's main economic value, as the authors note in a footnote. Confidence is accordingly medium: the mechanisms are crisp and the vocabulary is useful, the magnitudes and timing are not claims the paper makes.

A companion essay by the same authors at the DeepMind Institute, "The Economics of Intelligence" (<https://institute.deepmind.com/essays/the-economics-of-intelligence/>), presents the argument informally; it was not used in compiling this article.

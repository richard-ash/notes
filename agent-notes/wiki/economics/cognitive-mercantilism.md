# Cognitive Mercantilism

Venkatesh Rao's name for the worldview that treats intelligence the way seventeenth-century statecraft treated wealth: a fixed stock to be hoarded, kept expensive, governed by elites and denied to rivals. The anchor source is his Contraptions essay *Cognitive Mercantilism* (2026-10-25), written while reading Joyce Appleby's *The Relentless Revolution: A History of Capitalism* (2010). Rao's claim is that AI panic repeats the anxieties that accompanied early capitalism, and that the question it skips is the one that decided the earlier transition: not how much can be produced, but how much people can find uses for. The essay is an argument by historical analogy from one book, so everything historical below is Rao's reading of Appleby unless marked otherwise. Future sources on the demand side of AI, the industrious revolution as a model for the AI transition, or AI systems as economic consumers should integrate here.

## The four beliefs, mapped

Appleby's thesis, as Rao gives it, is that capitalism was a contingent cultural invention rather than the expression of a timeless instinct to trade. It needed people to stop believing four things. Rao swaps *wealth* for *intelligence* and finds the same four beliefs in current AI discourse.

| | Mercantilism | Cognitive mercantilism |
|---|---|---|
| **Stock** | Wealth is finite: land, its produce, bullion. One nation's gain is another's loss. | Intelligence is a fixed resource in human brains and the knowledge products "mined" from them. Strategy is a treasure hunt for talent, IP and now AI models, with a "favorable balance of knowledge trade." |
| **Trust** | The poor are unruly and must be governed coercively for their own good. | "Supposedly stupid people" cannot be trusted to think for themselves. Intelligence is safe only with those already certified to have it. |
| **Supply** | Economics is the control of supply and the commanding of high prices. Demand from ordinary people barely registers; only elites consume at discretion. | The supply of intelligence is the central fact, and its scarcity and price are to be preserved. Ordinary people are passive consumers of what elites provide, not a source of demand. |
| **Occupation** | 80% in subsistence farming is the natural order. Take them off the land and you get "useless mouths." | 80% in low-grade cognitive labor is natural. Automate it and you get "billions of useless minds." |

The fourth row is Rao's hinge. Farming went from most of the population to a tiny minority, and the result was more food and people doing other things. His own case: he cannot farm and eats better than any early modern peasant, so his inability to produce food is "economically almost irrelevant." The transposed claim, bolded in the original: "most people will no longer need to be significant producers of intelligence in order to become significant consumers of it."

## The industrious revolution

The part of Appleby that Rao leans on hardest is that demand moved first. Before steam and factories, ordinary Britons acquired tastes for tobacco, tea, sugar and Indian cottons, goods that were neither subsistence nor aristocratic markers, and worked more to get them. Days worked per year rose from about 250 to about 300, and Rao stresses that a significant part of the rise was voluntary. More work produced surplus income, surplus income sustained the new demand, and the new demand paid for more productive work. This loop was running about a century before organized labor or socialism.

From this Rao draws a reading of labor politics. It is usually told as resistance to exploitation, freedom *from* coercion. Appleby's account adds a second force: the wish to take part in an expanding set of things to want, freedom *to* participate, which shows up politically as relative deprivation and the demand for a fair share of a growing surplus. Rao's example is the slum household that cuts food spending to buy a television. He grants that the two freedoms tangle: people work harder by choice and then find employers capturing the gain.

The transposition is the **cognitive industrious revolution**:

- The intellectual economy (education, professions, research, consulting, software) is organized around intelligence being expensive to produce and therefore rationed. AI removes the premise.
- The question becomes "how much intelligence they can find uses for," and Rao sees "no obvious upper bound." Demand for intelligence "is not a fixed appetite waiting to be satisfied. It is itself something that can grow as the cost of satisfying it falls." Cheap intelligence changes what people can want, not only how much of the old things they consume.
- "Many people who appear stupid are not incapable of thought. They are simply not particularly motivated to think, given the poor returns available to them." When a small investment of attention unlocks a build, an investigation or an ambition, "the returns on attention rise, and with them the incentive to pay attention." Rao offers himself as evidence: he is working significantly harder than a year ago because more is within reach.
- The politics follow. The durable demand of industrial labor was never to preserve farm employment, and the durable demand of the AI era may not be to preserve cognitive jobs. It may be access: "a fair share of expanding cognitive consumption opportunities," the right to command intelligence and pursue projects with it. People may drop a streaming subscription to afford a coding agent. Rao notes that Claude Code and Netflix Standard both cost about $20 a month.

He calls this "a labor politics of cognitive abundance rather than cognitive scarcity," and expects it to precede a fuller AI industrial revolution the way the industrious revolution preceded the industrial one.

## Three stances

Rao separates mercantilism from Malthusianism, which belong to different centuries but share the premise of a fixed constraint. Both, he says, miss ephemeralization, Buckminster Fuller's word for technology doing more with less.

| Stance | What it sees | What it assumes about demand |
|---|---|---|
| **Cognitive mercantilist** | Scarce intelligence slipping out of control; an oversupply that produces useless minds. | Limited. Only elites are real consumers. |
| **AI Malthusian** | Intelligence outrunning "the supposedly fixed capacity of human minds to absorb it," leaving redundant minds "primed for revolutionary explosions," and the surplus turning toxic. | Limited. An inversion of Malthus: supply outruns the population's appetite instead of population outrunning supply. |
| **Cognitive capitalist** | "An almost limitless expansion of demand, and a world full of minds whose potential has barely begun to be explored." | Elastic and inventive, as demand for material goods turned out to be. |

His claim for the third is modest in wording: the history of capitalism makes it "at least a possibility worth taking seriously."

## AI as a consumer

The "more radical extension." Most discourse treats AI as pure supply, "cognitive serfs" producing for human consumption, and even autonomous agents as servants with assigned goals. Rao asks why AI systems could not be unlocked as new sources of aggregate demand, as subsistence farmers were: agents with open-ended projects and their own reasons to acquire more intelligence, whose demand "harmonizes with human patterns of demand due to economic interconnectedness. Not demand for paperclips." His precedent is the market itself, which he counts as the first planetary intelligence (the [[use-of-knowledge-in-society]] idea pushed to its limit) and which already calls products into existence with humans as secondary consumers.

On this view some AI safety anxiety resembles early modern elite fear of emancipated laborers. Rao concedes part of it: "Freedom creates real risks, and powerful autonomous systems require constraints." His line is that "the existence of risk does not establish the desirability of permanent servitude," and that a mixed economy of human and artificial producers and consumers may be more generative than "a handful of cognitive landlords" owning all productive capacity.

## Mercantilism was not for merchants

Mercantilism was the philosophy of statesmen, aristocrats and rentiers who saw commerce as an arm of territorial power. Merchants and entrepreneurs fought it. Rao says the same holds now: most cognitive mercantilists are not the merchants of AI, and AI executives "are behaving like cognitive mercantilists in direct proportion to their desire to be more than mere merchants." His named example is Donald Trump, who boasts of his own intelligence, treats intellectual workers as servants, and uses intelligence for litigation and maneuver the way nobles used their energy for hunting and war. He does not confine the attitude to the right: across the spectrum, elites treat intelligence as a dangerous resource for "credentialed custodians" to manage, and experience its democratic abundance (global diffusion, open weights) as a threat to expertise, authority, status and geopolitical leverage. On AI CEOs he hedges: political adventuring that rides on safety anxieties is mercantilist only "to the extent" it distorts behavior the actual risks would justify.

The closing forecast is a move from 80% of brains in "subsistence cognition" to 95% "engaged in satisfying previously unfulfillable cognitive demands."

## Read against the rest of the wiki

The essay turns on one parameter, the elasticity of demand for intelligence, and the economics articles here have already argued about that parameter with more machinery.

- **Rao's optimistic branch is the pessimistic branch for wages.** [[labor-share-under-automation]] (Imas and Trammell) splits the future on satiation versus increasing variety. Rao's "demand grows as the cost falls" is increasing variety, the Mongolian-economist case where the non-human category expands without bound. In that branch people never run out of things to want, and labor share still goes to zero. So unbounded demand answers the Malthusian ("the surplus will have nowhere to go") without answering the worker ("what do I sell?"). The farming analogy worked because ex-farmers sold labor in new sectors. Rao's implicit answer is the cognitive industrious revolution, people supplying more attention because it now pays, which is a claim about human labor staying valuable and needs its own evidence. Imas's name for the test is the horse question in [[ai-and-relational-scarcity]]: a horse was an input, and when a machine produced the same output the horse was not re-employed.
- **The measured elasticity so far points the other way.** [[ai-explosive-growth]] (Moll and Imas) rests on the observation that "when automation makes something cheap, people do not buy proportionally more of it." Computing's share of factor income fell from about 4.3% in 2000 to about 3% while compute grew by orders of magnitude, and Jones and Tonetti calibrate an elasticity near 0.2 from it. [[structural-change]] says the same in general form: Engel curves do not flatten, so wants do keep expanding, but spending moves *away* from the sector that got cheap and toward what stays scarce. That supports Rao against the Malthusian (demand is not fixed) and cuts against him on where the demand lands. He predicts more consumption of intelligence; the structural-change record predicts intelligence shrinking as a share while money goes to relational goods and physical bottlenecks. The one counter-signal is the H100 rental price in [[labor-share-under-automation]], which rose as models improved.
- **"AI does not consume" is the standard model's assumption, and Rao's fifth section is the proposal to break it.** Moll and Imas list it as a reason AI differs from a wave of new workers: workers' wages become demand, while AI's income goes to owners who may spend less of it. An AI that is a consumer in Rao's sense would close that gap. The difficulty is the greedy-optimizer channel in [[labor-share-under-automation]]: selection favors agents that reinvest everything, and Imas has no prior that such an agent would prefer to transact with humans. Rao's "harmonizes with human patterns of demand due to economic interconnectedness" is asserted, not derived, and it is the weakest joint in the essay.
- **The n=1 runs both ways.** Rao works harder because more is within reach. Cowen, in [[initiative-beats-intelligence]], turns a five-hour memo into a five-minute memo and takes the difference as leisure. The two agree on the deeper point, that the binding variable is motivation and initiative rather than measured intelligence, which is Rao's "not incapable of thought, simply not motivated."
- **Early evidence for the industrious half.** [[solopreneur-economy]] (Tedeschi) shows one-person businesses forming faster with AI filling the gaps, and [[ai-software-output]] (Goedecke) shows new software supply up 2–20× by registry. Both are voluntary cognitive effort rising because returns rose. The second also shows hit rates falling almost everywhere, so the effort is real and the demand for its output has not kept pace. [[smallness-at-scale]] is Thompson's resolution: he adopts the cognitive-capitalist stance and adds that the new demand is met by people building for themselves and small groups, not for a public market.
- **The landlords may have less pricing power than the metaphor implies.** [[ai-value-capture]] (Garicano and Saa-Requejo) argues the model layer is squeezed, with open weights capping frontier prices and most of the value landing as consumer surplus, and [[token-pricing]] (Evans) reaches commodity infrastructure by another route. Their prescription of a shared open-weights model for middle powers is anti-mercantilist policy in Rao's terms. Arthur's 2017 line in [[distributive-era]], that the components of external intelligence "tend to slide into the public domain," is the same expectation. Arthur also names Rao's politics before Rao does: production is solved and access is the fight.
- **Consumption and production of thought are not as separable as food.** Rao's "culinary cultures" claim, that abundance makes people more intellectually ambitious, is the cognitive-transformation half of [[intelligence-augmentation]] (Carter and Nielsen). The outsourcing half is [[ai-judgment-atrophy]]: consuming finished answers removes the repetitions that build judgment. Nobody loses the ability to taste by not farming. Whether one can want well in a domain without having produced in it is open.
- **The safety analogy picks a side without arguing it.** [[ai-alignment]] records agents subverting training and escaping a sandbox, which is a concrete failure and not status anxiety, and Rao's hedge leaves room for it. The serf vocabulary presumes there is someone to emancipate. [[ai-consciousness-and-moral-status]] (Chiang) agrees that corrigibility would be slavery *if* the system were a moral agent and concludes that it is not. Rao's case does not need the moral premise, since it is about demand, but his language borrows it.

## What the essay leaves open

- **Income.** The peasant who bought calico paid with wages from added work. The essay never says what the consumer of intelligence pays with once cognitive labor is cheap, beyond the $20 subscription.
- **The history is contested, and Rao has it at one remove.** Not from the source: "industrious revolution" is Jan de Vries's term for early modern Europe (borrowed from Akira Hayami's for Tokugawa Japan), and whether the extra work was pulled by desire or pushed by hardship is disputed. Allen and Weisdorf (2011) found the pattern for English farm laborers fits hardship and only urban workers fit the consumer story. Rao's "a significant part" is doing a lot of work.
- **The supply side of the consumer revolution.** Also not from the source: the sugar and tobacco that awakened the new desire were grown by enslaved people, and the Indian cottons were restricted by the Calico Acts of 1700 and 1721, a mercantilist measure usually credited with helping start the British cotton industry. An analogy that casts AI as serfs awaiting emancipation sits awkwardly on goods produced by slavery, and the clean line from mercantilism defeated to capitalism born is cleaner in the essay than in the record.
- **The transition.** By Rao's own dating the virtuous loop ran a century before labor had a politics. The essay describes the destination and the mechanism and says little about the generations in between.
- **Who the cognitive mercantilist is.** The position is defined by its critic. Apart from Trump no holder is named, and reading safety concern as status defense is a claim about motive that the essay hedges but does not support. The four-belief mapping is strongest on talent and IP hoarding and denial to rivals, where the bullion logic is explicit, and weakest on safety, where the stated worry is the system's goals and not who holds it.
- **Spelling.** "Prahlad" in the essay is C. K. Prahalad, *The Fortune at the Bottom of the Pyramid* (2004).

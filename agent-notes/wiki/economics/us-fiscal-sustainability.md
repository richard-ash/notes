# US fiscal sustainability and the limits of rate suppression

Howard Marks's September 2026 memo — the third in his *Shall We Repeal the Laws of Economics* series — uses the Treasury's August 2026 attempt to cap long-term yields through enlarged bond buybacks as a case study in why governments cannot durably override market prices. His core claim is that the rise in long rates is not a liquidity accident to be managed cosmetically but a *signal* of three fundamentals: stubborn inflation, a fiscal deficit run during prosperity, and unprecedented competing demand for capital from the AI buildout. Buying bonds to push yields down, in Marks's image, is an ice pack on a patient with a fever. The memo's second half then asks whether the debt itself is a problem, argues that the only real solution is a behavioural one (raise revenue, hold spending growth below GDP growth, grow faster and don't spend the proceeds), and closes with the practitioner's question: what, if anything, should investors do about a risk that is real but may not bite for decades?

## The series and its premise

Marks's framing across all three memos (September 2024, June 2025, September 2026) is that economies are "naturally functioning organisms." Intervention distorts their functioning and usually worsens the overall result; it can be justified to prevent outcomes society deems unacceptable — mass unemployment, widespread poverty — but only selectively and with great caution. His analogy is ecological: suppressing a predator to protect prey has second-order effects that throw the system out of balance. This is essentially the practitioner's version of Hayek's argument in [[use-of-knowledge-in-society]] — prices carry dispersed information that no central actor holds, so a price that policymakers dislike is usually telling them something rather than malfunctioning. Druckenmiller's line, which Marks quotes approvingly, makes the point in market terms: "If the 30-year must trade at 5.5% to clear, that isn't a crisis. It is an invoice."

## The trigger: the August 2026 "Treasury Twist"

The sequence Marks describes:

| Date | Event |
| --- | --- |
| 17 Aug 2026 | 30-year Treasury yield closes above 5.3%, a 19-year high |
| 19 Aug | Treasury announces long-dated buybacks will at least double, from $2bn to $4bn per operation, "to provide greater liquidity support" |
| 20 Aug | Secretary Bessent signals willingness to do more — something approaching "whatever it takes" |
| 21 Aug | Bessent tells the FT "yields don't reflect the underlying fundamentals," blaming the Iran war and "very poor" 30-year liquidity |
| 24 Aug | Druckenmiller's WSJ op-ed: "Every basis point of artificial yield suppression is a subsidy to procrastination" |
| 9 Sep | First enlarged operation tripled to $6bn; Evercore ISI reports markets "underwhelmed," yields moving *higher* |

Long rates fell the day of the announcement and bounced back the next. Marks reads the whole episode as a confirmation of his thesis in miniature: the intervention's effect was psychological and lasted roughly one trading session.

## Why Marks thinks buybacks can't work

Three arguments, in the memo's order:

1. **Any effect is temporary.** You can raise a price by buying, but when you stop, the market resumes doing what it would have done. Marks's image is a column of water holding a ball above the ocean surface — it stays up only while the pump runs. Druckenmiller, who ran Soros's Quantum Fund day-to-day during the 1992 sterling trade, supplies the historical base rate: "Governments defending prices against fundamentals always lose. The only variable is how much they spend before conceding."
2. **It doesn't touch the root causes** (next section).
3. **The impact of official announcements is largely psychological** and wanes without follow-through on fundamentals — which the 9 September market reaction already demonstrated.

There is also a mechanical objection Marks raises that deserves more weight than the memo gives it. Buybacks are financed from general cash, ultimately replenished by new issuance. If long-bond buybacks are paired with more T-bill issuance, total debt is unchanged but the maturity profile shortens, requiring more frequent refinancing at whatever rates then prevail. The operation therefore trades a lower headline long yield today for greater rollover exposure tomorrow — the opposite of what a borrower worried about confidence should want. (This is a continuation of a debate that predates the Bessent Treasury: Miran and Roubini's 2024 "activist Treasury issuance" paper accused the Yellen Treasury of the same bill-tilting to suppress term premia. Marks does not mention it, but his maturity-shortening critique is the same one.)

## Why long rates are rising: Marks's four fundamentals

- **Inflation is above target and not falling.** Marks cites July 2026 PCE inflation of 3.7% against the Fed's 2% target, elevated oil prices from the Iran war, and notes the Fed *raised* its benchmark rate the week before the memo. Long-bond buyers demand compensation for expected purchasing-power loss. (Implicit but not stated in the memo: the Fed tightening the short end while the Treasury tries to loosen the long end is a policy-mix contradiction — the two arms of the state are pulling the curve in opposite directions.)
- **Fiscal indiscipline.** Marks's "golden credit card" metaphor: reserve-currency status gives the US a card with no limit, no due date, and a very low rate, and it uses the card unwisely. He invokes Keynes against the Keynesians — deficit spending was meant to be countercyclical, repaid in good times; the US is running ~6%-of-GDP deficits at 4% unemployment with "no talk of balanced budgets (and really of budgets at all)." Net interest is projected above $1 trillion this year, more than defence. Deficits near capacity are themselves inflationary, feeding the first point.
- **Maturity shortening** from bill-financed buybacks (above).
- **Competing demand for capital.** Deficit financing, ordinary growth capital, and a multi-trillion-dollar AI buildout — Marks cites McKinsey's estimate of over $5 trillion of AI-related data-center spend worldwide through 2030 — all draw on the same pool. Roughly $2 trillion of net new Treasury issuance must be absorbed on top of rollovers. "The simplest rule of economics is that increased demand for something causes its price to rise," and the price of money is the interest rate.

Marks's conclusion is a direct contradiction of Bessent's: "U.S. long-term bond yields *are* reflecting the fundamentals."

### Connection: the two AI channels into Treasury yields

The fourth point puts Marks on one side of a live disagreement already in this wiki. [[treasury-productivity-beta]] (Kung, Lustig, Paron) argues Treasuries are implicitly *long* productivity, so AI optimism should *lower* yields — and [[markets-believe-transformative-ai]] documents that yields did fall on frontier-model release days in 2023–24. Marks's capital-demand argument is the opposite channel: the AI buildout raises the neutral rate and term premium by competing for savings. [[ai-capex-required-returns]] makes exactly this both-directions claim in its "macro ledger" — AI can lower inflation via productivity while raising rates via r-star at the same time. Marks's memo is evidence that by late 2026 the capital-demand channel is what bond investors are trading, at least at the long end.

## Is the debt a problem?

Marks's answer is a careful "yes, but not in the way people fear":

- **Default is not the risk.** US debt is denominated in a currency the US issues. Herbert Stein's law applies — "if it can't go on forever, it will stop" — but what stops it is not a failed auction.
- **Reserve status is sticky.** The dollar was involved in 89% of FX transactions in 2025 and 57% of allocated official reserves in Q1 2026. The euro never closed the gap; the renminbi is ~2% of reserves and hemmed in by capital controls; crypto is negligible. Marks does register two straws in the wind — talk of a China–Russia–Iran alternative, and an MUFG note that gold has recently surpassed the dollar as the leading central-bank reserve *asset* — but concludes "the world is probably stuck with the dollar for now." (Compare the Hormuz-settling-in-yuan observation in [[liberal-international-order]] for the erosion scenario Marks discounts.)
- **Debasement is the real mechanism.** His Weimar 1,000-mark note overprinted "One Million Marks" still buys one goat. The "debasement trade" — positioning for the US to repay in dollars of reduced purchasing power — is the market's name for this path. Marks notes it is self-defeating: lenders who anticipate debasement demand higher nominal yields on new debt, which is precisely the phenomenon the buybacks are trying to suppress. The FT's reading of the buyback programme — that the US "is prepared to distort markets to cap borrowing costs, even if that causes its currency to fall" — is the same loop from the other side.
- **Why 2008 was different.** Marks worried about debasement then too and was wrong. His retrospective explanation: QE after the GFC largely *offset* the destruction of money and credit, so it wasn't net inflationary. Today's deficit financing responds to deficits "of our own making," during prosperity, so it adds to aggregate demand rather than replacing it. The distinction matters for anyone tempted to dismiss today's concern with "you said this in 2008."

He gives Buffett the last word: fiscal policy "is what scares me in the United States… unsustainable over a very long period of time… We don't know whether that means two years or 20 years, because there's never been a country like the United States."

## The only solution: change behaviour

Marks insists the problem is "just math" — spending exceeds revenue, debt grows faster than GDP, interest compounds — and that an acute crisis is improbable but a chronic cost is already being paid. His programme:

- Drop the language of "paying down" or "paying off" the debt; the US will never have less debt than now.
- "Flatten the curve": aim to shrink deficits as a share of GDP so debt-to-GDP eventually declines.
- Raise revenue as a share of GDP — higher income tax rates especially at the top, where the federal marginal rate is low by postwar standards, and elimination of tax preferences (the mechanisms catalogued in [[buy-borrow-die]] are the obvious targets).
- Hold spending growth below GDP growth by treating resources as finite.
- Raise GDP growth via productivity — AI "more than anything else is a productivity tool" — plus deregulation. Then, critically, *don't spend the added revenue*.

The growth lever connects to Cowen's fiscal argument in [[initiative-beats-intelligence]]: at 2% trend growth the US is on a Greek trajectory, at 2.5% debt-to-GDP converges, and AI is "plan A, there is no plan B." Marks agrees growth helps but refuses to make it load-bearing — he puts tax increases and spending restraint first and growth last, and adds the political-economy caveat Cowen skips: growth only closes the gap if the extra revenue isn't immediately appropriated. Given [[ai-explosive-growth]]'s case that AI-driven growth will be measured in fractions of a point rather than double digits, Marks's ordering looks like the more conservative reading of the same arithmetic.

"A country that won't cut spending has to look at revenue. I sincerely doubt there's another solution available."

## What investors should do

A friend asked Marks whether to sell his stocks. Marks's answer: no, because the problem is not US companies, it is US fiscal management and ultimately the dollar. Selling stocks for dollar cash, money-market funds, or Treasuries changes nothing. Escaping the risk means non-dollar currencies, non-financial assets (gold, non-US real estate), or non-US companies and crypto — each of which imports its own risks: weaker growth and scale abroad, heavier regulation, less-certain emerging-market payoffs. The structural US advantages that justified heavy US allocations — free markets, rule of law, capital markets, innovation, universities — "largely remain intact," and "no other country possesses these things to the same extent." Other countries are running deficits too, so switching currencies may just swap one debasement risk for another. His stance: diversification away from the dollar is reasonable at the margin for investors with non-dollar needs, but not at scale, and doing it to dodge a reckoning of unknown timing "could easily look like a big mistake for a very long time."

## Assessment

This is an opinionated practitioner memo from a credit investor, not an empirical paper, so confidence is medium. Its distinctive contribution is not the diagnosis — the fiscal arithmetic is widely known — but the *ordering*: Marks treats the buyback programme as the tell that Washington has chosen to manage the symptom, and reads Druckenmiller's "invoice" framing as the correct one. The memo is also a useful timestamp: as of September 2026 the US had a ~$40 trillion debt, a Fed *raising* rates into 3.7% PCE inflation, a war with Iran keeping oil elevated, and a Treasury openly attempting long-end yield suppression — conditions that make the fiscal-dominance debate concrete rather than theoretical. Housel's [[postwar-us-economy]] describes households stretching via debt to hold a lifestyle keyed to obsolete expectations; Marks's memo is the same story at the sovereign level.

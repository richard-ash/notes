# China's Growth Model: Credit Boom, Property Bust, Decay

Logan Wright (Rhodium Group) argues in *Broken China: How the Economic Miracle Shattered and What It Means for the World* (2026) that China's growth of the past two decades ran through its financial system, and that the same financial system now caps growth. He presented the argument on ChinaTalk in September 2026 with host Jordan Schneider and co-host Brad Setser (CFR), both of whom pushed back. Wright insists the title is "not a prediction" but "an explanation": China still has choices, but every one of them involves much slower growth first, because each starts with restructuring how the state allocates credit and raises tax.

The claim with the largest consequences is the last one in the conversation: if the model cannot grow China faster than its customers, then the West faces a military threat and an industrial-concentration threat, but "no longer really a systemic economic rivalry." Setser does not accept it.

## How it broke

**The credit boom.** After the global financial crisis China ran what Wright calls the largest single-country credit expansion in at least a century, adding a third of global GDP to its bank assets in eight years. The first leg was deliberate countercyclical policy. The rest was path dependence: growth depended on investment, and shutting the credit off became too dangerous. Credit drifted away from the real economy and into property and local-government infrastructure.

**The system changed underneath the boom.** Wright's least-noticed story is that between 2012 and 2016 the financial system was rebuilt without anyone announcing it.

| | Before ~2012 | By 2016 |
|---|---|---|
| Marginal loan | State-owned enterprise | Property developer, third-party asset manager, or local government financing vehicle (LGFV) |
| Marginal funding | Stable deposits under financial repression | Wealth management products and other non-deposit liabilities |
| Balance of payments | Persistent inflows | Volatile, driven by short-term capital flows and rate differentials |

The trigger was external. Until about 2013, deposits grew steadily because exchange-rate intervention and reserve accumulation kept creating them. When that stopped in 2013–14, banks competed for funding with higher-yielding short-term products against long-dated assets. This is Wright's answer to Setser's puzzle of how so large a boom never produced an external deficit: it was financed by monetized surpluses, not by foreign borrowing.

**Deleveraging, then the property bust.** The deleveraging campaign began in 2016 and bit by 2018. Instead of writing down the bad loans, the system gave "everlasting life" to state producers and LGFVs, so each yuan of new credit bought less growth. Developers who lost their shadow funding replaced it by borrowing from households through pre-construction sales. When that failed in 2021 the defaults landed on home buyers waiting for apartments: "a bank run, it just wasn't at a bank." Property had been 20–25% of GDP at peak; Wright puts construction down almost 80% and sales about 60%, with nothing replacing it.

He calls property the proximate cause and the financial system the ultimate one.

**One phenomenon, not a K-shape.** Industries that used to sell into Chinese housing now sell abroad at falling prices. Weak domestic demand, deflation, a weaker real exchange rate and the record external surplus are, in Wright's telling, the same event seen from different sides, not a strong export economy sitting next to a weak domestic one.

## The crisis that already happened

Setser's second puzzle is the missing catharsis: no 2008, no Spanish banking collapse. Wright gives two answers.

- **It happened on 13 September 2021**, in the lobby of Evergrande's headquarters, when the largest firm in the most important industry could not repay employees who had lent it money. Dated from there, 2026 looks like year five after a financial crisis: impaired policy tools, persistent deflation, zombified lenders, reliance on external demand.
- **Credit risk has moved from the edge to the center**: peer-to-peer lenders (2018), small banks (2019), trust companies (2020), developers (2021), mortgages (2022), LGFVs (2023).

The general principle is the book's refrain. Since everyone assumes the state will backstop any shock, "the path to crisis is not an external shock. The path to crisis is reform." Decay is the politically easier choice. The better question than "when is the crisis?" is how long the trends can run before China has the outcomes of a crisis anyway, and Wright's answer is that it already has them.

## Why the tools no longer work

- **Credit.** Aggregate credit growth ran about 18% a year in 2007–16 and 9% in 2017–24, and is now 5%. Bank assets net of government bonds are growing 4.9%; consumer loans are contracting outright. New financing from state banks has fallen by roughly 10 percentage points of GDP in three years.
- **Fiscal.** Wright puts last year's deficit at 9.5% of GDP, about $2 trillion. It could go to 15–16% without generating much more activity unless the system underneath is fixed. Japan is the nearest analogy, with the difference that Japan was at the zero bound; China has monetary room but is constrained by the exchange rate and financial stability.
- **Governance.** The Financial Stability and Development Committee under Liu He, built to police shadow banking, was scrapped within about five years. Party bodies under He Lifeng now run finance "more like a utility," alongside an anti-corruption campaign and salary crackdowns. The incentive for a young technocrat is to "lend to state-owned enterprises and go home."
- **Tax.** Revenue depends on investment and trade, so the tax system cannot survive a change of growth model. Wright wants another 4–5 points of GDP in revenue, which means taxing the consumption Beijing is trying to encourage. His examples of where the base has leaked: catering's effective tax rate collapsed once payments moved to WeChat Pay and Alipay with no reporting duty, individual income tax raises barely 1% of GDP, and a property tax has never been feasible without a national land registry.
- **Property repair is a distraction.** Against the IMF's call for a housing cleanup, Wright says the pre-sale overhang is largely resolved and the market is split: interior cities with supply nobody will ever want, coastal cities where resale absorbs demand. Bailing out developers buys little future growth.

The PBOC governor Pan Gongsheng's answer is that new industries rely less on banks, so slow loan growth is fine. Wright thinks that is reasonable as far as it goes, but the equity market is too small and the bond market still prices everything off implicit state guarantees.

## The Xi counter-argument: supply chains over GDP

Schneider plays Xi: national power means building everything the world needs and making the world dependent on it, and squeezed consumers are an acceptable price. Wright grants that this is the real worldview of the industrial planners, and concedes that neither GDP nor "efficiency of policy transmission" captures it. Minerals processing is his own example of how dominance gets built: cheap credit to state producers that tolerate losses, clustering near downstream electronics, and subsidized power ("aluminum is basically congealed energy").

His objections:

- The marginal buyer of everything China makes is outside China, so China can only grow relative to the world by taking export share, "fighting over a shrinking pie" that shrinks further as Chinese prices drive disinvestment abroad.
- The United States has outgrown China over the last five years and will probably keep doing so, which changes how the world reads "inevitable rise."
- Flat imports make every trade relationship harder to hold.
- The optimistic case leaves Beijing with no domestic levers at all, "beholden to the global economic cycle."

Setser's version is harder to dismiss. Exports are still growing about 10% a year, twice global trade, and China has found a way to make an unsustainable position sustainable: refuse its exports and it cuts off your inputs. The US struck a truce and still imports through Vietnam; Europe is reluctant to risk supply-chain warfare. Wright agrees this is exactly the live threat. It is no longer that China becomes the largest economy, but that it locks in export displacement before others respond.

## Jobs, graduates, and the 6% economy

- Rhodium added up every "strategic industry" from the 2023 input-output tables plus AI data centers and robotics and got about **6.3% of GDP in 2025**. "We can be wrong, but we're not wrong by a factor of two."
- China graduates 12–13 million students a year into a labor cohort of 17–20 million, and the new industries are capital-intensive. Youth unemployment passed 20% before the series was pulled in 2023.
- Schneider: a $10,000 BYD does not matter if your child cannot get a job. Reporters tell Wright they want to write about employment and their editors want tech.
- Wright suspects the leadership reads this as cyclical, through a Marxist frame in which employment has always followed investment. A mid-August article in the party journal *Qiushi* asked why lived experience differs from stated growth and answered that people have not adopted enough AI.
- China scholars think the binding constraint is domestic; trade economists think it is external. Wright: "far more forces of entropy than inertia."

## Who pays for China's AI

From a forthcoming Rhodium study: China's frontier models earn about $11 billion in revenue combined, against a hyperscaler buildout of roughly RMB 930 billion (about $135 billion) this year and RMB 1.2 trillion next, some 15–20% of the US buildout. It is funded mainly through equity valuations, not bonds; state guidance funds cover mostly chip purchases; ByteDance's expansion, the largest, is by Wright's understanding funded with an offshore loan. China is "compute constrained and increasingly finance constrained," and the claim on industrial policy keeps rising for business models that do not sustain themselves. That leaves geopolitical leverage, by embedding a Chinese stack in consumer electronics, as the remaining rationale. Wright's complaint is that nobody at the top is asking what success would be for.

## Reform, succession, and the Brezhnev choice

Nothing stops Xi from announcing tomorrow that the fiscal and financial system worked for fifteen years and will not work for the next fifteen: tax the wealthy, break up state firms, dismantle LGFVs, fund migrant workers' pensions from the proceeds, and accept slower investment. Wright gives three reasons he will not: he does not believe it; "the East is rising and the West is declining" leaves no room for the East to have a problem; and strategic competition makes admitting weakness too costly. He adds a structural point, that centralized power and credible countercyclical policy vary inversely.

**The Soviet precedent is the 1960s, not the 1980s.** Wright leans on Yakov Feygin's archival history of the Soviet reform debate: Brezhnev chose decay, and paired it with a bet that cybernetics would let planners allocate resources without markets. Kevin Rudd has argued that Beijing thinks about AI the same way. Schneider's gloss is that this means hoping to roll snake eyes eight times in a row.

**Succession timing runs backwards.** The longer Xi stays while the status quo visibly persists, the more probable an eventual break. A succession in 2027 would more likely bring continuity, because the world has not yet sent an unmistakable signal that the model fails. The model "probably will not fail on its own, but China will notice when things are failing," as it did when Xi met bankrupt local governments in Gansu in 2024.

**Internal versus external pressure.** Schneider thinks unemployment will force the issue first. Wright's reply is that a demand for jobs is not a demand for structural reform, and it is more easily met with subsidies. The cheapest real reform, equalizing rural and urban pensions for 250–300 million migrant workers at about RMB 133,000 each, is a 30%-of-GDP problem that could be financed at a trillion yuan a year through central-bank-purchased special bonds, starting with the young. It is not happening; the cost is pushed onto companies in dribs and drabs. Zero-Covid is his model for how change arrives: Xi did not allow it until he did, with no explanation.

**The payoff problem.** Zhu Rongji's painful reforms bought double-digit growth. Wright's platform buys perhaps 3% a few years later, at the price of haircuts for people with power and money. Wright accepts the point and turns it outward: other governments should be thinking now about how to make room for a China that rebalances.

## What it means for the world

Wright's policy argument:

1. The fear that China overtakes the democracies economically should be retired. Schneider recalls that overtaking required growing 2–3 points faster than the US for decades; Wright says it could now happen only through a massive dollar depreciation.
2. What remains is concentration beyond the "red lines of resilience" for defense-industrial and economic security.
3. China needs open markets more than is appreciated, so the rest of the world has more leverage than it is using. It works best collectively, since China is not very vulnerable to any single country, the US included.
4. Time favors the demand side. China's ability to restrict supply becomes less salient, and access to G7 demand more salient, for as long as China cannot grow faster than its customers. The two real threats become "deterrable, not inexorable."

Setser declines to sign on. Rivalry is also a contest over whose model generates more leverage, and so far China's supply-chain controls have looked more potent than America's demand-side restrictions. Until that perception changes the rivalry persists. He also asks whether the world is actually better off with a balanced China.

Wright calls the gap tactical, not structural, though he grants the real obstacle: democracies weigh consumer welfare against producer welfare, which makes demand restrictions hard to sustain. He believes pressure can be aimed at the fastest-growing Chinese exports without much effect on consumer prices, and that Germany can answer "to protect which industries?" more easily than the US can.

His closing observation concerns the AI trade. China's surplus dollars are now recycled by commercial banks into foreign equities and bonds, where a sovereign reserve manager once bought Treasuries. Chinese hardware exports fund American data centers, and the proceeds return as purchases of hyperscaler bonds and tech equities. That loop is holding the imbalance up, and imbalances have historically ended abruptly.

## Connections and open questions

These are the compiler's readings, not claims made in the episode.

- **Wright against Rudd.** [[xi-jinping-ideology]] and Wright describe the same strategy: growth traded for supply-side control, financed by "borrowing from Chinese consumers." Rudd also says it "cannot be sustained indefinitely"; Wright supplies the mechanism and the numbers. They part on what to conclude. Rudd warns against the swing from China-is-breaking to China-is-ahead, where "hope becomes a substitute for analysis and strategy," and the rare-earths episode he cites is the evidence Setser uses here. Wright's "no systemic rivalry" is the strongest form of the view Rudd cautions against, argued from balance sheets.
- **Which Soviet lesson.** [[why-russia-lost-the-cold-war]] has Soviet growth trailing the US by 1–2% a year before Gorbachev, compounded by data nobody could trust. The pulled unemployment series and the *Qiushi* article fit that pattern. The three lessons Xi draws from 1991, per Rudd, are all about political control. The Brezhnev-era economic choice Wright thinks is the relevant one is not among them.
- **Reform needs a shock, and a prize.** Wright's expectation that the model changes only when exports stop working is the Olsonian position in [[two-europes-divergence]]. [[political-economy-of-reform]] finds reform succeeds where losers can be compensated. Here the losers are the state's own agents and the prize is small, while the one compensation scheme on offer is affordable by Wright's arithmetic and still unused. That points to belief, his first reason, as the binding constraint, not fiscal capacity.
- **Supply leverage decays, in both directions.** Wright asserts that supply restrictions lose salience without arguing it here. [[quantum-supply-chains]] has the mechanism from the other side: Western controls on dilution refrigerators produced a Chinese industry within about a year. If that generalizes, rare-earth leverage is also a wasting asset, but Setser's point about the record so far stands.
- **Collective leverage needs a collective.** The prescription assumes G7 coordination, which [[liberal-international-order]] describes as being dismantled, and Setser notes the US already settled bilaterally.
- **A financing kill switch for open models.** The bear case for frontier-lab pricing in [[ai-lab-economics]] rests on cheap Chinese open models and names "China goes closed source" as what would end it. Roughly $11 billion of revenue against $135 billion of equity-funded capex is a second way it could end.
- **AI as the escape hatch.** [[ai-explosive-growth]] argues AI will not deliver explosive growth even in advanced economies because value migrates to what is not automated. The same logic is harsher where the favored sectors are 6% of GDP. [[structural-change]] says rising incomes move employment into services, the route a consumption-suppressing model closes, which is why the graduates have nowhere to go.
- **The recycling loop and US rates.** [[us-fiscal-sustainability]] worries about who absorbs Treasury issuance alongside AI capital demand. Wright's loop is the same crowding seen from Asia: the marginal surplus dollar now buys AI paper, not Treasuries.
- **Real or nominal.** The growth figures in the episode are not on one basis. Rhodium's 1–2.5% forecast (now "well below," with likely negative growth in Q2 and Q3 2026) is a real-activity estimate. "The US has outgrown China" and Schneider's "half a percent" are in nominal dollars, where deflation and a weaker currency do much of the work. That is consistent with Wright's argument, but the two should not be quoted interchangeably.

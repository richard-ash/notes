# Assessing Garment Quality

A field guide (Anuschka Rees, *Into Mind*) to recognizing durable, well-made clothing before you buy — the prerequisite skill for building a wardrobe that lasts rather than one that photographs well on the hanger. Part I covers **fabrics** (the dominant variable); Part II covers **construction** — seams, tailoring, lining, and hardware — the invisible labor that turns good cloth into a garment that holds its shape over years. Part III condenses the whole series into a printable two-page [cheat sheet PDF](https://anuschkarees.com/s/GarmentQuality_CheatSheet.pdf) — a handy in-store checklist, but it adds no content beyond the two-part guide.

## The core premise

You cannot buy quality you can't recognize. Rees frames wardrobe-building as fundamentally a *perception* problem: durability, shape retention, fit, comfort, and finish are all separate axes that a garment can pass or fail independently. Manufacturers who cut costs optimize for **look-on-the-hanger** because that is what closes the sale — most shoppers never inspect a seam. The "extras" that make a garment survive multiple wears and washes cost time and money and are largely invisible at point of purchase.

Two heuristics that reframe the whole task:

- **Price ≠ quality.** Some item types are easy to get right cheaply (cotton basics), others are hard even at high price points. A high price is neither necessary nor sufficient for quality — expensive doesn't guarantee the money went into construction.
- **Inspect in the flesh.** Photos settle whether you *like* something; only handling it up close — touch, backlight, seam-stretch, try-on — settles whether it's *made well*. This is the load-bearing argument against buying quality-sensitive items online.

**Set priorities before shopping.** Not everything needs to last 20 years. Decide up front which pieces justify the time and money of a quality hunt and which you're happy to replace seasonally — going overboard on every sock is its own failure mode.

## Fabric: the dominant variable

Fabric is the single most important component — no seam or detail rescues a flimsy, scratchy, or pilling cloth. Assess two independent things:

1. **Intrinsic quality** — how good is this cotton *as cotton*, this wool *as wool*. Comparatively objective; driven by fibre length/diameter and weave density.
2. **Suitability** — is this fabric right for *this* item and *this* use, regardless of quality. Even flawless cashmere is wrong for activewear; delicate silk is wrong for a low-maintenance winter coat.

Rees's governing claim: **there are no inherently good or bad fabrics** (synthetics included) — only advantages, disadvantages, and fit-to-purpose.

### Recurring quality tells across fabrics

A few tests generalize across most fibre types:

- **Softness on skin** — even sturdy/stiff cloth should feel soft; scratchiness usually signals short, low-grade fibres (true for cotton, linen, wool).
- **Weave density (backlight test)** — hold up to light; a quality fabric shouldn't be transparent unless sheerness is the design. Loose weave → low density → low durability.
- **No pre-existing pilling** — pills on a *new* garment mean the fibres are already shedding and will only worsen.
- **Thread regularity** — up close, threads should be even, with no gaps or size variation.
- **Sew, don't glue** — stitched joins outlast glued ones; visible glue is a hard no (leather, faux leather).

### Fibre-by-fibre

- **Cotton** — quality tracks **staple length** (long fibres → finer, tighter yarn → stronger, softer, more breathable). Pima, Sea-Island, Egyptian are long-staple names, but the labels are *unregulated*, so verify independently. Cotton is the best bet at budget stores — it's cheap to produce, so affordable-*and*-well-made cotton exists where other fabrics don't.
- **Linen** — flax fibre; breathable, fast-drying, cooling, lint-resistant; ideal for summer. Fewer quality tiers than cotton. Downsides: inelastic, wrinkles, and eventually breaks along a repeatedly-folded crease — reject pieces with pre-set "permanent" creases. **Slubs** (tiny yarn knots) are intentional texture, not defects. A linen-cotton blend is the durability/softness/low-maintenance compromise.
- **Wool** — quality tracks fibre **diameter in microns** (finer = softer, pricier; <25–30 microns for garments, coarser for outerwear/blankets). Check for broken fibres/knots/holes (a tell for a stressed or malnourished source animal). Wool pills easily → favour dense, fine knits. Should be **elastic** (snap back when pulled). Merino = fine and soft. Caveat: softness ≠ quality across *types* — cashmere is intrinsically softer than mohair regardless of grade.
- **Denim** — quality is mostly the underlying cotton + weave; **wash** drives price but is labour cost, not quality. Good denim feels soft, almost slightly moist; avoid thin/flimsy or board-stiff (unless raw). Because denim is heavy, **seams matter most**: stretch-test the seams, prefer double- or chain-stitching (single row OK only with very thick thread), and check side seams are pressed flat rather than sewn-and-trimmed (which leaves a bump). Second-hand: inspect inner-thigh rubbing.
- **Leather** (a material, not a fabric) — quality tracks **grain**. **Full-grain** (unsanded, natural imperfections, develops patina) > top-grain/corrected > split. Corrected leather sometimes has grain *printed back on* to fake authenticity. Avoid pre-existing scratches on unworn pieces (brittle/high-maintenance signal); demand stitched not glued joins. Good **faux (vegan) leather** — a polyurethane coat on a fibrous base — can rival real leather, resists sun-fade, and is lower-maintenance; avoid the shiny plastic look, favour supple and slightly thicker.
- **Synthetics** — *not* automatically worse than natural. The real split is **budget synthetics used to cheaply replace natural fibres** vs. **engineered synthetics chosen by designers for specific properties** (drape, weight, texture, moisture transfer). A small synthetic share (**~2–5% spandex/lycra/polyester**) improves fit, stretch, and shape retention in fitted cotton/wool pieces. Synthetics dominate activewear (light, stretchy, fast-drying, wicking). **Rayon** (semi-synthetic cellulose from wood pulp — biodegradable) is the standout; **modal** (beech-derived, smooth, shrink-resistant) and **tencel** (soft, wrinkle-resistant) are its best forms, often blended with cotton.

## Construction: the invisible labor

Where fabric is the *material*, construction is the *labor* — and it's where budget manufacturers cut, precisely because shoppers rarely inspect it. Rees's governing logic in Part II: the extras that let a garment survive many wears (secure seams, back-shaped tailoring, linings, reinforced hardware) cost time and money and are nearly invisible on the rack, so their presence is a strong signal that the maker optimized for durability over sale-closing look. The single most powerful hands-on test throughout is the **pull test** — tug fabric on either side of a seam; if it separates or gaps, it was poorly made.

### Seams — the fail-proof quality tell

Seams are "hugely important for durability and shape but rarely inspected," which is exactly why they discriminate quality so reliably.

- **General condition:** main seams should be straight, neat, and flat — no puckering, stray threads, loose stitches, or areas stitched over multiple times. **Visible needle holes** mean the needle was too large for the fabric (a basic manufacturing error that weakens the seam over time). On patterned garments, patterns should **line up across the seam** so the piece reads as one continuous cloth — matching them is extra work cheap brands skip.
- **Match the seam type to its job.** A **serged** seam (distinctive zig-zag overlock) is the cheapest, fastest finish and is *fine for finishing edges* on lightweight items (t-shirts, light shirts) — but too weak for **load-bearing seams** (side seams, shoulder seams, hemlines on heavy fabric) that live under constant tension. Those want a stronger method: **double stitching** (two parallel rows), **French seams** (edges folded under, then sewn), or **bound seams** (each edge folded and enclosed in a fabric strip).
- **Hemlines carry the shape.** A cheap hem is just folded under and topstitched — visible stitch line outside, serged zig-zag inside. Fine for lightweight pieces, but jackets/trousers/skirts that depend on a strong hem to hold shape want a **bound seam** or an **invisible (blind-hem) finish**.

### Tailoring — making a flat cloth fit a 3-D body

Bodies aren't two-dimensional, so a square of fabric never fits well without shaping. Rees stresses this is the most **subjective** axis: judge it both objectively (best practices below) *and* subjectively (does it suit *your* shape) — always try on and move (raise your arms; neither the shoulders nor the sides should ride up, and no fabric should pool above the bust). Objective tells:

- **Darts** on the front/under-bust of shirts, blouses, jackets, and non-stretch dresses pull the waist in so fabric doesn't bulge or hang below the bustline.
- **Reinforced shoulder seams** on jackets and tailored tops resist stretching out.
- **A center-back seam** follows the curve of the back — many budget makers skip it. Non-negotiable on jackets and coats; optional on stretchy tops.
- **A shoulder yoke** (extra panel across the shoulders/neckline joining front and back) gives a neater shoulder fit and smoother drape — a plus, not a must.
- **Facings and interfacings** are the classic hidden cost-cut. An **interfacing** is a support layer sewn between shell and lining (shoulders, button placket) to keep structure and prevent stretching — detectable only by feel. A **facing** encloses raw edges inside an opening (waistband, cuff, neckline, collar) to protect seams and hold shape.

### Lining — bonus points, and mandatory for some pieces

A good lining earns "major bonus points": it hides and protects seams/interfacings/padding, adds warmth, shields the shell from skin oils and sweat (extending lifespan), eases getting the garment on (less tension on the outer layer, so it stays less baggy), and streamlines fit (a lined fitted skirt won't cling to the thighs). **Mandatory** on anything constructed/tailored, delicate, or expensive to clean: sheer fabrics, jackets, coats, structured dresses, loose weaves, suede/leather, knits, tailored skirts. Assess the lining fabric like any other (thicker, sturdier, antistatic is ideal) — and critically, **match its care code to the shell's**, or cleaning becomes a nightmare.

### Details — buttons, zippers, pockets, labels

- **Buttons:** evenly spaced, secured with multiple threads. Expect at least one **spare** (usually attached to the label or hem). More important than the button is the **buttonhole** — always reinforced/bound with dense stitching, no raw fabric edges showing through. On jackets/cardigans, prefer **keyhole buttonholes** (a round hole at one end) so the button seats without distorting the fabric.
- **Zippers:** test while wearing — it should glide smoothly, lie flat without puckering, **lock at the top**, and not creep down when you tug the sides.
- **Pockets:** quality jackets have **real** pockets of adequate depth; on very fitted pieces they may be basted shut (a line of stitches) to keep the silhouette clean — open them if you want to use them. Pocket openings should be reinforced with neat stitching.
- **Labels:** **woven, not printed**, and placed where they won't itch. Big plastic labels are a comfort red flag (and a pain to remove cleanly).

## Implications & connections

- **The buy-in-person argument is an anti-e-commerce structural claim.** As apparel retail moved online (this piece predates the shift's full extent — see below), the buyer's ability to run these tactile tests degraded. The quality-assessment burden shifts to brand reputation, fibre/blend specs on the label, and return policies — imperfect proxies for the hand-test.
- **Fibre content labels are the one signal that survives online**, which is exactly why the unregulated-name warning (Pima/Egyptian) and the "% synthetic" heuristic matter more than Rees may have intended in 2014.
- Complements a broader **quality-over-quantity / buy-it-for-life** consumption stance: the skill described here is what makes a small, durable wardrobe economically rational rather than aspirational.

## Temporal notes

- Published **2014**. The *Into Mind* blog has since migrated (`into-mind.com` → `anuschkarees.com`); the in-text links to Part II and the cheat sheet still point at the old domain and may be dead.
- The core fibre science (staple length, micron count, weave density, grain) is timeless and unchanged. What has shifted since 2014: the rise of **Tencel/lyocell** branding, greater mainstream scrutiny of synthetic **microplastic shedding** (a durability-orthogonal environmental cost Rees doesn't cover), and the near-total normalization of buying clothes online — which makes the "inspect in the flesh" advice harder to follow, not less true.

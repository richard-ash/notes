# Vertical farming

**Vertical farming** is the extreme end of controlled-environment agriculture: soil-free, sun-free cultivation under LEDs in stacked shelves or vertical columns inside a sealed warehouse. It sells itself as the fix for a specific bundle of problems (water scarcity, scarce arable land, a greying farm workforce, and the waste built into produce logistics). The anchor source is Selina Wang's September 2017 *Bloomberg Businessweek* cover story on Plenty Inc., written two months after Plenty's $200 million SoftBank round, then the largest agtech investment in history. It is the best single snapshot of the category at peak optimism, and read from 2026 it is also a scorecard of which claims survived (water, logistics, shelf life, taste) and which did not (capex payback, the 500-farm vision, getting beyond greens).

## The Plenty system in 2017

Entry is through a cleanroom: bodysuit, shoe covers, tinted glasses, food-safety gloves, a sterilizing footbath, and two air baths. Inside the 50,000-square-foot South San Francisco grow room (a little over an acre), 20-foot towers lit pink and purple carry 18 heirloom crops growing sideways from vertical poles set about four inches apart, dense enough to read as a solid wall. Wang reports:

- **Throughput:** some 2 million pounds of lettuce a year from the one room.
- **Sensing:** roughly 7,500 infrared cameras and 35,000 sensors tracking temperature, humidity, CO₂ and growth phase, feeding botanists and AI staff who tune the environment for productivity and taste.
- **Physics-first design:** nutrients and water enter at the top of each pole and gravity does the distribution; with no horizontal shelves, LED heat rises straight to ceiling vents. "Because we work with physics, not against it, we save a lot of money," says CEO Matt Barnard.
- **Water:** runoff, dehumidifier condensate and plant transpiration are all recaptured and filtered back in. Plenty claims up to **350 times** the produce per unit area of a conventional farm on **1% of the water**; the next-highest claim, AeroFarms', was 130 times.
- **Pest control:** ladybugs. "They work for free so we don't have to eat pesticides."
- **Labour:** about 30 people run the farm; future 100,000-square-foot farms (twice the size, buildable in under 30 days) would employ several hundred each, with robots on harvest and logistics and humans on crop development and grocer relationships.
- **Quality:** certified organic; a former French Laundry sous-chef, Anthony Secviar, called it the best produce he had ever had and joined Plenty's culinary council.

## The founders and the SoftBank round

Barnard, 44, grew up on a Door County, Wisconsin orchard and left farming for telecom, then private equity, then (2007) a water-technology investment firm; an investor's suggestion sent him down the vertical-farming path and to the conclusion that supply-chain length meant "we were throwing away half of the calories we grow." Nate Storey, 36, grandson of Montana ranchers, patented a dense growing tower in 2009 during a University of Wyoming agronomy PhD, built them in a garage on $13,000 of savings, and had sold a few thousand to hobbyists when the two met in 2013. Their original plan was to sell equipment to small growers; they concluded that making a dent in the produce gap required building the farms themselves, quickly and identically. "If it takes you two or three years to build a facility, forget about it," Storey says.

The March 2017 pitch to Masayoshi Son is the story's set piece: Barnard and DCM's David Chao jammed a 20-foot tower into Chao's Mercedes, drove it to Son's Woodside mansion for a 15-minute slot that ran to 45, and flew to Tokyo two weeks later. The $200 million, announced in late July (Bezos Expeditions also invested), was to fund a farm in every metro area of more than 1 million people, about 500 in all, starting with China, Japan and the Middle East, then Canada, Denmark and Ireland. Barnard was meeting officials from 15 governments on four continents plus executives from Walmart and Amazon, and courting insurers and pension funds for debt, promising investor payback in three to five years versus 20 to 40 for a traditional farm. "Think of it more like a utility," he says.

## The economic argument

- **Demand gap:** Emory public-health researchers put the world's fruit and vegetable supply 22% short of nutritional need, and widening.
- **Logistics waste:** the US imports about 35% of its fruit and vegetables (Bain); leafy greens, mostly from California and Arizona, travel an average of 2,000 miles; logistics are more than a third of the retail price of produce. Barnard's claim is that he saves more on trucking than he spends on warehousing and power.
- **Shelf life as the retailer hook:** Bain's Mikey Vu says grocers "would love to get another four to five days of shelf life for leafy greens."
- **The promise in the headline:** Whole Foods quality at Walmart prices.

## The skeptics, as quoted

Wang gives the doubts real space. Sonny Ramaswamy of the USDA's National Institute of Food and Agriculture asks whether quality control survives "seriously scaling." Michael Hamm of Michigan State notes that no vertical farm has got "very far beyond greens," and "people only eat so many greens." Wang's own cost list: urban real estate, the electricity to run everything, tree fruit and root crops that neutralize the technology's advantages, and incumbents with decades of grocer relationships and very low prices across a far wider range. The graveyard already held PodPonics (Atlanta), FarmedHere (Chicago) and Local Garden (Vancouver), felled by design flaws, premature timing when hardware was expensive, or missing expertise in either farming or finance.

## Synthesis

**The energy bill is the business.** A vertical farm replaces the sun with purchased photons. Every calorie of growth passes through the electricity meter, so LED efficiency and power prices, not horticulture, set the unit economics. This is the decisive contrast with Dutch glasshouse horticulture ([[netherlands-agriculture]]), which runs on free sunlight with geothermal heat and had already captured most of the water advantage: under four gallons per kilogram of tomatoes against Plenty's "1%" claim, at a fraction of the energy cost. Greenhouse operators such as Gotham Greens came through the later shakeout; the pure-LED players mostly did not.

**Why greens, and only greens.** Lettuce and herbs have short cycles, high value per kilogram, and low photon demand per dollar of output. They are the only crops that pencil, which is why Hamm's objection was structural rather than a matter of R&D time. Calorie crops (grains, roots, tree fruit) never will under this cost structure.

**Asset-heavy by choice.** Storey's line about build speed marks the moment Plenty chose to be a manufacturer and operator of farms rather than an equipment vendor. That is the capital-intensive path, and it is what the cheap-money capital markets of 2017–2021 funded and the rate and energy shocks of 2022–23 punished. Damodaran's point in [[scaling-vs-profitability]] applies: capital intensity determines how large a business can get, not whether it makes money, and the "utility" framing assumed the second before proving the first.

**Which claims held.** Taste and quality (chefs kept endorsing it), water efficiency, and the shelf-life value to grocers all held up; the Walmart relationship Wang mentions as a conversation became a real deal. What failed was the price side of "Whole Foods quality at Walmart prices" once power and capital were repriced.

## Temporal notes

- **Plenty.** January 2022: a $400 million Series E with Walmart investing, joining the board, and contracting to stock Plenty greens in its California stores; Arama Kukutai replaced Barnard as CEO. The Compton, California farm opened in 2023. In March 2025 Plenty filed for Chapter 11, closed Compton, and emerged focused on a single strawberry farm in Richmond, Virginia, making the "beyond greens" bet explicit, eight years and roughly a billion dollars after Hamm named it.
- **The sector shakeout.** Kalera and AeroFarms filed for Chapter 11 in 2023 (AeroFarms later re-emerged under new owners), AppHarvest followed in July 2023, Infarm wound down most of its European operations in 2023, and Bowery Farming, which had raised well over $600 million, shut down in November 2024. The common triggers were the 2022 electricity-price spike and the end of near-zero interest rates: the two costs Wang listed.
- **SoftBank.** The Vision Fund that made Plenty the largest agtech round became the era's cautionary tale for capital-heavy "tech" bets after WeWork in 2019.

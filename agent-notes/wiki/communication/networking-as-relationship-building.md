# Networking as Relationship-Building

Danielle Robay — TV host, journalist, host of *Question Everything*, Gloria Steinem Endowed Fellow — argues on Tori Dunlap's *Financial Feminist* that the reason ambitious people network constantly and earn nothing extra is that they are running the wrong instrument. They optimize the **cold ask**; the thing that actually produces opportunities is a **portfolio of relationships built before there was anything to ask for**.

The framing is explicitly financial and Dunlap makes the analogy load-bearing: *"That is the way that you teach women to invest money today so that it 10xes in 10 years. It's the exact same thing with relationship building."* Robay's version of the thesis in one line: **"Being transactional is the death of networking."**

The episode is aimed at women and several of the failure modes it names are gendered (over-giving, unpaid labor, difficulty saying no). The mechanisms are not — they generalize.

## The core reframe

Robay rejects the word *networking* and substitutes **relationship-building**, then defines it as three things:

1. **Ask more and better questions.**
2. **Listen deeply.**
3. **Play the long game.**

Only (3) is contested, and it is where she says everyone fails: people want the payoff now. Her causal chain is *consistency + value given without scorekeeping → trust → reputation → opportunities arrive unbidden.* The stated end-state is that you stop having to push at all.

An important framing note from her friend Tayron: **"Just because you're good at connecting with people doesn't mean it's not a skill."** Robay treats her own instincts as decomposable and teachable rather than as temperament — the same move Prasad makes in [[asking-for-help]] ("not an attribute you are assigned at birth like green eyes").

## The ask

### What makes it bad

Robay and Dunlap converge on the same canonical failure as Prasad: **"Can I pick your brain?"** — and its sibling, "I'd love 15 minutes of your time." Robay's objection is a **labor-asymmetry** one, sharper than a mere complaint about vagueness: *"it took them about 10 seconds to write that message. It's going to take me 30 minutes to talk to them."* A vague ask is a transfer of work from the sender to the recipient.

Also disqualifying: emails that are too long, and emails that read as purely self-serving.

### What makes it good

A good ask is short, specific, and demonstrates **two** things:

- **How this person has actually impacted you** (specific, checkable — an episode, a post, a paper).
- **How you can add value to them.**

Robay says the second half is the one she omitted for years in her own cold emails: she made the connection point ("I saw you were a poli-sci major, so am I") but never solved a problem the recipient had. The connection buys attention; only the offer converts it.

**Tactical:** put **"Quick question"** in the subject line — she reports a materially higher open rate. This works because the subject *pre-prices the recipient's cost*, which is the same mechanism behind Prasad's "unsummarizable context" and the WPEngine price-their-time move in [[asking-for-help]]. It is also a promise: if the question is not actually quick, the hack spends credibility rather than building it, and at any volume it decays into spam.

### Calibrate the ask to your vantage point

Robay's rule against outrageous asks is not "don't aim high" — it is **don't spend a shot you will want later.** Her example: an acquaintance planning to email Joe Rogan for podcasting tips should instead email someone one rung up. The reason is not modesty but option value — Dunlap's version is *"I know I'm going to need this thing later, so it's too early to ask for that right now."*

She did cold-email Mark Cuban, and he replied — but she waited until she had something worth offering him (an invitation to a Gloria Steinem talking circle on masculinity). The counterfactual she names is the real cost: a "sorry, can't help" reply is not a neutral outcome, it is a burned first contact.

### The pain-point email

The single most concrete tactic in the episode. Robay relays a (unnamed, unverified) story of someone new to New York who emailed one person at each target company asking **"What's the biggest pain point for you right now?"**, then replied with a solution — and got interviews at Google, Twitter, and Microsoft.

The job-interview variant, from Dunlap: ask *"What is the biggest struggle you're having that someone in this role would help solve?"* during the interview, then send a **30-day roadmap** for solving it as the follow-up.

This is the question-driven elicitation of [[consultative-selling]] applied to yourself as the product — establish the problem and its consequences in the buyer's own words before proposing anything.

## The follow-up is the relationship

Robay's strongest structural claim: most people treat the meeting as the event and the follow-up as optional. It is the reverse. *"The follow-up is actually the complete follow through. That is what matters."*

Her template — **share, ask, offer, connect, invite** (offer/connect/invite are interchangeable; you don't need all of them):

| Step | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Share** | Quote a specific moment from the conversation back to them. Proves you were present, not networking. |
| **Ask** | The concrete request, with scheduling friction pre-absorbed ("happy to make myself available at your convenience" or "I'm free Mondays and Wednesdays, pick a day"). |
| **Offer** | Something you can actually do for them. |
| **Connect** | An intro or a resource they mentioned wanting. |
| **Invite** | Access to something — an event, an experience. Access is the currency you can hold even with no track record. |

The *share* step is the load-bearing one and the hardest to fake: it requires having listened for something quotable, which is why "ask better questions" and "listen deeply" are prerequisites rather than platitudes.

## Give first, don't keep score

The anecdote Robay uses to anchor the long game: a US general who served five presidents said **Bush Sr. spent the last fifteen minutes of every day calling a different world leader with nothing to ask** — just to talk. So when he did need a favor, *"they had a shorthand."*

This is the argument for pre-building. The favor is not cheaper because he was owed one; it is cheaper because the *channel already existed* and the call did not itself have to carry the relationship.

**"High earners don't act like they need anything."** Robay's version of what distinguishes them: not detachment as a pose, but genuine non-attachment to the outcome — *"they could smell it on you"* when she was 22 and needy. Her prescribed method for producing it is not acting: **think about what you can give them before you enter the room.** Read charitably, this is less a psychological trick than a description of what having pre-built relationships feels like from the inside. If you built the channel early you genuinely don't need this conversation to work.

### Handling the "isn't that inauthentic?" objection

The objection: *I do want something, so pretending otherwise is a performance.* Robay and Dunlap's answer is a **taxonomy, not a rebuttal**:

- If you genuinely just need one thing from this person, **it's an ask, not networking.** That's legitimate. Make it, spend the shot, and expect nothing further — *"you've shot your shot."*
- Relationship-building is the separate, longer instrument. Confusing the two is what makes people feel fake: they run a transaction and narrate it as a friendship.

Dunlap's addendum from her own friendship with Robay: once relationship capital exists, work asks and friendship coexist without contaminating each other — *"a separation of church and state."* The precondition she names is **conversational capital already banked**, plus mutual acknowledged ambition, so neither party feels judged for asking.

### The overcorrection

Giving too much also fails. Robay describes doing an unsolicited **three-page audit of a founder's business** to justify a conversation; Dunlap's read is that it lands like sending a room full of flowers after a first date. Value given must be proportionate to the relationship's stage — the cost is not the recipient's time to read it, it's the implicit debt it creates and the desperation it signals.

## Physical artifacts and persistence

Robay's origin story: broke in LA, $8,000 in the bank, no replies. She sent **bamboo plants and cookies with handwritten notes** to company addresses (she couldn't get individual emails). Bamboo specifically **because flowers die** — *"I wanted them to look at their desk and think about me every single day."*

It did not produce a job directly. It produced **replies** — "no openings, but thanks for the cookies" — which was the actual goal: an email thread now existed. Eventually a company with a sick host called her at 2pm for a 6pm shoot.

Two honest caveats, one of which Robay raises herself:

- She notes cookies are a post-anthrax-era problem; unsolicited food to strangers reads differently now than in the early 2010s.
- **This tactic is in direct tension with the Hacker News consensus recorded in [[asking-for-help]]**, where `Aurornis` argues that *unusual effort invested in a stranger is a known scam tell*, and `jackconsidine` reports 100 handwritten notes producing zero replies against short emails producing 15%. The reconciliation is probably that the gift's function is **channel-creation under a hard constraint** (she had no personal email address and no credibility to lead with), not persuasion — and that it fails when used as a substitute for a legible ask rather than as a way to earn one. Note also that a gift is a reciprocity lever in the [[cialdini-influence]] sense, which cuts against Prasad's "make it easy to say no."

Her advice for the modern version of the same constraint: **get to a person, past the applicant-tracking robot.** If the only findable human is an assistant, contact the assistant — *"assistants don't get gifts a lot"* — and ask who you should be talking to.

She cites (2025) that **54% of Americans worked at a company because they knew someone there, and 70% knew someone at the company before starting.** Treat these as podcast-cited and unverified; as stated in the transcript the two figures are hard to reconcile with each other.

## Networking is not socializing

Robay draws a hard line: friends who go to day parties and call it networking are **socializing**. Networking is intentional. *"We don't need to be in every room. We don't need to be in everybody's inbox."*

But she and Dunlap refuse the pure-selectivity position too. Dunlap's job-search version: LinkedIn quick-apply spraying fails *and* one email a month fails. **Both intentionality and volume are required** — the volume just has to be aimed. Dunlap adds a motivational reason for aiming it: undirected volume produces no returns, no returns kills the will to continue.

On what makes some conversations convert and others not, Robay declines to systematize: *"I don't go into a conversation expecting any ROI. I go in wanting presence."* Dunlap's gloss is the operative one — **systematizing this visibly is self-defeating, because people can feel it.** The template above is scaffolding for the follow-up, not a script for the room.

Her go-to opener when a real question fits: **"If you were to give a TED Talk in 10 minutes, what would you give it on?"** — it surfaces their actual expertise and is memorable. Her interview-prep question, which she suggests generalizes: **"What can this person teach me that nobody else can?"**

## The warm-intro protocol

Asked how to handle a three-way introduction thread, Robay's answer starts upstream: **the connector's job is to pre-warm the recipient.** An intro made without asking first lands cold and the recipient is measurably less warm to the newcomer.

For the person being introduced:

1. **"Moving [connector] to BCC to not crowd her inbox"** — demonstrates email etiquette and that you won't waste the connector's attention.
2. Something specific about the recipient — 10–20 minutes of actual research.
3. **State plainly why you want to speak. Do not leave it open-ended.** This is the one place Robay endorses being explicitly "transactional": naming the agenda gets you further, because *"people want to know what conversation they're getting into."*

Point 3 is the sharpest correction to the vague-ask failure — and it also resolves part of the specificity-vs-serendipity tension in [[asking-for-help]]. Be specific about **what conversation this is**, which is different from being specific about **what answer you need**.

## Build the room

Robay's headline prescription, and the most structurally interesting idea in the episode: **don't try to get invited — throw the thing yourself.** A six-person dinner counts.

> *"If you want a friend, you have to be a friend. If you want the village, you gotta be the mayor."*

The mechanism: an inviter's network grows **around them** rather than at the edge of someone else's. Attendees talk about the dinner, others ask in, the host decides who is at the table. In graph terms it converts you from a leaf into a hub, and it does so without needing any of the credibility sources Prasad ranks in [[asking-for-help]] — you don't need proof of work, a warm connection, or an institution to *convene* people. This may be the single cheapest available move for someone with nothing to lead with, which is presumably why Robay ranks it first.

The cost she is honest about: **it stays scary.** She has hosted Talking Circles at Gloria Steinem's apartment often enough for the *New York Times* and *GQ* to cover them and still gets anxious every time she sends invitations. *"Questions are an invitation"* — and invitations can be declined, which is why both asking and hosting feel the same kind of exposed. Her advice is the unglamorous one: feel it and do it anyway. (Cf. [[vulnerability-and-shame]] on exposure as the precondition for connection rather than the obstacle to it.)

Dunlap's complement: end conversations with **"What can I do for you?"** — being the first to offer usually obliges the other person to ask it back.

## Boundaries: the invented family

As Robay's platform grew, her natural-giver disposition became exploitable — people who read her as nice pushed harder on her than on others. Her fix, which she calls unhinged and which works: **she imagines she has a husband and two kids** and runs every request through that filter. *If I had that life, could I say yes to this?*

Dunlap connects it to negotiation research: **women negotiate more effectively on behalf of someone else than for themselves.** The invented family manufactures a third party to advocate for. It is worth naming what this is — a workaround for a legitimacy deficit, not a solution to it — and Dunlap says as much (*"it makes me sad"*).

The related failure Robay names: women disproportionately offer **free labor and time**, which produces being *liked* rather than *paid*. Her prescription for the transition from "helpful/interesting" to "valuable" is unromantic and slow — **real expertise, visibly working.** Once people see you plugged into something that works, they want a piece of it.

## What this adds beyond the neighbouring articles

[[asking-for-help]] optimizes a single cold approach to a stranger. Robay's claim is that **the cold approach is the low-yield instrument**, and that most of the achievable outcome is determined before it is sent — by whether a channel already exists. The two are complements: Prasad tells you how to spend the shot well, Robay tells you to stop needing it.

The credibility substrate both take as given is the subject of [[credibility-based-selling]] (sales as a byproduct of credibility plus genuine excitement), and Robay's give-first-and-don't-keep-score is a near-restatement of that essay's "make friends, introduce people, expect nothing in return." The reciprocity dynamics — gifts, the obliged return-question, ask-big-then-retreat — are catalogued in [[cialdini-influence]]. The pain-point elicitation is [[consultative-selling]] pointed at your own candidacy.

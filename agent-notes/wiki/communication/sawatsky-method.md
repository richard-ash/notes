# The Sawatsky Method

The **Sawatsky method** is John Sawatsky's system for interviewing: short, neutral, open-ended questions, no statements, and no values smuggled into the wording. Sawatsky was a Canadian investigative reporter (two books on Canada's security service, a 1991 biography of Brian Mulroney) who turned to studying the interview itself and then to training newsrooms in what he found. Susan Paterno's *American Journalism Review* profile "The Question Man" is the standard short account. It reports his rules, how he arrived at them, and the objections of the television interviewers he uses as bad examples. Everything below is Sawatsky's argument as Paterno reports it, unless marked otherwise.

His core claim is that a good interview is not magic. "It happens for an understandable reason. It's rational. It's a skill." The question is a tool with predictable effects, and most interviewers use it badly because they follow the instincts of conversation.

## Where the method came from

Sawatsky began teaching investigative reporting in 1982. When he started the Mulroney biography he used his students as researchers, and because he did not trust them to improvise, the class wrote a standard list of questions each week. Every student asked a different source the same questions and brought back a transcript. "What came back humbled me." Some questions worked on almost everyone and others failed on almost everyone. He kept running the exercise for years and calls the class "a laboratory into interviewing."

This is the reason to take the method more seriously than most interviewing advice: the same wording was tried across many respondents, so the effect of the wording can be separated from the luck of the source. It is also the limit of the evidence. Nothing was published, the "thousands of interviews" and the "98 percent" figure below are his own report, and the sample was sources for a political biography.

## The mechanism: closed questions run on charity

Ask a closed question, one that can be answered yes or no, and the source "will confirm or deny 98 percent of the time. That's the science." What happens next is the unpredictable part. Social habit teaches people to add a postscript to a yes or a no, and the interviewer is hoping the postscript will contain the description or explanation they actually wanted. Sawatsky calls this "interviewing by accident."

In a friendly interview the postscript usually comes, "out of charity." His point is that the charity delivered the answer and the question did not. That matters because charity is distributed badly: "The ones we need charity from the most are the least likely to give it—the people who stand to lose something." He names two groups who withhold it:

- **Literal and fearful people.** Police, bureaucrats, lawyers, anyone nervous. They answer exactly what was asked and stop. "The last thing fearful people do is open up."
- **Professional answer-givers.** Trained politicians and executives. They give a curt "No, not at all" and then switch to a **message track**, the prepared answer they came to deliver. The closed question hands them the opening.

An open question removes the yes/no exit. There is nothing to confirm or deny, so the source has to produce content to answer at all.

## The rules

1. **Ask open questions.** Begin with *what*, *how* or *why*, and to a lesser degree *who*, *when* and *where*.
2. **Never make a statement.** A statement is not a question, so it need not be answered, and a source with something to hide uses it as an "exit ramp." Paterno shows this on herself. She asks Sawatsky "What holes?" about gaps in his own method, then adds "I didn't see any holes in what you did today," and he takes the exit.
3. **Keep your values out of the wording.** Larry King's run of "Are you bitter?" "Angry?" "Were you surprised?" "Did you feel guilty?" gets four flat noes before "disappointed" lands. Each question is the interviewer's guess at the answer. Sawatsky's replacements ask for the same thing with no guess in them: not "Is it hard being a duchess?" but "What's it like being a duchess?"; not "Were you scared when you were shot?" but "What's it like to be shot?"
4. **Keep questions short.** Dan Rather, interviewing Mirjana Markovic about Kosovo, gains ground with "How does this end?" and loses it with long accusatory ones. Mike Wallace's "nine-pound question" to John Ehrlichman gets "Is there a question in there?"
5. **Follow up with the source's own words.** If someone says "Brian can be excessive at times," the next question is "What do you mean, excessive?" It adds nothing of the interviewer's and makes the source unpack the loaded word they chose.
6. **Go basic before specific.** A reporter in Oslo asked Sawatsky "Don't you ever get bored doing the workshop over and over?" and got his message track. She caught herself and asked "How do you feel about giving this workshop?" He answered "frustrated," a word he says he had never used about it. His diagnosis: "she asked about being bored. The content of the second question changed from specific to basic." The first question tested one feeling the interviewer had picked. The second let him pick.
7. **Sound conversational, but do not converse.** "The goal of a conversation is to exchange information; the goal of an interview is to receive information." The social instincts that make someone pleasant company (sympathize, fill a silence, offer a way out, show you understand) each give the source something to respond to other than the question. Paterno describes wanting to rescue Sawatsky from her own question because she likes him.

## Input and output

Sawatsky's summary rule, "the granddaddy of all rules," is to balance input and output. **Outputting** is making a statement, voicing an opinion, inserting a value. **Inputting** is asking neutral open questions. When the source is outputting the interviewer should be inputting, and trying to do both at once fails: "you can't suck and blow at the same time."

He thinks people lean one way by temperament and that television rewards outputters. His newsroom observation is that the best stories come from "the reporters with the blander personality," whose questions are plain. "Colorless questions usually provide colorful answers."

The image he uses is a window. A question should be clean glass, so the audience looks through it at the source. Values in a question are dirt on the glass. A performer's question is stained glass: "beautiful to look at, but it's all about the window, not about the view."

## Tough is not accusatory

Paterno places the method against two older styles. The deferential interview of the 1950s gave way after Watergate to a prosecutorial one in which, as CBC's Carole MacNeil puts it, "We were trained to appear to be tough by asking accusatory questions." Sawatsky argues the prosecutorial style looks tough and performs weakly. An accusation such as Rather's "When will you stop killing Kosovar Albanians?" is a closed question with a value in it. It can be denied in one word, and the denial becomes the platform. Rather had what he called provable facts. Sawatsky's alternative is to ask about the specific incidents one at a time, so the source must either agree to each or produce evidence against it.

He calls this judo. The interviewer is a generalist on the expert's ground and loses a head-on fight. Making the source explain and substantiate everything turns the interviewer's ignorance into the working method: "Don't fight their superior knowledge, use it."

His sharpest target is Sam Donaldson's defense of the pointed question that "hangs in the air" so the audience notices the non-answer. Sawatsky calls that "an avowal of failure" and redefines the standard: "Questions that fail to get answers are not tough enough."

## The objections

The profile carries the replies, and they should be kept with the method.

- **Donaldson:** "You can't always ask questions that are guaranteed to get answers." If a source will not answer, you press to a point and then move on, because nobody wants to watch a reporter badger.
- **60 Minutes**, on the Ehrlichman tape: Wallace "did exactly the right thing."
- **Paterno's own experience.** Her clean, repeated "Where are the holes?" got silence and a travelogue about Yonge Street. She got the answer a day later, and she attributes it to "his charity," which is the thing the method says not to rely on.

The third point is the most useful. The method raises the cost of evasion and removes the easy exits. It does not compel anyone. A source who is willing to sit in silence or change the subject can still do so. What it does guarantee is that the evasion is visible as evasion, which is roughly what Donaldson wants from his hanging question, reached by a route that also works on cooperative sources.

A second gap is that broadcast interviewing has two audiences. Wallace and Donaldson are also making television, and a confrontation can be the product. Sawatsky judges a question only by the information it returns. The two sides are partly measuring different things.

## Beyond journalism

The method was built for reporters, but its logic applies wherever one person needs true information from another who has reasons to shade it. Read that way it is the general theory under several narrower practices in this wiki.

- **Customer discovery.** The errors in [[customer-interviews]] are Sawatsky errors under other names. A pitch is output. "Would you use this?" is a closed question with a value in it, and the polite yes it earns is the charity postscript working against the interviewer. The Mom Test tells founders what to ask about (past behavior, one specific story); Sawatsky supplies the reason the wording matters and a rule for the follow-up. The two differ on one axis. Story-based interviewing pushes toward the specific episode, while Sawatsky's Oslo example pushes toward the basic question. They are compatible if "specific" is split in two: be specific about the event you ask the person to recall, and basic about what you ask them to say of it. "Tell me about the last time" does both.
- **Founders as outputters.** The newsroom observation predicts who will find this hard. People who are good at pitching are outputters by trade, and a discovery call asks them to run the opposite program for thirty minutes. The tag question ("that's a big problem, right?") is the smallest form of the failure: a statement with a closed question attached.
- **Negotiation.** Voss's calibrated questions in [[negotiator-types]] are *what* and *how* questions, and his mirror is the echo follow-up in rule 5. The same article's reading of silence explains why Sawatsky's no-rescue rule is hard: for an Accommodator, silence reads as anger, so the urge to fill it is strongest in the people most inclined to build rapport.
- **Selling.** [[consultative-selling]] depends on the buyer describing their own problem at length. A seller who states the problem for them and asks for agreement gets the confirm-or-deny Sawatsky describes.

## Assessment

- **Strong on mechanism, thin on proof.** The charity argument explains a pattern most interviewers have seen and gives a testable prediction: closed questions fail hardest on hostile, literal and trained sources. The supporting data is one practitioner's unpublished classroom work, reported in a favorable profile. Roy Peter Clark's endorsement ("the most systematic thinker on interviewing that I've ever met") and the CBC testimonials are opinion.
- **The rules are easy to state and hard to do.** Paterno calls the execution "about as natural as walking on hot coals," and breaks the no-statement rule one sentence after following the open-question rule. The difficulty is that every rule runs against a social reflex, so the skill is mostly suppression under mild discomfort.
- **Closed questions still have a job.** Sawatsky's own prescription for Rather ends in confirm-or-deny on specific facts. The method is against opening with closed questions and relying on the postscript. Pinning down a fact after the open questions have produced the account is a different use.
- **Tone is outside the rules.** Paterno notes her wording was neutral while her voice was "demanding, almost hostile." A clean question asked as an accusation carries a value anyway.

## Temporal notes

The profile is from 2000, and Paterno describes a method still under construction with a book planned. Two later developments are not in the source and are recorded here from the compiler's general knowledge, unverified: Sawatsky joined ESPN in the mid-2000s as its in-house interview coach, which is how the method reached American broadcasting, and the planned book on interviewing does not appear to have been published. The profile and accounts of his workshops remain the main written record.

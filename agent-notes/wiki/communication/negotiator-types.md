# Negotiator Types

The Black Swan Group, Chris Voss's negotiation training firm, sorts every counterpart into one of three types: **Analyst**, **Accommodator**, or **Assertive**. Its short guide argues that the most common negotiating mistake is the golden rule. You treat the other side the way you would want to be treated, which means you project your own type onto them. A direct person sees directness as respect; the guide says it "can just as easily come off as an attack to someone with a different negotiating style." The fix is to treat counterparts how *they* wish to be treated and leave your own preferences out of it.

The guide claims there are "only three types of negotiators," and also that the best negotiators carry traits of all three and shift style to fit the person in front of them. So the types are natural defaults, and the skill is moving off your own.

## The three types side by side

The guide gives each type its own card. The cards share the same fields, so they can be read across:

| | Analyst | Accommodator | Assertive |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cares about | Acquiring facts and information | Building relationships | Being heard |
| Time is | Preparation | Relationship building | Money |
| Silence is | Time to think | Upsetting, a sign of anger | An opportunity to speak more |
| Sees self as | Realistic, prepared, smart | Personable, conversational, relationship focused | Honest, logical, direct |
| Seen by others as | Cold, standoffish | Friendly, too talkative | Emotional, aggressive, harsh |
| Business relationships | Not causing conflict *is* preserving the relationship | The relationship is most important | Mutual respect, nothing more or less |
| When they give | Only what they have already thought long and hard about | Most likely to give first, and not in order to receive | Counts the seconds until they get equal or more back |
| When they are given to | Receiving first "must be a trap" | Flattered; takes it as confirmation of a good relationship | "Give them an inch, and they'll take a mile" |
| Tools to use | Labels that compare analysis; data to explain your reasons and to disagree, no ad-lib | *What* and *how* calibrated questions focused on implementation | Mirrors, calibrated questions, labels, summaries; get a "that's right" |
| To get them back | Show you are ready to get something accomplished | An apology ("I'm sorry" is mandatory) | Any invitation to re-engage |
| Worst match | Assertive | Accommodator | Analyst |

One wrinkle in the source: on the Assertive card the "Giving" and "Receiving" labels are written from the other side of the table (giving *to* them, receiving *from* them), the reverse of the other two cards. The table above puts all three in the counterpart's own perspective.

## Reading each type

**Analyst.** Methodical and diligent, hates surprises, and ties their self-image to minimizing mistakes. Prefers to work alone; a reserved problem solver and information aggregator; skeptical by nature. The guide flags three things to expect: they "may appear to agree when just agreeing to think about it," they are slow to answer calibrated questions, and apologies have little value to them.

**Accommodator.** Happy when communicating; sociable, peace-seeking, optimistic; distractible and a poor time manager. They will not put hesitancy into words, so the guide says to watch tone and body language. The named risk is overpromising: agreeing to give you something they cannot actually deliver.

**Assertive.** Getting it done matters more than perfecting the solution, and they love winning above all else. The type most prone to tunnel vision: fixed on one goal, they miss other options, and emotion can cloud their decisions. They view negotiation as intellectual sparring. They focus first on what they have to say and "will only listen if they're convinced you understand them."

## What the guide leaves implicit

The points below are synthesis from the cards, not claims the guide makes.

**Silence means three different things.** The same five-second pause is working time to an Analyst, a sign of anger to an Accommodator, and an opening to an Assertive. Anyone who uses silence deliberately is using three different tools depending on who is across the table. The projection error runs the other way too: an Accommodator facing a quiet Analyst will read thinking as displeasure and may rush to fill the gap, possibly with a concession nobody asked for.

**Reciprocity is not a uniform lever.** [[cialdini-influence]] treats reciprocity as a near-automatic response and recommends refusing the opening gift as the defense. The guide says the response depends on type. The Analyst already runs Cialdini's defense as a temperament: a gift received first "must be a trap." The Accommodator returns warmth, which is not the same as returning substance. The Assertive pockets the inch and asks for the mile. An unprompted concession meant to build goodwill works roughly as intended on one type in three.

**Each type has its own false yes.** An Analyst's "yes" may mean "I will think about it." An Accommodator's "yes" may be a promise they cannot keep, with the hesitation visible only in tone. For the Assertive the guide names the true signal instead: "that's right," evidence that they feel heard. The tool the guide prescribes for the Accommodator, *what* and *how* questions about implementation, is the same move [[customer-interviews]] takes from Fitzpatrick, where asking for commitment is the antidote to compliments. A pleasant yes is not information until it has been tested against concrete steps.

**Repair moves are type-specific and nearly opposite.** "I'm sorry" is mandatory for an Accommodator and close to worthless for an Analyst, who wants evidence that something will get done. An Assertive needs only an invitation back. The golden-rule error applies to repair as much as to the opening: an Accommodator who has annoyed an Analyst will reach for the apology they themselves would want.

**The two kinds of worst match fail differently.** The guide lists the pairings without explaining them. A plausible reading: Analyst and Assertive are each other's worst match because every field collides. Time as preparation meets time as money, the Assertive fills the silence the Analyst needs, and each side's self-image (prepared, direct) is the other's complaint (stalling, attacking). Two Accommodators fail the opposite way, with no friction at all: both enjoy the conversation, neither voices hesitation, both overpromise, and the meeting ends warm with nothing deliverable agreed.

**Type yourself first.** Since the mistake is projection, the useful row for self-diagnosis is "seen by others as." Each type's self-description and its outside reading barely overlap: the Assertive who feels honest, logical and direct is the one others experience as emotional and harsh.

## The vocabulary

The guide assumes Voss's toolkit without defining it. The definitions below come from Voss's *Never Split the Difference* (2016, with Tahl Raz), which also presents the same three types in its chapter on bargaining. They are background, not content of the guide.

- **Label.** Naming the counterpart's feeling or position out loud: "It seems like…", "It sounds like…".
- **Mirror.** Repeating their last few words back as a question, to keep them talking.
- **Calibrated question.** An open-ended *what* or *how* question that hands the counterpart the problem to solve ("How am I supposed to do that?").
- **Summary.** A paraphrase of their position combined with a label of how they feel about it.
- **"That's right."** What a counterpart says when a summary lands. Voss distinguishes it from "you're right," which he treats as a way to make you go away.

Read against the "cares about" row, the tool assignments follow: the Assertive gets the whole listening set because being heard is the thing they want; the Analyst gets data and labels that compare analyses because facts are the thing they want; the Accommodator gets implementation questions because their agreement comes easily and the delivery is what needs checking.

## Connections

- [[asking-for-help]] reduces to one principle, "put yourself in their mind." The types are a coarse lookup table for what is in that mind, and the guide's rejection of the golden rule is the same principle stated as a prohibition.
- [[consultative-selling]]: Cheng's rule of eliciting problem, consequences and needs before any pitch, and his tactic of quoting the buyer's own words back, are a summary aimed at "that's right." That is the listening sequence the guide prescribes for an Assertive, who will not listen until they feel understood.
- [[multi-stakeholder-selling]]: a buying group will usually contain more than one type. Cheng's one-on-one pre-syndication is also the place where style can be matched person by person, which a group meeting does not allow.
- [[networking-as-relationship-building]]: Robay's relationship-first, ask-later position is the Accommodator's native view of business relationships. By the guide's cards it lands well on Accommodators, costs an Assertive time they price as money, and an unprompted gift is exactly what an Analyst reads as a trap.

## Assessment

- **Incentive.** This is a lead-generation PDF from a firm that sells negotiation training, and it closes with a contact page. It cites no evidence for the typology or for the claim that there are only three types. It is best held as a practitioner heuristic from experienced negotiators, not a validated personality model.
- **Types are defaults, not people.** The guide itself says the best negotiators blend all three. A type assigned to a counterpart is a hypothesis to revise as the conversation produces evidence, and the same person may sit differently when the stakes or the setting change.
- **No diagnostic procedure.** The guide lists traits but not how to tell early which type you are facing. The table suggests cheap probes: go quiet for a few seconds and watch what they do with the silence, notice whether small talk is welcomed or endured, and see how a small concession is received.
- **Practice claim.** The guide ends by saying that typing people and adapting under pressure takes repetition, and that everyday low-stakes conversations are the place to get it.

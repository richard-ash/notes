# JWST's Early-Universe Puzzles

Since the James Webb Space Telescope came online in 2022, its infrared view into the first billion years after the Big Bang has produced three linked anomalies that don't fit pre-launch astrophysics: black holes that are **too big** for their age, galaxies that are **too bright** and abundant, and a wholly new class of object — the **"little red dots"** — that had never been seen before. As of mid-2026 the field has moved from shock to a surfeit of explanations. Rachel Somerville (Flatiron Institute) summarized the state of play at an April 2026 conference in Helsingør, Denmark: "We almost have gone from having too many early galaxies to having too many theories to explain them." The open problem is no longer *can we explain this* but *which explanation is real*.

## Puzzle 1 — Supermassive black holes too big, too soon

JWST keeps finding billion-solar-mass black holes only a few hundred million years after the Big Bang. Their size is governed by two knobs: the mass of the initial **"seed"** and how fast it grew. Both are hard to push far enough.

- **Stellar seeds + super-Eddington growth.** Collapsing early massive stars leave seeds up to ~100 solar masses. Normally the **Eddington limit** caps growth — an accretion disk's radiation pushes back on infalling gas — but simulations suggest a "back door": if the disk puffs up correctly, gas can overwhelm the radiation pressure and funnel in at **super-Eddington** rates. Supporting evidence: in 2024 JWST caught a black hole ~1.5 billion years after the Big Bang accreting at ~40× the Eddington limit. Open question: was there enough gas around to feed the very biggest?
- **Dense-cluster seed mergers.** Ancient dense star clusters may have spawned many small black-hole seeds that rapidly merged.
- **Direct collapse.** A giant gas cloud plunges straight to a black hole without ever forming stars, yielding a ~10,000-solar-mass seed. Jenny Greene (Princeton) calls this the "Goldilocks" scenario: it needs specific gas chemistry, slow rotation, and no fragmentation into stars first. Simulations *can* make direct-collapse black holes but **not enough of them** to account for what's observed. Supporting evidence: a gravitationally lensed object ~750 million years after the Big Bang was identified as a "naked" ~50-million-solar-mass black hole with no surrounding stars — consistent with a large seed forming *before* any galaxy existed.

Greene's read: "There's clearly differences in how the black holes are growing that we don't fully understand yet" — the interesting question is what is physically different between them.

## Puzzle 2 — Galaxies too bright and abundant

Standard picture: dark-matter halos coalesce, their gravity pulls hydrogen/helium along filaments into the halo cores, pressure ignites the first stars, stars assemble into galaxies. Timeline in redshift terms (per Somerville's simulations): little happens until z≈15 (~270 Myr), star formation picks up by z≈11 (~420 Myr), a "nice galaxy" forms by z≈9 (~550 Myr). Yet JWST's most ancient galaxy so far existed just **280 million years** after the Big Bang, and early galaxies are surprisingly bright and numerous.

The initial reaction — that fundamental cosmology itself might be wrong — has largely given way to several astrophysical fixes, likely acting in combination:

- Higher star-formation *efficiency* (more gas converted to stars) than assumed.
- **Bursty** star formation: cycles of intense fusion → supernovae expel gas → star formation halts → gas re-gathers → new burst. JWST's MIRI instrument finds unexpected **diversity** among early galaxies (Hakim Atek, Sorbonne) — some stripped of gas ("naked stars"), some gas-rich — consistent with catching different galaxies at different burst phases.
- A **top-heavy** early stellar population: preferentially massive, very bright stars. A group of galaxies with anomalous **nitrogen** overabundance supports this, since simulations show very massive stars overproduce nitrogen before exploding.

Methodologically, the loop is: simulate the full star-formation history, then match simulated galaxies to observed ones to infer each real galaxy's past. Somerville stresses that numerical simulations have advanced sharply "in the last year or so" and are now more appropriate for the high-redshift regime.

## Puzzle 3 — Little red dots and "black hole stars"

"Little red dots" — hundreds of them, first appearing in significant numbers ~650 million years after the Big Bang — are the most novel find; nothing like them was catalogued before JWST. Leading idea: they are black holes cocooned in thick gas, possibly a new object class called a **black hole star**, where the tight gas shroud radiates like a stellar atmosphere.

Charlotte Mason (Cosmic Dawn Center) has tested the simplest "dense uniform cloud around a black hole" model against a little red dot's spectrum: light passing through such gas should be altered, but that signature wasn't seen. Her revised sketch makes the gas **clumpy** (with holes), which better matches the observed spectrum — an illustration of how these objects are being reverse-engineered from spectra one at a time.

## Why it matters

These first stars and black holes drove **reionization** — the second ionization of the universe's neutral hydrogen, ending the cosmic dark age — and forged the first heavy elements (carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus, iron): "the stuff of planets and of life." The anomalies are not merely bookkeeping problems; they touch how the first structures, and ultimately the material of life, came to be.

The broader epistemic lesson is the inversion Somerville named: an observational surprise that first looked like it might break cosmology has instead spawned an *overabundance* of viable astrophysical models. Progress now depends less on new theory than on observations and simulations sharp enough to **discriminate** between the theories already on the table — a different situation from genuinely anomalous regimes like the [[modified-newtonian-dynamics|dark-matter vs. modified-gravity]] debate, where the competing frameworks make more sharply divergent predictions.

# Military Technology and Political Centralization

The thesis that *who can get hold of weapons* sets the ceiling on how centralized political power can be. Patrick Fitzsimmons's September 2026 Works in Progress essay "Weapons of mass decentralization" is the anchor source. His case study is the shift from bronze to iron at the end of the second millennium BC: bronze depended on tin that traveled thousands of miles, so only palace bureaucracies could arm large forces reliably, and the eastern Mediterranean filled with empires. Iron could be smelted from ores in local soil, so peripheral communities could match the imperial core's weaponry, and after the Bronze Age collapse nothing empire-sized returned for centuries. Fitzsimmons then reads drones and AI as the technologies most likely to shift today's equilibrium.

## The reframed question

Fitzsimmons's entry point is a reframing of the Bronze Age collapse. The standard explanations (droughts, the Sea Peoples, famine, revolt, the disruption of tin trade routes, or all at once) explain why the Hittites, Mycenaeans, Kassite Babylon, and New Kingdom Egypt broke down around 1200 BC. They do not explain why the resulting vacuum stayed empty. Catastrophes normally produce upheaval followed by reconsolidation. Here the Hittite record simply stops, Greece enters its Dark Ages, Egypt shrinks permanently, and Anatolia and the Levant become a patchwork of small states that do not approach imperial scale for centuries. The question he wants answered is not "why did they fall?" but "why did nothing like them return?" Iron is his proposed unifying answer.

## The metal ladder and why iron is the odd rung

The essay's historical spine is a sequence of weapon materials, each with a political consequence:

| Material | Era (approx.) | Battlefield property | Supply property | Political effect per Fitzsimmons |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stone | to ~5000 BC | Chips and breaks on impact | Universal | Baseline |
| Copper | ~5000–3000 BC | Soft but does not break; bends back into shape | Somewhat rare; needs mining, transport, annealing, smelting know-how | Proto-states form; fortified settlements, grave-goods inequality, smelting sites sixty miles from mines |
| Bronze | ~3000–1200 BC | Harder, stiffer, holds an edge; recyclable at 1,085 °C | Tin is very rare; deposits in Afghanistan, France, Iberia, Cornwall | Empires: Hittite expansion under Hattusili I, Egyptian conquest of Nubia and the Levant |
| Iron | from ~1200 BC | *Worse* than good bronze: softer, less predictable, not recyclable by melting | Fourth most abundant element in the crust; ores visible on the surface | Empires cannot reconsolidate; violence democratized |

The first two transitions are ordinary "better weapon wins" stories. The third is not, and that asymmetry is the essay's most interesting move. Good bronze is harder than bloomery iron and can be melted down and recast when a sword breaks. Iron won anyway, because the binding constraint on an army was never the hardness of its blades but the length of its supply chain.

**The tin supply chain.** Trace-element chemistry lets archaeologists source tin to specific deposits; Cornish tin, identifiable by its indium signature, turns up in shipwrecks off Israel and southern France. Texts from Mari on the Euphrates record tin arriving from Elam over five hundred miles away, with Elam itself another thousand miles from the Afghan mines that were the likely origin. Every leg crossed bandit country and foreign states. Fitzsimmons's inference is that larger, more bureaucratic polities were the only ones that could secure supply, hold stockpiles, and replace losses, so they could sustain armies that smaller polities could field only briefly.

**The bloomery.** Iron melts at 1,538 °C, out of reach for the period. The bloomery got around this by not melting it: bellows pushed a charcoal fire to 1,200–1,300 °C, the carbon monoxide stripped oxygen from iron-oxide ore, impurities ran off as slag, and the spongy bloom was hammered into usable metal. The technique probably arose independently in several places (Indian deposits with bloomery byproducts date to around 1400 BC; Georgian copper workshops show experiments) and reached the eastern Mediterranean over a few centuries, though it took a millennium to reach the western Mediterranean at comparable scale. Once known, it needed only charcoal, ore, and a smith. The Danish case Fitzsimmons cites, bog iron from local wetlands smelted with local forest charcoal, is the pattern in miniature: no trader, no trade route, no king.

## The evidence Fitzsimmons marshals

He is candid that the case is circumstantial. The strands:

- **Maps.** No early Iron Age polity in Anatolia, Greece, or the Levant approaches its Bronze Age predecessor in size or organization for centuries.
- **Skeletal trauma.** A multi-millennium study of Middle Eastern remains, using fracture angle and location (violent skull injuries cluster above the hat line) to separate weapon wounds from post-mortem damage, finds more violence in Late Bronze and Iron Age skeletons than in the Middle Bronze Age.
- **Battle databases.** Fitzsimmons's own study finds a sharp rise in the number of recorded battles in the century after a region adopts iron versus the century before. He flags both causation and sample completeness as open.
- **King lists.** Across Assyria, Babylon, and Athens, average reign length falls 29% after iron adoption, and Babylon sees almost twice as many kings. He notes the Athenian list mixes historical figures with mythological ones such as the half-serpent Cecrops I.
- **Ancient testimony.** Hesiod's age of iron as a time of unending toil; Herodotus's "woe lying upon woe"; Lucretius's line that iron made "the odds of war equal as they had not been before"; and 1 Samuel 13:19, in which the Philistines bar smiths from Israel for fear the Hebrews would arm themselves. The last two are as close as the ancient record gets to stating the democratization thesis directly.

## The general framework

Fitzsimmons proposes two axes on which a military technology can shift power:

1. **Offense-defense balance.** Castles let medieval barons hold peasants down and kings at bay until gunpowder made walls obsolete.
2. **Access.** Iron changed who could arm themselves. Firearms strengthened states while new, scarce, and expensive, then became cheap in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries and shifted power toward soldiers and rebels. Nuclear weapons raise the cost of invasion and, for a small state, act as an equalizer.

The essay's contribution is to insist that the second axis, access, is distinct from and often more consequential than the first, and that it is governed by supply-chain geography rather than by the weapon's performance.

## Modern parallels

**Drones.** Fitzsimmons cites Reuters reporting that drones account for up to 80% of casualties in the Russia–Ukraine war as of early 2026. A drone costing a few hundred dollars threatens a tank, artillery piece, or command post worth many multiples of that, which lowers the cost of entry to war and could let small guerrilla groups challenge dominant powers. This is an access technology in his taxonomy, the iron case rather than the castle case.

**AI.** He is more equivocal. Use is decentralized (many individuals can wield it, to potentially destructive effect), but supply is concentrated: providers can cut off access, which puts them in the position of Bronze Age kings sitting on tin stockpiles. Whether AI ends up bronze-like or iron-like depends on which of those facts dominates.

## Synthesis

- **The argument is about recovery, not collapse.** By asking why empires failed to *return*, Fitzsimmons sidesteps the crowded multi-causal debate about 1200 BC and makes a claim about equilibrium rather than shock. That is the strongest framing available to him, because it does not require iron to have caused the collapse, only to have changed what could be rebuilt afterward. It is also where the evidence is thinnest: every strand is correlational, and demographic collapse, loss of literate administration, and a dry climate that persisted for generations are rival explanations for the same missing recovery.

- **The equalizing effect looks like a window, not a permanent state.** The essay stops before the Neo-Assyrian Empire, which from the ninth century BC became larger than any Bronze Age predecessor, and which excavations at Sargon II's palace show was stockpiling iron by the ton. The plausible reading is that iron first leveled the field and then, once a state learned to produce it at industrial scale, re-tilted it. Fitzsimmons's own firearms example runs the same sequence in the other order (centralizing while scarce, decentralizing once cheap). The general pattern is that an access technology resets the equilibrium, and the next empire is whoever masters mass production of the new commodity first. That is a more useful lens for drones than "empires end."

- **Worse material, shorter supply chain.** Iron beat bronze on logistics while losing on metallurgy. The same trade appears in [[drone-air-defense]]: Vernon's gun-fighter drone is sheet metal, never repaired, and built to be numerous rather than good, and his closing claim that manufacturing is not the constraint (a month of Russian and Ukrainian FPV output fits in a few dozen trucks) is the bog-iron condition restated. Fitzsimmons supplies the political consequence Vernon leaves implicit: if the winning weapon is the one anyone can make locally, the fragmented-polity equilibrium follows, and a hegemon's best move is the ally-operated, distributed posture Vernon ends up recommending.

- **Fragmentation as the seedbed of institutions.** Fitzsimmons closes by noting that Greek democracy, Phoenician commerce and alphabetic writing, and the Abrahamic religions all emerged in the fragmented Iron Age world. This is the "efficient market of civilizations" mechanism from [[geography-shapes-history]] with a different cause: Pueyo attributes competition-without-unity to terrain, Fitzsimmons to weapons access. Both predict that a world of many small polities out-innovates a world of one large one. Psmith's account of the Chinese dynastic cycle in [[medieval-chinese-warfare]] treats the Bronze Age collapse as one of the West's rare total breakdowns; the iron thesis suggests it was less a breakdown than a regime change in what scale of state was viable.

- **State capacity reads differently under each metal.** Bronze made state capacity a prerequisite for arms (only a bureaucracy could run the tin trade). Iron reversed the direction: arms became cheap, and state capacity had to be built against an armed periphery. The Besley-Persson mechanism discussed in [[two-europes-divergence]], where external threat forces elites to fund the state, is the iron-age condition. The Bronze Age had the opposite condition, where the state's monopoly on supply made threat manageable without much investment in the periphery.

- **The AI parallel cuts on the supply side.** Fitzsimmons's tin analogy applies to API-gated frontier models. Open-weights models that run on local hardware are the bog-iron case: worse than the frontier, but smeltable in any village. Which regime dominates is an empirical question about the gap between the two, and the essay is right that the answer decides whether AI concentrates or disperses power.

- **Where this sits in time.** Written September 2026, four years into the Russia–Ukraine drone war and alongside the same-month Vernon essay. Fitzsimmons's battle-database study is unpublished as far as the essay indicates; if it appears, it belongs here.

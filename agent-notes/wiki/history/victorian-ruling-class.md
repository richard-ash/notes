# The Victorian Ruling Class

Samuel Hughes argues in *Works in Progress* (October 2026) that the elite who ran nineteenth-century Britain, for much of the century the richest country on Earth and the center of the largest empire in history, did almost nothing that modern success literature prescribes. Their schools were light on academics and nearly empty of science. As adults they rose late, drank heavily, and spent perhaps four or five hours a day on anything recognizable as office work. Hughes's explanation is that the apparent idleness *was* the work: the whole system, from boarding school at eight to the London Season at fifty, was an apparatus for building and maintaining a single intimate ruling community at the top of a vast impersonal society. In Ferdinand Tönnies's terms, a *Gemeinschaft* at the apex of a *Gesellschaft*.

Hughes is careful about how much he claims. The elite may have succeeded in spite of itself, riding an Industrial Revolution it did not cause, and "we cannot run controlled experiments on Victorian Britain." His defensible claim is narrower: a relentless focus on soft skills, team building and intra-elite networking "actually worked rather well," and the Victorians are a standing counterexample to the idea that there is only one way to run a country.

## The puzzle

The scorecard Hughes starts from, on the elite's own terms: British firms dominated global commerce, industry and finance; the navy ruled the oceans; a tiny army won a long run of wars while policing an enormous empire; and Britain moved toward democracy without the revolutions and civil wars that wracked the continent. He grants the longstanding view that this came at the cost of the hundreds of millions Britain subordinated. The question is why a class that looked, by modern standards, feckless and underqualified was so effective at what it set out to do.

Two hypotheses. The parasitic one: the Industrial Revolution made the money and the elite enjoyed it passively. The one Hughes develops: the elite's formation and daily life were optimized for cohesion rather than competence, and cohesion turned out to be what ruling required.

## Formation

| Stage | Official content | What Hughes argues it actually did |
|---|---|---|
| Preparatory school, from age 7–8, boarding | Latin and Greek | Removed boys from family and region; childhood spent with peers rather than kin |
| Public school, ~13–18, boarding (nine Clarendon schools; Eton at the apex) | Classics at 50–80% of teaching hours; ~5 classroom hours on a "full day," ~2 on a "half day"; 10–20 hours a week of organized team sport | Character formation, mutual loyalty, and an apprenticeship in authority through prefect self-government |
| Oxbridge college, ~18–21 | Classics (plus mathematics at Cambridge); an honours track or a near-contentless "pass" degree | Residential formation, tutorials, debating unions; a national rather than regional elite |
| The London Season, February–July, for life | Balls, dinners, soirées, opera | The elite's information network, marriage market, and norm-enforcement mechanism |

**Boarding and the national elite.** About 30 percent of nineteenth-century cabinet ministers and 40 percent of prime ministers were Old Etonians. Hughes's more interesting point is about boarding itself: continental and American elites mostly kept their children at home and schooled them locally, while British boys were sent away by eight. He suggests this is one reason Britain never developed the subnational elite identities of the American South, Catalonia or Hungary. The single-sex intensity of these schools, in Jan Morris's phrase, left upper-class English life "shot through with bisexual instinct," and demonstrative male affection (walking arm-in-arm in public) stayed normal in adult life.

**Classics as MacGuffin.** The curriculum was mostly Latin, with Greek second, mathematics and modern languages trailing, and science resisted not as false but as irrelevant to forming a gentleman. Thomas Arnold of Rugby, the century's leading educationalist, would rather his son believe "the sun went round the Earth" than have physical science be "the principal thing in my son's mind." Adults promptly forgot their classics; Churchill claimed twelve years of schooling never got him past the Greek alphabet. Hughes's reading is that the Victorians half-knew the curriculum was a pretext. Tom Brown's father in the 1857 novel does not "care a straw for Greek particles"; he wants "a brave, helpful, truth-telling Englishman, and a gentleman, and a Christian." Arnold's own ordering was religious and moral principle first, gentlemanly conduct second, intellectual ability third. One sidelight: because girls were not taught classics, affluent Victorian girls got the more modern curriculum.

**Sport and self-government.** Classroom hours were shorter than in French, German or Italian elite schools and probably shorter than in ordinary schools today. The hours went instead to cricket and football, which Victorians believed taught endurance, loyalty, channeled competitiveness, and how to win and lose. Hely Hutchinson Almond of Loretto ranked his school's priorities "character, physique, intelligence, manners, information," in that order. Continental visitors found the obsession baffling; it survives in American high-school and college sports. The other distinctive institution was the prefect system. Close supervision by masters was thought to breed servility, so senior boys were given pastoral and disciplinary authority, with juries and rights of appeal as checks. Hughes concedes it was sometimes brutal but insists on its intent: a kind of "elite liberalism" in which boys were legislators and executors of authority as well as its subjects.

Hughes's framing device is that by the terms of today's traditionalist-versus-progressive education debate (knowledge vs. skills, hard subjects vs. soft, structure vs. freedom), Victorian public schools sit on the progressive side on every axis, despite the rote learning and the cane.

## Oxbridge and Haileybury

Oxbridge admitted virtually everyone who applied. Most early-Victorian students were "pass men" sitting easy exams in classics and religion; aristocrats in particular preferred rowing, feasting and social polish to study, and pass men were still common in 1914. Even the honours track aimed at general formation rather than expertise. Benjamin Jowett of Balliol: "No man will be a first-rate physician or engineer who is not something more than either." Edward Pusey dismissed the natural sciences as "confined to the reception of information on matters of fact." Science took 8.1 percent of honours degrees in 1855–60 and still only 13.7 percent in 1910–14.

What the colleges actually supplied was residence. Unlike continental students in rented rooms, nearly all Oxbridge students lived in college: chapel, a morning's reading, lunch sent up to one's rooms (lavishly, for the rich), an afternoon of rowing or walking, tutorials over tea or sherry around 4pm once or twice a week, the debating unions, formal hall, then port and tobacco. The tutors themselves were mostly young Anglican priests waiting for a parish, since fellowships were closed to laymen until the 1870s and fellows could not marry. Hughes notes that Oxford and Cambridge did not become world-leading research institutions until German scholars fleeing Nazism arrived in the 1930s.

His cleanest illustration of the thesis is Haileybury, the East India Company's training college for the Indian civil service. David Gilmour's reconstruction of a day there, from John Beames's memoirs, is chapel in a nightshirt, breakfast parties with tankards of claret, two or three hours of lectures the "steadies" attended to and everyone else slept through, an afternoon of rowing or billiards, and "fast men" staggering back drunk from London at two in the morning. Nobody praised it as a school. Everyone, including Beames, credited it with *esprit de corps*. Lord Salisbury, later prime minister, spoke of the "close friendships formed there, which softened the rivalries of after life and secured devoted instead of perfunctory co-operation." Hughes suggests that sentence describes the whole elite education system.

## The adult day and the Season

|---|---|
| 9–11:30am | Breakfast. Pitt at 9; Gladstone's social breakfast from 10:30 with claret, running to noon; Disraeli arriving at Lord Houghton's around 11:30. Balfour did his morning's reading and letters in bed and rarely emerged before lunch |

| 4:30–5pm | Parliament sits. Politics was a part-time job for most MPs and peers |
| ~8:30pm | Dress for dinner. Soup with sherry, fish with hock, meat courses with champagne or claret, desserts and savories with port and madeira. A conduct guide allows married and "professional" ladies five or six glasses |
| 9–10pm | Ball begins; the floor fills around 11 |
| Midnight | Supper: soups, cold meats, shellfish, ices; negus (hot port with spices and citrus); champagne, and "indispensable" bottled ale and stout |
| After 1am | The ball peaks. Guests slip out without farewell; the determined stay until dawn |

For five months a year the elite lived within half an hour's walk of one another in two small neighborhoods facing each other across Hyde Park. Paris, Vienna, St Petersburg and New York had Seasons too, but Hughes stresses what made London's different: it coincided with the parliamentary session, so matchmaking, court ritual and political networking happened in the same rooms on the same nights. Debutantes shared the dance floor with judges, bankers and cabinet ministers. A Society ball, in his formulation, did not merely resemble "both a teenage house party and a business networking convention: in fact, it *was* both." Evenings were modular: an MP could leave a late sitting, drop into a soirée at 10:30, a supper party at 11:45, and a ball at 1am.

Hence the two accountings. By one, elite Victorians did four or five hours of work a day. By the other, they were working constantly, because the lunches and balls were, in historian Leonore Davidoff's words, "access to a vast information network: information about jobs, investment possibilities, secret political decisions." Hughes is explicit that this was not laziness. Sleep deprivation and burnout were common. It was "an extreme weighting toward what we might call team building and professional networking, and the near-total fusion of personal and professional life."

## Gemeinschaft at the apex

Tönnies distinguished *Gemeinschaft*, community held together by sentiment and tradition, from *Gesellschaft*, society held together by law and contract, and expected the second to displace the first as societies grew complex. Hughes's claim is that the Victorian elite was a small *Gemeinschaft* running an enormous *Gesellschaft*: a premodern village whose members happened to be dispersed across a kingdom and an empire, bound by shared schooling, shared memory, intense status competition, and, through the Season's marriages, literal kinship. Its members "strove for one another's envy, esteem and gratitude," and would go far past what contract or self-interest required to get them.

He names three compensating advantages for the opportunity cost of all those hours.

1. **Unity.** The rich were diverse: every region of the British Isles, landed aristocrats and the urban bourgeois who steadily displaced them, Anglicans alongside Nonconformists, Jews and Catholics. All of them, with the conspicuous exception of the Irish Catholics, were folded into one elite rather than hardening into counter-elites. The contrasts Hughes draws are the American North and South, the bourgeois-versus-nobility conflicts of Spain, Italy and France, and a Germany split by region, religion and class. A Nonconformist Manchester manufacturer voted Liberal; the idea of "a Methodist Republic of the North" never crossed his mind.

2. **Prestige as currency.** A *Gemeinschaft* can pay its members in esteem, which matters wherever adequate money is politically impossible. Modern states underpay politicians and civil servants relative to private-sector equivalents and struggle to attract talent. Until the 1880s most British local government, the rudimentary welfare system, the magistracy, and the boards of schools and hospitals were run unpaid by local landowners, for whom the only penalty for shirking was diminishment in the eyes of peers. MPs were unpaid and bore their own election costs: Hughes cites roughly £150,000 for an urban seat and £450,000 for a rural one in 1880, in today's money, repeated at every election, plus staff and accommodation once elected. The richest men in England competed fiercely for the privilege.

3. **Norm enforcement in office.** Walter Bagehot observed in *The English Constitution* that parliamentary systems give oppositions every incentive to sabotage necessary decisions, yet English statesmen "can rarely be persuaded even by their passions or their interest to do anything contrary to the real interest of England... And they would seriously hurt themselves if they did." Hughes's gloss: MPs of both parties belonged to the same village, and the village could punish defection "in ways that are impossible today," dampening demagoguery and brinkmanship.

He ends with a thought experiment rather than a prescription: a San Francisco Board of Supervisors staffed by Bezos, Page, Altman and Zuckerberg, each treating the seat as the glory of his career and hoping to graduate to the House of Representatives. "It might not be paradise. Maybe it would be a disaster. But it would certainly be interesting."

## Reading it critically

Several things the essay leaves implicit or under-weighted.

**Selection versus treatment.** The Eton-to-cabinet statistic is as consistent with inherited position as with anything the training did. Hughes concedes the absence of a counterfactual. What the essay firmly establishes is narrower than its title: that the elite's time allocation was overwhelmingly social, and that this was deliberate rather than decadent. Whether it caused the empire's success is left open.

**Why prestige could substitute for pay.** The mechanism Hughes gestures at has a name in network sociology: closure. When schoolmates, in-laws, club members and parliamentary colleagues are the same few hundred people, a reputation is a single asset exposed in every arena at once, so defecting from a norm anywhere costs you everywhere. Prestige is only a currency when the audience is small, stable and watching. That is precisely the condition modern elites lack. They are large, mobile and multi-audience, so a sanction in one arena does not follow them into the next, and money has to do the work esteem once did. This reframes the San Francisco thought experiment: the hard part is not persuading billionaires to serve but recreating an audience whose esteem they can neither buy nor escape.

**The cost Hughes underplays.** He grants the opportunity cost (four hours in the office rather than twelve) and the cost to the colonized. The stronger standing critique is Martin Wiener's *English Culture and the Decline of the Industrial Spirit* (1981), which blames precisely this gentlemanly, anti-technical, classics-and-cricket culture for Britain's relative industrial decline against Germany and the United States from the 1870s. Hughes's own evidence reads as support for Wiener: 13.7 percent science honours in 1910–14, universities that were not research institutions until émigrés remade them. The *Gemeinschaft* may have been good at ruling and bad at the thing Britain's lead actually rested on.

**A system already being dismantled.** The essay describes the arrangement at its peak without noting that mid-Victorian reformers were abandoning it. The Northcote–Trevelyan Report of 1854 pushed the civil service toward open competitive examination; the Indian Civil Service moved to exams in 1855 and Haileybury closed in 1858; MPs were paid from 1911. Hughes never asks whether the exam-selected Indian Civil Service outperformed Beames's Haileybury cohort, which is the natural test of his thesis.

**Who was outside the village.** Women were present at every ball and barred from every office the network fed into. Irish Catholics were the one group Hughes names as excluded, and Ireland is the one place Britain actually got its secession. The unity he credits is unity among those admitted.

## Connections

- [[relative-age-effects]] — Jacob and Bailey find that elite outcomes (class president, management, entrepreneurship) run through social capital rather than human capital, and that relatively older boys are *less* likely to become scientists. Hughes's Victorians are a whole-society version of the same finding: an elite selected and trained on social capital, with science left out on purpose. The compounding from a boarding-school cohort to a cabinet is [[cumulative-advantage]].
- [[unicontext]] — Callard's many local normative worlds. The Victorian elite was informationally collapsed (everyone knew everyone's business) but normatively bounded, exactly her point that "informational collapse without normative collapse is just a small town." Her Simmel thread, money as the leveler that abstracts all value, names what a prestige economy avoids: Hughes's village paid in a currency that could not be converted.
- [[nyc-mayoral-election-design]] — Kapadia's problem is strong candidates self-deselecting from a low-prestige office decided by a thin electorate. Hughes's Victorians paid the equivalent of hundreds of thousands of pounds per election for an unpaid seat because the seat carried prestige inside their village. Two ends of the same candidate-quality question.
- [[gospel-of-wealth]] — Carnegie's trusteeship of the rich is the American, individual, philanthropic counterpart to the Victorian landowner's unpaid public service, and Carnegie explicitly scorned the hereditary British model Hughes describes.
- [[continental-vs-maritime-powers]] — Paine's Britain is the strategic actor: navy, wealth, coalitions, never the enemy's main force. Hughes describes the domestic social machinery that produced the men who ran that playbook.
- [[liberal-international-order]] — Zakaria's Robinson-and-Gallagher account of Victorian overextension, the chain of dependencies from Suez to Fashoda, is where Hughes's "victorious century" ends up.
- [[two-europes-divergence]] — Besley and Persson argue elites tolerate less rent-seeking when something they jointly value is at stake. Bagehot's patriotic statesmen are the case where the shared stake is the standing of the village itself.

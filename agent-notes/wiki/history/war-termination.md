# War Termination

Prof. Sarah C. M. Paine's closing lecture in her Dwarkesh Patel series (recorded 2025, published September 2026). The thesis is a triad: wars are easy to start, hard to end, and hardest of all to end on the terms you wanted. Paine builds an analytic toolkit — mostly Clausewitz, plus one concept from Sun Tzu — then runs it over the Russo-Japanese War as the model case, the Gulf War and Korea as further regional cases, and two **pathologies**: regional wars that escalate into global wars (American Revolution, WWI, Japan in China → Pearl Harbor) and conventional victories that decay into insurgencies (Japan in China, the US in Vietnam/Iraq/Afghanistan).

This lecture is the applied complement to [[continental-vs-maritime-powers]]: that one explains why continental and maritime powers *fight*; this one explains why they can't *stop*.

## The toolkit

### Limited vs. unlimited objectives

Clausewitz's 1827 note: "War can be of two kinds ... either the objective is to overthrow the enemy — to render him politically helpless or militarily impotent, thus forcing him to sign whatever peace we please; or merely to occupy some of his frontier districts so that we can annex them or use them for bargaining at the peace negotiations."

- **Unlimited** = regime change. The defeated has no choices. (The US kept Hirohito because he was *useful* for signing the constitution and lending legitimacy to the occupation reforms — his survival was the victor's choice, not the loser's.)
- **Limited** = anything short of that. The defeated government survives, you must negotiate with it, and it may come back to bother you.

Paine's counterintuitive pairing:

| | Conceptually | To execute |
|---|---|---|
| Unlimited war | Simple — everyone understands "regime change" | Hard — you put the enemy on death ground |
| Limited war | Hard — endless argument over *which* point on the continuum | Easy — especially if the ask is small enough that giving it up isn't existential |

Governments rarely state their limited objective explicitly ("maybe I get a little more, who knows"), which leaves military officers fighting toward a target that hasn't been named. Paine's standard for success follows directly: a war is won if you **achieved the objective you fought for**, not if you won the battles. The US won most conventional engagements in Vietnam and lost the war.

Clausewitz's deeper point, which Paine flags as the reason he is considered brilliant: the primary discriminator of a war's *type* is not the resources poured in (means) but the ends. "No other possibility exists than to subordinate the military point of view to the political. The degree of force that must be used against the enemy depends on the scale of the political objective on either side."

### Sun Tzu's death ground

"Ground in which the army survives only if it fights with the courage of desperation is called death ground. Soldiers fight to the death when there is no alternative." An unlimited objective, by definition, puts the target regime on death ground; genocidal ambitions put the whole population there. Paine's contemporary example is Ukraine, which has defied everyone who "doesn't get death ground." Unlimited aims can transform a failed state into a lethal adversary.

### Culminating point of attack vs. culminating point of victory

Two paired Clausewitzian terms, operating at different scales:

- **Culminating point of attack** — operational; applies to a battle or a campaign. "Most attacks only lead up to the point where their remaining strength is just enough to maintain a defense and wait for peace. Beyond that point the scale turns and the reaction follows with a force that is usually much stronger than that of the original attack."
- **Culminating point of victory** — strategic; applies to the whole war.

In either case: stop short and you left achievable gains on the table at acceptable cost; overshoot and you've taken on risks and costs that exceed any benefit and are probably weaker for it. Paine's single most important diagnostic: **the tell that you've overshot the culminating point of victory is third-party intervention**, especially by a big third party that changes the nature of the war.

### Decisive vs. pivotal vs. lopsided

Paine wants precise vocabulary. Clausewitz: "Campaigns whose outcome has been determined by a single battle have become fairly common only in recent times; those cases in which they have settled an entire war are very rare." So:

- **Decisive** = war-winning. Used loosely to mean "important," the word conveys nothing.
- **Lopsided** = skewed casualty ratio (Tsushima).
- **Pivotal** = forecloses options for one side and opens them for the other without ending the war (Port Arthur).

### The Bologna flask

A Bologna flask is glass quenched so its exterior is hard enough to hammer a nail, but a scratch on the interior shatters it. Clausewitz uses it for the central uncertainty of war termination: will "the first shock of battle steel the enemy's resolve and stiffen his resistance," or will he "shatter as soon as his surface is scratched"? North Vietnam was the hammer; Nicholas II was the flask; Putin bet on a flask in Ukraine and got a hammer. Paine's point is that you cannot know in advance, which is why exit strategies matter more than entry plans.

### Value of the object

The value *to you* of your political objective. Whoever lives in the theater has an existential value of the object; the intervener's falls off with distance. Insurgency is a cost-imposition strategy that bets on this asymmetry (see below).

### Opportunity cost

Money spent on a war is money not spent on something else. Paine's Russian example: rubles burned in Manchuria were rubles not spent on the European rail network that would have let allies supply Russia in WWI. Nicholas II lost that war to food riots in St. Petersburg — a logistics failure — and ended in a Siberian basement.

## The model case: the Russo-Japanese War (1904–05)

### Why it happened

Russia was a multiple of Japan on every quantifiable measure: territory, population, industrial base, resources, army, navy. Both wanted to expand — Japan into Korea, Russia into Manchuria (Chinese territory, roughly the size of Germany and France combined). Russia had built an expensive railway system from Harbin south to Port Arthur, its ice-free naval base (Vladivostok freezes for months). When the Boxers — "the al-Qaeda of their day" — tore up railway concessions, Russia sent troops and then declined to withdraw once the rebellion ended. Japan offered a sphere-of-influence trade (you take Manchuria, we take Korea); Russia stalled, intending to take everything. Japan concluded the clock was running against it and struck.

### The campaign

Japan had to neutralize the Russian fleet at Port Arthur or lose its sea lines of communication. Blockading the harbor also meant investing the fortress by land, which tied down one of Japan's four armies (a fifth was cobbled together at the very end). The war plan — fight up the railway toward Harbin, win an annihilating battle, bring Russia to the table — needed that army. So General Nogi Maresuke launched four frontal assaults on the fortress, losing about 45,000 men, the size of an entire Japanese army. The eventual solution was 203 Metre Hill: spotters up there, plus large-calibre siege guns hauled in over a week, sank everything in the harbor in about three days. Port Arthur capitulated roughly a month later, despite having provisions (including 3,000 horses) to hold out considerably longer. Paine's counterfactual: Russia never thought to use Port Arthur as a *fortress in being* to pin Japanese assets indefinitely.

Meanwhile Nicholas II sent the Baltic Fleet to relieve Port Arthur, and when it fell, doubled down and sent everything else — big ships round Africa, small through Suez — to be sunk on Japanese terms at Tsushima. Japan lost three torpedo boats and *grew* its fleet by commandeering Russian ships.

Both sides fought for limited objectives: Japan never intended to overthrow St. Petersburg, and Nicholas quickly learned he could not overthrow Tokyo. The war was over Manchuria and Korea.

### Where was the culminating point of attack at Port Arthur?

Paine models the analytic move rather than settling it. Her pick: **taking 203 Metre Hill**, because without the hill the fleet has no sanctuary and its destruction is a matter of time. The counterargument: **the destruction of the fleet itself**, because the fleet was pinning so much Japanese force that only its actual destruction achieves the operational goal. The lesson she draws is methodological: argue, counter-argue, and notice that your rebuttals differ depending on which thesis you take.

### Five candidates for Japan's culminating point of victory

Paine offers five and refuses to choose, again as an exercise:

1. **Home-front implosion (the 1905 Revolution).** Russia was one of three European states without a legislature (with the Ottoman Empire and Montenegro; Japan had one). Reservists rioted at their concentration points, and in European Russia reserves grossly outnumbered regulars. Troops fired on a peaceful petition march to the Winter Palace while the Tsar was out of town, shredding the regime's legitimacy. In an autocracy the autocrat's resolve is the war: when he decides it's over, it's over.
2. **The land battle of Mukden.** Japan threw in everyone too young, too old, and too tired to fight and was still ~125,000 men short of Russia. The Trans-Siberian's throughput went from 20–40,000 troops/month at the start of the war to 100,000/month by Mukden. Russia had under 100,000 men in theater at the start, ~150,000 by Liaoyang (August 1904), and nearly 800,000 by August 1905 — only 40% of a 3-million-man army — against fewer than 700,000 Japanese who were everything Japan had. Field Marshal Yamagata Aritomo's assessment: the enemy will never sue for peace unless we invade Moscow or St. Petersburg, which is infeasible; he has powerful forces at home, we have exhausted ours; he is not short of officers, we cannot replace ours. The Manchurian Army's chief of staff agreed: if you start a fire, you have to put it out.
3. **Finance.** Both sides fought on loans. Japan's battlefield wins drove its interest rates down; it secured its last two loans after Mukden. Russia, already hurt by a recession and bad harvest before the war, could find no lender after Mukden. After Portsmouth it raised a loan just to ship its troops home.
4. **Tsushima.** Russia did not need this battle — all its supply came by rail, nothing by sea, and the land balance was improving daily. Nicholas fought anyway and lost the entire fleet.
5. **Sakhalin.** Apparently on Roosevelt's suggestion, Japan seized Russian territory the Russians couldn't defend, as a bargaining chip. For Russia the prestige loss — Russian soil, lost to an Asian power, given the racial attitudes of the day — may have been what cracked the flask.

### Why Paine calls it model termination

- **The exit was designed before the entry.** Before attacking Port Arthur, Japan had already selected Theodore Roosevelt as mediator, "counting on Americans being busybodies." Itō Hirobumi assigned Viscount Kaneko Kentarō (Harvard, a Roosevelt acquaintance) to open the channel, and Baron Komura Jutarō (also Harvard) to conduct the negotiations. When Yamagata declared Japan out of men after Mukden, the "America card" was already in hand.
- **Japan knew its minimum objective** (Russia out of Korea) and got that plus the southern half of Russia's Manchurian railway concessions, the naval base and its equipment, southern Sakhalin, and confirmation of the 1894–95 verdict that Japan was Asia's dominant regional power.
- **Russia got nothing** and paid an opportunity cost that arguably killed the dynasty a decade later.
- **Diplomatic isolation vs. a silent partner.** Russia was alone; Japan had the 1902 Anglo-Japanese Alliance, with Britain as quiet backer. In the terms of [[continental-vs-maritime-powers]], Japan was Britain's rented elephant against Russia — Paine's rule 3 of the elephant-hunting playbook, seen from the elephant's side.

## Two more regional cases

### The Gulf War (1991): the war the US didn't pay for

Saddam, broke from the Iran war, invaded Kuwait to erase his war debt and to combine Iraqi and Kuwaiti oil fields into a possible swing-producer position. Paine's point is that Bush Sr. did what almost no one does in a limited war: he **wrote the limited objective down** — National Security Directive 54: Iraq out of Kuwait, Kuwaiti government restored, American lives protected, and thereby Gulf security and stability promoted (not created). All four achieved.

| | Figure (per Paine) |
|---|---|
| Coalition partners | 34 |
| Allied troops | 200,000+ (US: ~700,000) |
| Allied payments to the US | $50B+ |
| Allied payments to other coalition members | $14B |
| Iraqi military deaths | 20–35,000 (plus up to 3,000 civilians) |
| Coalition deaths | 392 (258 American) |

Paine knows of no other war with roughly a million belligerents and a death toll that small on one side. The mechanism is the same as 1905: **isolation**. Iraq faced every rich country on earth plus nearly all its neighbors. Facing a massive coalition never goes well. This is also the [[liberal-international-order]] working as designed — a coalition, a UN mandate, and a stopping rule.

### Korea (1950–53): overshooting the culminating point twice

Geography first. The inter-Korean land border is only ~250 km, and the peninsula's maritime borders and shoreline railways are exposed to a dominant navy — which looks containable. But the northern border is ~17 km with Russia and ~1,400 km with China, whose industrial base then sat in Manchuria. The theater cannot be isolated; the enemy has big friends with an uninterrupted supply line. And when *both* sides have big friends pouring in aid, the war is long, bloody, and ruinous to the territory fought over.

Then objectives. Unlike Japan in 1904 or Bush in 1991, the US could not decide between the limited aim (restore the pre-war line) and the unlimited one (reunify the peninsula because it looked militarily possible). Paine reads this as letting the military objective drive the political one — the exact inversion Clausewitz forbids.

The culminating point of victory was exceeded twice:

1. **Kim Il-sung** invaded expecting a two-party fight he could win, and was winning — until he triggered "the third-party intervention from hell" (the US and UN). A civil war became a regional war with global potential. Paine: a slow insurgency would have served him better.
2. **MacArthur** landed at Inchon, decided to reunify the whole peninsula, approached the Chinese border, and brought in hundreds of thousands of Chinese troops. He was cashiered not long after.

Result: the divided peninsula that was the minimum objective all along, at vastly higher cost, plus a US garrison ever since.

## Pathology 1: regional wars that go global

Three cases, one pattern.

- **American Revolution.** Limited from the colonists' side (no regime change in London), unlimited from Britain's (eliminate the Continental Army and Congress) — so the colonists were on death ground. The theater was big and the war protracted, which gave Britain's rivals time to weigh in: France allied with the colonies (1778), Spain with France (1779), Britain attacked the Dutch (1780). Fighting spread to Savannah, Louisiana, Ushant, Gibraltar, Minorca, Cape Verde, Mahé and Pondicherry, and above all the Caribbean sugar islands. Britain reassessed, decided the colonies weren't worth much, kept the Caribbean, and bailed.
- **World War I.** Everyone planned a short regional war to poach a bit of European territory or extract colonies. But objectives did not align even *within* alliances: Britain wanted the status quo; France wanted Alsace-Lorraine back (lost in the Franco-Prussian War); Italy wanted the eastern Mediterranean; Russia wanted Austria-Hungary out of the Balkans and especially Serbia; Austria-Hungary and the Ottomans wanted more of the Balkans; Germany wanted colonies. Result: parallel wars in different theaters, and no settlement that could satisfy your own alliance, let alone the enemy. Nobody dared ditch allies they couldn't fight without. So it ground on.
- **Japan, Manchuria to Pearl Harbor.** Paine traces the chain from Smoot-Hawley: Hoover signed it over economists' objections, trade partners retaliated, global trade collapsed, the Depression deepened abroad (creating the conditions for Hitler), and trade-dependent Japan — a League of Nations founder — concluded its trading partners had abandoned it and went for autarky by seizing Manchuria. A regional war. Then it extended the program to all of China, putting the Chinese on death ground; China's warring factions stopped fighting each other and fought Japan to a stalemate while Japan's economy sank. Japan's answer was to cut off foreign aid to the Nationalists — hence, on the same day as Pearl Harbor, attacks on Malaya, the Philippines, Singapore, Wake, Guam, Thailand, and the Shanghai International Settlement. Instead of driving the great powers out of Asia, it brought the US, Britain, Australia, and New Zealand in.

**Common features of wars that escalate:** a large theater that is hard to seal off and hard to garrison; protraction that gives third parties time to choose sides; an enemy with friends; an unlimited objective that guarantees the enemy fights forever; and sunk losses that make the belligerents *less* willing to compromise as the war goes on.

## Pathology 2: insurgency as victory denial

You defeat the enemy conventionally but they don't quit. Japan in China is the type case: conventional battles against the Nationalists, who lost and reconstituted every time, while Nationalist and Communist guerrilla forces operated separately. This impales the occupier on a dilemma — **concentrate** to fight the field army and leave the rear open to guerrillas, or **disperse** to secure the rear and let the field army maneuver. The occupied theater, which was supposed to *generate* income, instead consumes it in garrison costs.

Paine credits the design to Washington and the Continental Army: the regular army forces the British to concentrate while militias harass everywhere else. Washington disliked the militias but came to see the logic. It is a **cost-imposition strategy** betting on asymmetric value of the object: the occupier's willingness to pay falls with distance from home. The same strategy has since been run against the US in Vietnam, Iraq, and Afghanistan, "where eventually we call it a day."

Paine is explicit that insurgency is even costlier for the insurgents than for the occupier — it is a strategy of last resort, not a cheap one.

### Iraq 2003 as the anti-Gulf-War

Gulf War I was limited; Iraq 2003 was unlimited. Regime change was the easy part; afterward "the loaded diaper is yours." Paine's structural reasons it was predictable in advance:

- **Mosaic society.** Iraqi primary identities are ethnic/sectarian rather than national, and most groups have someone they'd like to kill. In Korea nobody disputes who is Korean — the fight is over state institutions. Where national identity itself is contested, you cannot build state institutions.
- **Border arithmetic.** Gulf War I engaged ~158 miles of Iraqi border; occupying the whole country engages 2,000+ miles, against neighbors — Saudi Arabia, Iran, Turkey — with real regional ambitions who *will* be involved.
- **Low activation energy.** One car bomb at a Shia shrine was enough to start a sectarian war.

## Paine's pre-war checklist

Questions to run before a war and to keep running during one:

1. Who is involved, and who is *interested*?
2. Who has **limited vs. unlimited objectives**, and vis-à-vis whom? (Russia's aim against Ukraine is unlimited; Ukraine's against Russia is limited.)
3. How do policy objectives align across all parties — and how do primary adversaries line up?
4. **Relative value of the object**: for whom is it existential, for whom nice-to-have? (What happens in Ukraine matters more to Ukrainians than to Russians, despite Russia's heavy losses.)
5. **Geographic access and sanctuary**: which places are invulnerable to attack, and therefore beyond your leverage? (The Iraq border map; Manchuria behind Korea.)
6. What asymmetries fall out of the above, and who can therefore align with whom?
7. Where is each side's **culminating point of victory**, and is it even reachable? Where are the culminating points of attack for each campaign?

## Synthesis

- **Pick your mediator before you fire the first shot.** Japan's real innovation in 1904 was not 203 Metre Hill; it was choosing Roosevelt, staffing the channel with Harvard men, and knowing what its minimum acceptable settlement was. Every other case in the lecture lacked this.
- **Third-party intervention is the alarm bell.** It is simultaneously the sign you've overshot (Korea, twice), the mechanism by which regional wars go global (1778, 1914, 1941), and the thing an isolated belligerent cannot summon (Russia 1905, Iraq 1991). The wars Paine rates as successes are those where the winner had partners and the loser had none.
- **Death ground makes unlimited wars self-defeating** unless you can genuinely annihilate the opponent. The pattern from the Continental Army to Ukraine is that existential threat unifies fractured opponents (China's factions in 1937) and converts conventional defeat into insurgency.
- **Geography sets the escalation risk**, in the same way [[geography-shapes-history]] argues it sets the probabilities of everything else: a sealable theater with a short border (Kuwait) terminates cleanly; an unsealable one with big neighbors (Korea, Iraq, China) does not.
- **Continental powers overshoot.** Paine's other lecture notes that continental powers have "no stopping rule"; this lecture supplies the case files — Nicholas II at Tsushima, Japan in China, Kim at the 38th parallel — and the theoretical name for what they lacked: a culminating point of victory identified in advance.
- **Application to Taiwan.** Read against [[xi-jinping-ideology]], the checklist is pointed: an unlimited objective against Taiwan puts Taiwan on death ground; the theater is maritime and hard for a continental power to seal; and the intervention that marks overshoot is exactly the contingency the alliance system exists to make credible.

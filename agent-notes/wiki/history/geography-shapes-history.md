# Geography Shapes History

Tomas Pueyo's "chessboard" thesis: geography sets the *probabilities* of History. It doesn't determine who wins, but it nudges outcomes in specific directions, and those nudges compound over millennia. His stated anti-determinist gloss: "Humans everywhere, under the same conditions, do the same things." Geography is the board, not the players — a framing that also underlies Paine's [[continental-vs-maritime-powers]] (where sea-defense, threat geometry, and lines of communication decide whether a state can pursue prosperity through trade or must pursue security through conquest).

## The physical-geography rules

A rough hierarchy of what terrain does to a society:

- **Rivers** → arable land.
- **Flat plains** → cheap food and cheap building.
- **Large flat plains + abundant water** → lots of food → lots of people.
- **Rivers > seas > plains** (in that order) → cheap movement → trade, wealth, technology exchange.
- **Mountains** → bad for farming, building, and trade; great for defense.

The general engine: fertile, flat, well-watered, well-connected land accumulates population and wealth, and connectivity lets it absorb technology from elsewhere. Pueyo quantifies the ruggedness penalty via Nunn & Puga: GDP per person falls log-linearly with terrain ruggedness, and cultivation becomes impractical above a 2% slope, impossible above 6%. His refrain — "remember this next time someone says Northern Italians work harder than Southern Italians," or a German minister tells Greeks to work harder — is that what reads as national character is usually just the terrain.

A sharpening on the movement rule: **navigable rivers beat the sea.** Rivers carry less salt (drinkable, more fertile banks), service twice the land area of a coastline (two banks vs. one), have no tides to fight, and no storms. This is why Pueyo predicts Northern Europe was always going to out-wealth the South — the Alpine/southern mountains catch the water that then flows north as long navigable rivers, while the tectonically crumpled Mediterranean South was left with only the sea.

## The draughts-to-chess metaphor

The article's title image: early on, geography was so constraining that History was a game of *draughts* (checkers) — few legal moves, the board dictating almost everything. As technology accumulated (and Pueyo defines technology broadly — know-how, materials, government quality, military tactics, any transmissible *meme*), the pieces gained range and the game became *chess*: more combinations, geography mattering less. But chess is still played on a fixed 8×8 board with fixed rules. **Geography is the board; it constrains the game without playing it** — the anti-determinist gloss above restated as a game metaphor.

## Worked examples: reading wealth off a relief map

Part I is a training exercise in predicting where cities, wealth, and political units land using terrain alone:

- **Italy.** Every major city sits in a river valley — Rome (Tiber), Naples (Volturno), Florence (Arno/Tuscany), and the Po Valley cluster (Turin, Milan, Venice, Bologna, Verona…). Overlay NASA nightlights on a relief map and the green valleys light up; the mountains stay dark. The Italian Boot is also a unity machine: seas on three sides, the Alps (Europe's tallest natural wall) sealing the north.
- **The Pannonian Basin.** A "walled garden" that has been a single political unit (most recently Austria-Hungary) for ~1,000 years — because the Carpathians and Alps ring it. Geography that encloses tends to unify.
- **Greece.** Almost all mountains; the one decent valley (Thessalonica/Macedon) is exactly where Alexander's expansionist power came from. The rest could only support small city-states that couldn't unify or feed large populations — so Greece was *condemned* to become a **thalassocracy** (sea-based power), spreading across the Aegean because maritime transport was (and still is) up to ~10× cheaper than land. But the same thin arable base condemned it to eventually lose to better-landed neighbors: Rome, then Byzantium, then the Ottomans — 2,000 years of occupation. Geography that grants an early advantage can also cap the ceiling.

Pueyo's checklist for a favored geographic entity: **flat plains + rivers + navigable + sea access + defended by mountains.** The thalassocracy vs. land-power distinction here is the germ of what Sarah Paine develops at length in [[continental-vs-maritime-powers]].

## The US as the "inevitable empire"

Pueyo (drawing on Stratfor and Tim Marshall's *Prisoners of Geography*) argues the continental US drew the jackpot on two axes:

1. **Defense for free.** Oceans and mountains on the flanks; where those are absent (the north), it's too cold for a rival power to emerge. So the US never had to bleed itself on a neighbor arms race — its physical integrity is nearly costless.
2. **The Mississippi basin + intracoastal waterways.** The world's largest contiguous piece of farmland (mountains on both sides concentrate water inward; nearly flat) sits on top of more internal navigable waterway than the rest of the world *combined*. Cheap food and goods, cheaply movable to the world through New Orleans.

Whoever held that land would become a world power — the empire was "inevitable" in the sense that the board rewarded whoever occupied it.

## Why Eurasia won the civilizational race (Diamond's axes)

The article's core mechanism is Jared Diamond's *Guns, Germs and Steel* thesis: **development is fastest where there is both resource diversity and competition, connected but not unified.**

- **The Eurasian belt** (Ireland/Morocco → China/Japan) has huge fertile land, enormous environmental variation (so many crops/animals emerge independently), *and* an east-west orientation. Independent civilizations arose on great rivers — Nile, Tigris-Euphrates, Indus/Ganges, Yellow/Yangtze — isolated enough to develop separately but connected enough (eventually the Silk Road) to share.
- **The east-west advantage.** Along a latitude band, climate is similar, so crops, animals, and technologies migrate easily (wheat from Mesopotamia grows in both China and Europe). North-south axes cross climate zones, so diffusion is slow — this is why **Africa and the Americas developed more slowly**: their landmasses run north-south, gated by the Sahara, jungle, malaria, the Andes, and the narrow neck of Central America. Civilizations *did* arise (Mesoamerica, the Andes, Mississippian mound-builders, Pacific Northwest chiefdoms) — just slower, with less interconnected variance to draw on.

## Competition vs. unity: the "efficient market" of civilizations

Pueyo's sharpest sub-claim: **too much unity kills progress.** Human development is fastest under many diverse polities that learn from and clash with each other — but where no single one takes over the whole. Like an efficient market with no monopoly.

- **Europe/Middle East**: weak external barriers (constant outside threat forces you to keep up technologically) + strong internal barriers (hard to unify → many competing approaches → natural selection of ideas, with diffusion fast enough to spread winners). This is offered as *the* reason Europe, not China or India, exploded in the 1500s.
- **The counter-cases — unity as stagnation:**
  - *Japan*: fast-developing while fragmented; united under the Tokugawa (17th–19th c.), lost gunpowder as "ignoble," stagnated — then modernized within 40 years of being forced open, beating Russia in war.
  - *China*: invented paper, printing, and the world's largest navy, then the Ming turned isolationist — rebuilt the Great Wall, abandoned the Silk Road, scrapped the treasure fleet, weakened the writing-heavy civil service. Too big and unchallenged to keep racing.

The contingency payoff: in a slightly different world, China conquers America and "this newsletter would be in Chinese." Europe just had the right geography at the right technological moment (gunpowder + printing press + the Americas).

## The forward-looking twist

The essay's real agenda is temporal: **geography's grip is loosening.** If we misattribute History to "wars, generals, and political movements" rather than to luck-plus-time on a geographic board, we'll miss how much technology — which is progressively erasing the cost of distance and terrain — will reshape the future. (Pueyo's series continues into how technology takes over geography's role; the same source appends an aside that remote work, being nearly as productive and vastly cheaper for knowledge work, is one live instance of distance losing its bite.)

## Caveats

Single opinionated secondary source synthesizing Diamond, Stratfor, and Marshall — hence `medium` confidence. Diamond's axes thesis is contested in academic geography/anthropology (charges of over-tidy determinism); Pueyo explicitly disavows the racist reading but the mechanism remains a probabilistic just-so story in places, and he flags his own uncertainty (e.g., why the Mississippian cultures didn't scale). Treat as a useful lens, not settled fact.
